## Chapter 8

The halogen beam slices across the *Harbor Bell*'s white coaming, blinding bright against the black sleet.

I roll sideways off the aft cockpit bench before the light reaches the stern tarp, catch the icy port gunwale with my wet leather gloves, and drop onto the timber stringer beneath the **Coal Pier** deck. My right shin bangs hard against a barnacle-crusted crossbeam. Cold salt spray splashes up through the planking gaps as the launch heels slightly under my weight, its rubber fender buoys squeaking loud enough to carry over the surf.

Above me, boot heels hit the pier planks.

*Tick-thud. Tick-thud.*

Conrad doesn't rush. He stops at the head of the stern boarding ladder, sweeps the halogen beam across the green canvas tarp over `Crate #06`, then down along the port rub-rail where my wet glove smeared the fiberglass. A thin crust of black ice coats the timber stringer under my boots. I press my back flat against the wet granite piling beneath the deck, holding the crowbar tight against my ribs so it won't clink against the salt-crusted iron tie-rods. Through the gaps between the wet deck planks, the yellow glow of the swinging stern lantern stripes my wool sleeves while sleet hisses into the black oil-slick chop beside my heels.

The beam holds on the stern mooring cleat for five slow counts, catching the taut hemp line and the galvanized shackle pin.

Then Conrad steps back, clicks the searchlight down to a narrow yellow spot, and walks back toward the shoreward apron—not heading north toward the Customs House, but turning straight into the open side door of the **Disinfection Steam Plant** thirty paces up the causeway.

Wait. If he's sweeping the Steam Plant next, why did he leave the iron side door swinging wide behind him when he came out?

The heavy door groans shut, followed by the rattle of a loose latch bar. Through the high corrugated-iron clerestory windows at the far end of the boiler house, pale yellow worklight spills across the sleet-swept gravel for ten seconds, then snaps dark again as he kills the overhead switch. He isn't locking up for the night. He's running a perimeter check—and the Steam Plant is the only roofed structure between the Coal Pier and the **North Ledge Signal Tower** where I can get out of the wind long enough to read my father's 1998 binder.

My teeth chatter hard enough to click together. My wool trousers are frozen stiff from the knees down from wading the steam tunnel, and my fingers ache inside my wet gloves. Staying under the pier pilings until the `00:30` ebb means hypothermia before midnight.

I edge hand over hand along the icy timber stringer toward the shoreward bulkhead, pull myself up onto the granite apron beside the yellow hydraulic hand-truck and its coil of spare anchor chain, and crouch behind the pier's iron mooring bollard while the storm swell booms under the planks. As soon as Conrad's yellow beam disappears behind the Steam Plant's far masonry gable, I sprint low across the sleet-slick gravel and slip through the leeward coal-chute door before he can double back along the seawall.

***

The **Disinfection Steam Plant** smells of wet coal ash, scorched machine oil, and hot transformer copper.

Inside, the cavernous nineteenth-century boiler house stretches into pitch blackness under a steep corrugated-iron roof that drums like a snare under the driving sleet. Two rows of massive **cast-iron coal sterilization retorts**—cylindrical pressure chambers built in the 1890s to bake typhus mattresses and quarantine cargo—sit on brick cradles down the center aisle. Overhead, a network of rusted perforated iron catwalks and vertical steel ladders links the upper coal loft to a tangle of heavy steam-line headers and brass isolation valves hanging dark in the rafters.

I ease the heavy iron coal door shut behind me until the latch tongue rests quiet against the strike plate, then crouch low behind the brick cradle of **Retort #2** where the curved iron belly shields me from the main aisle. Above my head, a narrow timber-and-iron coal loft overhangs the far gable—just dark enough to read a binder by flashlight if the aisle below stays clear. My wet wool coat clings heavy against my ribs.

A metallic *clack* echoes from the far south wall.

I freeze behind the cold brick cradle, holding my breath.

Conrad didn't leave the building by the seawall. He walked the outer gravel loop around the coal yard and came back in through the south boiler door.

Now, in the pitch dark, he doesn't even click his searchlight back on. He knows the room by heart. I hear no frantic sweeping of corners, no wild pistol-light stabbing into the rafters. Just a steady, unhurried *tick-thud... tick-thud...* moving along the perforated checker-plate floor grating on a fixed, practiced circuit.

He stops at the main steam header along the east wall. A cast-iron valve wheel squeaks a quarter-turn under a gloved hand, testing the seat. Two paces north, a brass inspection tag clinks against an overhead manifold pipe. Then his boots cross to the north bulkhead door, where the heavy steel padlock hasp rattles once in his palm to confirm the pin is seated.

*Valves, seals, breaker plates.* Always in the exact same order.

I press my cold cheek against the rough brick of Retort #2, listening to the cadence of his right boot on the iron grating. Whoever Conrad is, he doesn't hunt like a panicked hired gun. He moves like a marine surveyor working a hull-and-machinery checklist—methodical, patient, making sure the physical plant matches whatever Victor Garroway expects to see on paper before the `00:30` ebb.

His footsteps pause ten paces from Retort #2. Above the boiler aisle, a narrow steel ladder rises to the upper iron catwalk where the main steam-line isolation valve and brass pressure-bleed petcock sit suspended twenty feet above the coal floor. Conrad tests the bottom rung with one gloved hand, listens to the iron grating settle, then turns back toward the south masonry wall. A faint whiff of menthol nasal spray and white lithium grease drifts past my hiding spot as he passes, close enough that I hear the wet rustle of his oilskin trench coat against the brick corner.

Two heavy steel cabinet latches snap open in the dark. A dim green pilot LED paints the underside of his jaw and the pale scar along his right cheek as he checks a row of meter faces inside a gray steel enclosure, runs his thumb along the breaker throw, and shuts the cabinet door.

Then the exterior iron door bangs against its brick stop, letting in a freezing gust of sleet, and the *tick-thud* of his steel toe-tap fades north up the gravel path toward the Customs House.

I stay crouched behind Retort #2 until the sleet drowns out his stride completely. Only then do I peel off my right leather glove for three seconds to flex my stiff fingers. My watch glows `6:18 PM`.

***

I stand up stiffly, shielding my flashlight lens under my left palm, and cross the checker-plate floor grating to the gray steel enclosure on the south masonry wall.

Beneath the cabinet, a thick black armored conduit—identical to the trunk line I felt humming in the Ward C steam tunnel—rises straight out of a submerged granite floor trench and enters a heavy oil-cooled step-down transformer bolted to the brick. Fresh gray marine silicone seals the floor collar where the conduit clears the nineteenth-century masonry, and a stamped brass grounding strap bolts straight into the wet granite seawall. Even through my wet boot soles, the **sixty-cycle hum** vibrates steady and warm against the iron floor plate, cutting right under the drum of sleet on the corrugated roof.

I pop the two cabinet latches and swing the steel door open.

Inside sit three massive **480-volt commercial knife-breakers**, each labeled in fresh black-on-white engraved phenolic tags:

**`MAIN BAY FEED — 480V`**
**`WARD C — 4°C VAULT & HIGH-TEMP ALARM`**
**`ADMINISTRATION / CUSTOMS HOUSE`**

Beside the middle breaker sits a red digital telemetry shunt wired to a pier-wide alarm relay and a small amber status diode. Above the busbars, a laminated wiring schematic shows the split trunk lines: one heavy armored feeder running north up the tunnel toward the Customs House office, and a second line feeding back to Ward C's `4°C` compressors and exterior piezo klaxon. If I pull the **Customs House** knife-breaker and trip the **Ward C** temperature alarm right now, the whole compound goes dark and the refrigeration klaxon starts screaming across the cove. Both Garroway and Conrad will come running straight down the gravel path before I've even opened the 1998 binder in my duffel or checked the **North Ledge Signal Tower**.

Leaving the switchgear untouched is the harder play, and the smarter one. If I save that breaker trip for later, Conrad's own routine guarantees he'll walk straight back to this wall alone while Garroway guards the launch.

I close the gray steel door carefully, latching only the bottom clasp so it won't rattle in the draft.

As I step back to follow the submarine cable trench toward the iron ladder climbing up to the sheltered coal loft, my light catches a clear **waterproof plastic inspection sleeve** bolted to the side flange of the 2023 transformer housing.

Inside sits a laminated electrical commissioning tag, yellowed slightly along the edges by salt damp. I pull my loupe from my coat pocket, press the brass bezel against the plastic face, and read the stamped contractor lines:

**`COMMISSIONING TAG: B.I.Q.S.-04 — 480V STEP-DOWN`**
**`MARINE CONTRACTOR JOB: #PM-2023-418 / BARGE CRANE: 14 CWT`**
**`UNDERWRITER ROUTING: HM-884-19B / DATE: OCTOBER 18, 2023`**

Forty-eight hours before Warehouse #4 burned to the waterline.

I recognize every digit of that job number and fourteen-hundredweight barge-crane load from my `2023` audit—the exact Harbor Mutual policy routing code (`HM-884-19B`) that Victor Garroway testified under oath had been falsified on my Portland crane logs. Beside the printed routing line, a penciled rigging note records the night tide when the Providence barge unloaded the transformer skid here while Commercial Street Wharf burned. I snap two quick photographs of the laminated tag through the plastic sleeve, slip my loupe and phone back into my wet wool coat pocket, and turn toward the iron ladder leading up to the dark coal loft above the retorts, where I can finally open the 1998 binder out of the driving bay wind and sleet.
