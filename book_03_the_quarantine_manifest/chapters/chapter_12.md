## Chapter 12

I pull the manila folder stamped **`B.I.Q.S.-04 — COLD STORAGE UPGRADE (NOV 2014)`** onto my knees and open the brass fastener.

No medical examiner's report sits inside. No toxicology sheet, no coroner's check, no Portland clinic slip.

Instead, clipped to a Harbor Mutual marine property renewal dated **November 11, 2014**, is a heavy-stock **cold-storage upgrade work order** for Ward C's `4°C` lockers—authorizing a thirty-year shell-trust extension and an auxiliary brine-compressor overhaul for the Providence maritime preservation trust. Three contractor invoices sit behind it, detailing stainless-steel thermal gaskets, replacement evaporator coils, a secondary glycol loop, and a covert barge delivery scheduled for the third week of November. Every invoice is stamped **`PAID — STATE STREET ESCROW #884-19B`**, routed through the exact same Boston account Margot Kiefer-Hall used for her survivor annuities.

Stapled directly behind the work order sits a single **Station Decommissioning & Survey Certificate** drafted on **`Calder Maritime & Cargo`** letterhead. Above the seal block, three typed paragraphs declare Ward C's nineteenth-century basement morgue structurally condemned, empty of biological material, and permanently disconnected from shore power—paperwork designed to satisfy Rhode Island state coastal inspectors if anyone ever checked the island's tax-exempt preservation status. A blank notary jurat sits in the lower-left margin, waiting for a Portland marine notary's stamp that never arrived.

My father's name is typed cleanly beneath the surveyor's seal block.

The signature line above it is **blank**.

I run my bare thumb across the empty black rule under my flashlight beam, feeling the cold paper grain catch against my skin. **`November 11, 2014.`** Seven days before my father collapsed behind his drafting table on Custom House Wharf during my sophomore year of college.

I remember that Tuesday night in November as if the phone were still pressed to my ear—sitting on the scuffed linoleum of my dorm hallway in Orono while a Portland patrol sergeant told me my fifty-two-year-old father had suffered an acute coronary alone at his desk, with no witnesses and no warning signs. The hospital called it sudden cardiac arrest. Nobody ordered a full toxicology panel on a middle-aged marine surveyor who drank too much black coffee and worked late in a cold wharf office. When Nina and I packed up his office on Custom House Wharf three days after the service, his steel wall safe stood swung open and empty except for a brass parallel ruler, a stack of blank survey forms, and three estate ledger boxes—the 1998 binder already gone.

They didn't bury a toxicology file in a Boston vault because they didn't need to. When the thirty-year Rhode Island quarantine lease expired in 2014, Garroway needed Thomas Calder's survey stamp one last time to certify Ward C's cold storage as decommissioned on paper while upgrading the lockers underneath. My father took Garroway's bargain in 1998 to save Elena's life—and when he refused to sign the 2014 cover-up, seven days later his heart stopped in an empty Portland office.

I know what Arthur Cutler's autopsy looked like in Vermont eleven days ago. Concentrated **`CutlerCor`** leaves no needle mark and mimics an acute coronary unless a lab knows to run a potassium-channel assay. I can't prove the dose on this blank sheet of paper—not without a body exhumed or Garroway's own mouth—but staring at that unsigned line dated seven days before my father's funeral tells me everything I need to know. Every piece of the 2014 puzzle snaps shut without a single missing tooth.

I slide the 2014 upgrade work order and the unsigned decommissioning certificate into my duffel beside the 1998 binder, zip the oilskin liner shut, and pull the oak file-room door open an inch.

***

The rear corridor sits empty, lit only by a dull orange seam beneath the closed Chief Surgeon's Office door.

I step out into the hall, pull the oak door shut, and turn my father's **`"M"`** master key quietly in the brass mortise lock so Garroway won't see an unlocked door when he comes back down the hall. My watch glows `9:18 PM`. Outside, the wind slams sleet against the mansard eaves, and somewhere below the seawall the rising flood tide booms against the granite riprap as it nears high water.

Down at the front of the house, the heavy exterior oak portico door creaks open, letting in a cold blast of sleet and the sharp metallic *tick-thud* of Conrad Vale's right boot on the granite porch.

"Pier gate's chained and the launch bilge is pumped," Conrad's gravel voice carries through the thin pine partition from the front vestibule. "I'm walking the south seawall once before the squall hits."

"Check the Steam Plant breakers on your way back," Garroway answers from the porch threshold, his tone calm over the wind. "Margot, stay by the stove until I'm back from the pier."

The front door thuds shut. Heavy boots crunch off down the gravel steps, followed by a second pair of leather soles heading south toward the Coal Pier. Inside the Chief Surgeon's Office, a chair scrapes across the floorboards and Margot's heels click briefly toward the front coat closet, then back into the office as a kettle hisses on the cast-iron stove and a poker clangs against the grate.

I move fast along the dark hallway wall, keeping my boots on the painted pine border where the joists don't groan, my shoulder brushing the peeling institutional green wainscoting. The sharp tang of charred bond paper and sweet Latakia tobacco drifts beneath the Chief Surgeon's door, thick enough to coat the back of my throat. I slip past the cracked office door while Margot's back is turned to the stove.

Even in a two-second glance through the gap, the room looks like an active furnace bay: a cast-iron drum stove glowing cherry-red along its belly, two gray plastic tubs stuffed with shredded bond paper, and a black **portable RF cellular jammer** sitting on the windowsill with its stubby twin antennas blinking red. A heavy brass ashtray overflows with gray Latakia pipe dottle beside a half-empty tin of Turkish tobacco and a tin cup of cooling black coffee. Beside the tubs, a neat stack of empty green banker's boxes waits against the wainscoting, ready for whatever comes out of the file room next.

I clear the doorway silently and slip through the open archway into the front **communications alcove** overlooking the side yard.

A portable kerosene space heater ticks softly in the corner, throwing a faint warm haze that smells of **Latakia pipe ash** and hot brass. My numb fingers sting as the dry heat hits my damp wool sleeves. On a scarred mahogany clerk's desk beside the shuttered window sits a ruggedized satellite router with amber traffic LEDs flickering, a live **marine VHF base-station transceiver** wired to a heavy coiled handset, a thick coaxial antenna feed snaking up through the ceiling molding, and Victor Garroway's open encrypted laptop. Beside the window sash, a narrow wooden latch holds the lower storm pane slightly swollen in its frame—my only fast exit if footsteps come back down the hall.

My eyes drop straight to the laptop screen.

***

A green banking-portal confirmation window glows against the dark glass, linked to a gold-plated hardware security token plugged into the USB port—**State Street Escrow Account `#884-19B`**.

Even from across the desk, I recognize the account number from Page 44 of Arthur Cutler's *Caretaker's Ledger* in Vermont, and from the 2003–2025 survivor annuity vouchers sitting inside my duffel. Beside the laptop sits Margot's leather card case, tossed carelessly onto the blotter where Garroway must have lifted her corporate token while she argued with him by the stove. Beneath the green banner sits an active **SWIFT offshore wire authorization**, timestamped `9:15 PM` and marked **`CLEARING — IN PROGRESS`**:

**`TRANSFER AMOUNT: $6,400,000.00 (REMAINING ESCROW RESERVE)`**
**`ORIGIN: STATE STREET ESCROW #884-19B (K-H FIDUCIARY)`**
**`BENEFICIARY: HARBOR REACH HOLDINGS LTD. — GRAND CAYMAN (SOLE SIGNATORY: V. GARROWAY)`**

Six point four million dollars. Every last cent of Margot Kiefer-Hall's Boston escrow reserve, routed out from under her nose into Victor Garroway's sole-signature Cayman shell while she stood ten feet away arguing over 1998 trust papers. Garroway isn't taking Margot off Barrow Island at the `00:30` ebb. He's draining her vault, leaving her signed 2003–2025 annuity sheets and her fingerprints in the burn room, and letting federal marshals find whatever is left of her at dawn.

I pull my phone from my coat pocket, lean across the mahogany desk, and snap two sharp photographs of the green Cayman wire screen, making sure the timestamp and the Grand Cayman beneficiary line sit crisp in the frame.

Beside the laptop, a small thermal receipt printer spits out a narrow curl of white paper—the bank's hard-copy wire confirmation—and stops with a soft mechanical *snip*. I tear the warm slip free, fold it twice, and tuck it into my coat pocket beside my father's master key, sweeping a cheap blue **ballpoint pen** off the yellow legal pad beside the printer into the same pocket without thinking twice.

Then I turn to the **marine VHF base-station transceiver** humming softly on standby above the desk, its green power diode glowing steady against the dark paneling. The channel readout is locked to `16`, the coast-guard distress frequency, though the squelch knob is twisted all the way down to mute the static. I depress the brass release tab on the side of the cradle, unclip the heavy **coiled VHF handset**—checking the standard four-pin modular plug at the base of the cord—and wrap the rubberized cord once around my palm. This is the exact piece I need to bridge the 24-volt repeater eighty feet up on the **North Ledge Signal Tower**.

I tuck the coiled handset into my coat pocket, reach down to close my duffel zipper—and freeze.

In the black windowpane above the mahogany desk, behind my own pale reflection, a second silhouette stands motionless in the alcove doorway. Her tailored wool coat is dry from the stove, and the dim green glow from the laptop catches the edge of a compact matte-steel frame in her right hand.

Cold steel presses hard against the base of my skull, just above my wet wool coat collar. A faint scent of expensive bergamot cuts through the Latakia smoke, sharp and cold in the narrow alcove.

"Drop the handset on the desk, Tessa," **Margot Kiefer-Hall** whispers, the compact `.380` pistol muzzle biting into my skin. "Slowly. One sudden twitch and I put you through the glass."
