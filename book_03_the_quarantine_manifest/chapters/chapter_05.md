## Chapter 5

Ten minutes until he vents the cellar.

I pull back from the rusted shutter gap, cross the warped floorboards of the **East Isolation Ward**, and follow the gray twilight toward the center stairwell. The interior oak door leading to the main landing hangs off one brass hinge, swollen tight against the granite threshold by decades of bay damp. I brace my left palm against the plaster wall, plant my boot heel beside the lower jamb, and shove until the wet wood gives with a sharp splintering pop.

Beyond the door rises an **interior iron fire staircase**, its perforated cast-iron treads slick with condensation from the unheated masonry. Above me, the second-floor **Quarantine Gallery** wraps around a narrow central lightwell capped with wired storm glass, turning the upper floor into a dim gray cage smelling of damp lime plaster, pigeon feathers, and salt rust. Cold sleet ticks steadily against the overhead wired panes like gravel thrown from the dark.

I climb to the second-floor landing, keeping my boots on the outside stringer where the iron bolts into the brick wall so the treads don't ring. Every step pulls on my bruised left hip from the chimney fall, and my wet trouser cuffs slap cold against my shins.

Down the gallery corridor stand six shuttered isolation cubicles, their glass partition frames stripped bare and their iron bedframes stacked against the far wall. Frosted cobwebs hang thick from the cast-iron transom rods, swaying gently in the draft sneaking through the mortar cracks. At the north end, the iron stairs continue up to a third-floor attic landing sealed by a padlocked steel roof hatch—no way out onto the slate roof without silhouetting myself against the open bay. Beside the second-floor stairwell rail sits an old **wheeled iron stretcher dolly**, its canvas litter rotted to gray shreds over heavy tubular steel. I test one cast-iron caster with my boot toe. The wheel turns smoothly, its axle packed with old cup grease that hasn't hardened—and the front caster sits inches from the top lip of the straight iron stair run, held back only by a rusted foot-brake dog.

At the far rear corner of the gallery, behind a louvered wooden service door, a narrow **iron service ladder** drops straight down a dark vertical shaft beside the main steam risers—an old orderly bypass built to reach the sub-floor pipe trenches without tracking contagious-ward mud through the main corridor. I test the top rung with my gloved hand, confirming the bolts hold solid in the brick, then pull the louvered door nearly shut and step back to the stairwell.

A vertical **cast-iron radiator chase** runs up the masonry pier directly behind the landing rail, its sheet-metal access panel hanging open on a single rivet. A salt-crusted brass steam valve sits cold against the brick. I kneel and shine my hooded flashlight beam down the narrow gap beside the cold steam riser.

Two floors straight below, a faint amber reflection glints off wet concrete near the Ward C basement threshold. The pipe chase drops all the way past the first-floor sluice room into the ceiling plenum above the `4°C` morgue vault. Whatever happens down in that cellar will carry straight up the hollow iron pipe like a speaking tube.

I click my flashlight off, check my watch—`4:10 PM`—and move to the leeward gallery window overlooking the basement bulkhead well.

Through a quarter-inch gap between the corrugated storm shutter and the brick sill, I can see the sloped iron bulkhead doors below, the granite steps glistening with sleet, and the red CO₂ break-glass box hanging shattered beside the curb. Already the gray sky over Narragansett Bay is darkening into true winter night, the wind driving sleet pellets horizontally across the granite yard. Fifty paces north—no, just up the dark gravel slope—a thin gray ribbon of smoke lifts from the **Customs House** brick chimney and flattens sideways into the bay mist. Garroway and Margot aren't waiting for daylight to burn their paper trail.

Two minutes later, the steel toe-tap clicks down the gravel path.

***

He doesn't carry a flashlight in his left hand this time. He carries a **heavy black rubber half-face respirator** strapped over his nose and mouth, a compact **portable exhaust blower** slung by its yellow cord in his left hand, and a **matte-black 9mm pistol** drawn in his right.

*Tick-thud. Tick-thud.*

The steel toe-tap on his right dress boot hits the top granite riser beside the bulkhead well. He sets the blower on the wet slush, plugs its heavy pigtail into a weatherproof junction box mounted beside the red CO₂ pull-station, and slides back the exterior steel bolt.

The twin iron bulkhead leaves groan upward against their greased hinges. A thin white plume of displaced CO₂ vapor curls out into the driving sleet, biting faintly sour even up at the second-floor shutter gap.

He drops the flexible yellow exhaust duct down the granite steps, flips the blower toggle, and stands back against the brick wall with his pistol raised toward the stairwell while the fan roars for four solid minutes, churning the gray air above the well. His head doesn't turn once toward the upper shutters. He expects a corpse on the floor grate—a Portland clerk who walked into an empty hospital and never walked out.

I don't stay at the shutter. I creep back to the open vertical **radiator chase** beside the second-floor stairwell, keep my navy duffel strapped tight across my shoulders so I can move in three seconds, and kneel with my ear close to the cold cast-iron riser. My wet wool coat drips ice water onto the dusty floorboards, and the thin pink scar on my left wrist aches where the damp cuff rubs it raw. Below, the blower motor whines down to a dead stop, leaving only the hiss of sleet against the wired-glass lightwell overhead.

Down below, the blower fan clicks off.

A moment later, the steel toe-tap descends the granite steps. *Tick-thud. Tick-thud.*

The brass lever on the tin-clad fire door depresses. The door swings inward against its rubber stop.

Silence holds for five seconds—broken only by the low sixty-cycle purr of the submarine cable transformer and the metallic rattle of the four `4°C` refrigeration lockers cycling in the cold. Even through two floors of brick and iron pipe, I hear the rhythmic *hiss-click* of his respirator exhale valve as he sweeps the vault corners with his pistol light.

Then his boots cross the concrete drain grate.

He stops beside the stainless-steel autopsy table. Through the hollow radiator chase, I hear the sharp intake of breath behind his rubber respirator valves, followed by the heavy scrape of the yellow hydraulic hand-truck shifting as he checks the floor around the pried Portland crate. A booted toe kicks aside the damp pine slats, then pauses. Next comes the crisp latch-click of Lockers 1 through 4—checking to make sure the five remaining `M/V Northern Cross` crates haven't been touched.

"What the hell," he mutters, his voice muffled through the respirator filter.

His footsteps cross fast to the rear masonry wall of the vault. Iron scrapes against brick as he pulls my loose soot-blackened half-brick out of the **1890s autoclave clean-out hatch** and hauls the rusted sterilization door wide open. Cold bay surf booms up the tidal culvert into the vault, echoing faintly up the iron pipe beside my cheek.

***

A leather holster snaps shut below as he sheaths his pistol for a second to unclip a handheld **marine VHF radio** from his trench coat lapel.

Static hisses up the pipe chase, then a clean squelch tone on Channel 72.

"Mr. Garroway," the operative says, his gravel voice tight now over the roar of surf in the culvert. "It wasn't a salvage squatter. The brass mortise lock on the tin door was turned clean without scratches. Whoever came in had a key—and only one key was ever missing. It's Calder's daughter. No body on the drain grate. The rear autoclave hatch is pried open from the inside, the crowbar is gone, and Thomas Calder's 1998 binder is missing from the table. Lockers One through Three are still sealed."

A three-second pause crackles through the speaker, followed by Victor Garroway's calm, unhurried baritone over the airwaves.

*"Check the upper ward immediately,"* Garroway says. *"Margot and I are sealing the Customs House files and sweeping the pier. If she reaches the steam tunnels or the signal tower, put her down. Do not let her near the launch."*

"Copy," the operative says.

I hold my breath beside the open radiator chase, keeping my boots flat on the dusty pine floorboards so not a single dry nail creaks.

Down in the morgue vault, the heavy autoclave hatch clangs shut. A sharp beam of high-lumen white light stabs upward inside the brick flue—so bright that a pale halo leaks through the rusted damper seams behind the first-floor sluice room and glints faintly along the dark plaster below me. A second later, he spots the dislodged sluice-room grate on the first floor or guesses the vertical shaft stops there.

He knows I climbed the chimney. He knows I'm inside Ward C.

I stand up silently beside the second-floor stairwell, gripping the **forged-steel crowbar** in my right hand while the split scar on my index knuckle throbs hot against the damp leather. I can't outrun a 9mm pistol across open gravel in the sleet, and I can't hold a dark hallway without an exit. My eyes lock onto the **wheeled iron stretcher dolly** sitting inches from the top lip of the straight iron stairs—and behind me, the louvered door hiding the vertical service ladder to the sub-floor pipe trench. If I kick the dolly's foot-brake dog loose as he climbs, forty pounds of tubular iron will buy me five seconds to drop down the orderly shaft into the dark utility tunnels before he clears the smoke and dust.

Below me, the tin-clad vault door slams shut. His footsteps pound back up the granite steps—not out into the sleet toward the Customs House, but around the leeward corner toward the **interior fire-stairwell door** I forced five minutes ago. He moves fast, breathing hard through the rubber respirator, the leather sling of his radio snapping against his oilskin coat.

At ground level, the swollen oak door scrapes open against the threshold, throwing a fresh draft of freezing bay air up the stairwell.

Then the first **steel toe-tap** strikes the bottom iron tread of the interior staircase, ringing loud and metallic up the dark masonry shaft straight toward my floor.
