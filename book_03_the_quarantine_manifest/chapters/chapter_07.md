## Chapter 7

Two pairs of boots turn away from the launch rail and crunch back up the sleet-slick timber planks toward the shore end of the **Coal Pier**.

*Tick-thud. Tick-thud.*

Conrad's steel toe-tap keeps pace beside Garroway's quiet leather soles until the wind off Block Island Sound swallows their voices. A moment later, a heavy iron pier gate clangs shut at the landward head of the planks, followed by the crisp click of a padlock snapping home. Above the pilings, the amber deck lantern swings in a slow arc, throwing shifting stripes of yellow light across the black chop.

I wait until the echoes die completely to make sure neither man doubled back into the dark. Freezing bay water surges up around my shins inside the culvert, numbing my ankles again. Then I wedge the flat chisel tip of my **forged-steel crowbar** behind the rusted iron bar of the storm-culvert grate, bracing my left palm against the hinge pin so the heavy iron won't clang against the granite piling when it gives.

Barnacle crust and century-old salt scale pop loose with a dry snap. I ease the hinged grate outward just wide enough to squeeze my navy duffel and wet shoulders through, keeping my boots braced on the narrow granite footing below the tide line while black swells slap against my calves.

Above me on the pier's shoreward apron, the **yellow hydraulic hand-truck** sits parked beside the iced granite slipway rails and a coil of spare anchor chain, its pneumatic tires gleaming wet under the swinging deck lantern.

A timber maintenance ladder—furred with frozen kelp and slick green algae—climbs the inside of the nearest pier piling straight to the stern mooring cleat. I hook the crowbar back behind my belt, grab the icy wooden rungs with my raw, bleeding right knuckle screaming inside the wet glove, and haul myself onto the lower deck rub-rail of the cabin launch.

The fiberglass hull pitches under my boots, rolling gently against black rubber fender buoys while hemp dock lines groan against the pilings.

Painted in faded gold leaf across the salt-streaked transom sits the vessel's home-port name: ***HARBOR BELL — PORTLAND, ME***.

Victor Garroway's private boat. Registered out of the exact same Custom House slip where my father used to moor his wooden survey launch before he died—and before Harbor Mutual's civil judgment forced me to sell off the last of his harbor gear just to make rent. Even the varnished teak swim platform at the stern carries the faint brass ring of a Portland harbor master's seasonal mooring tag.

I drop silently into the recessed cockpit, keeping low below the white coaming while sleet hisses across the fiberglass deck and freezes in thin gray crusts along the scuppers.

***

The aft cockpit smells sharply of raw marine diesel, wet hemp line, and cold galvanized steel.

Lashed tight beneath a stiff green canvas tarp against the stern davit winch sits a heavy rectangular steel box wrapped in thick loops of **galvanized anchor chain**—deadweight ballast rigged to carry it straight to the bottom of the Block Island trench once the launch clears the breakwater at midnight. Caught in the chain's clevis hook clings a damp sliver of pine slat from the Portland Custom House Wharf crate Conrad pried open in Ward C. Beside the winch drum lies a set of bolt cutters and a spare brass shackle pin.

I kneel beside the winch frame, pull my **folding steel multi-tool** from my coat pocket, and use the pliers jaw to unhook two brass bungee snaps along the port corner of the tarp without shifting the heavy chain links. Even a single clink of steel on steel would carry down the pilings through the wet air.

I peel the stiff canvas back slightly and cup my gloved hand over my flashlight lens.

Beneath the tarp sits **Refrigerated Steel Containment Crate `#06`**. Condensation beads across its insulated stainless-steel lid, still holding the `4°C` chill from Ward C's Locker 4. Four heavy brass corner-latches hold the lid compressed against a thick neoprene seal, each wired with a dull gray lead customs tag identical to the intact tags I saw inside Lockers 1 through 3. An internal lithium battery pack—wired to a small green digital readout recessed into the side flange—keeps the cryogenic gel core steady at four degrees without shore power. Riveted directly above the front clasp sits a tarnished brass identification plate stamped in crisp industrial block letters:

**`B.I.Q.S.-04 / NORTHERN CROSS — SPECIMEN 06 / DONOR #12-C (CALDER, E. M.) — 4°C CRYOGENIC LOCK.`**

My throat goes dry against the cold sleet air.

Twenty-eight years. Twenty-eight winters while Nina and I grew up believing our mother's body was ash and white sea roses scattered off Eastern Promenade, her tissue blocks and vials sat locked inside stainless steel on a dark granite island, kept cold by a Boston trust fund and an insurance underwriter's submerged power cable. Every time I stood on the Portland seawall with my father on Mom's birthday, he was looking south toward Rhode Island, carrying this weight in silence because Victor Garroway held our foster-care threat over his head. And three years ago, when I flagged the compressor pallet weights on Commercial Street Wharf, Garroway didn't just frame me to protect a warehouse—he framed me to keep Thomas Calder's daughters away from this crate.

I run my gloved fingers down the cold steel face of the crate to the case-hardened clasp.

No ordinary padlock. A recessed **high-security tubular key cylinder** sits flush inside the steel shroud—seven-pin radial brass, immune to a flat multi-tool blade or a standard tension wrench. Even if I had half an hour with a full locksmith's roll, I couldn't pick a radial tubular cylinder in freezing sleet without snapping the pick tip. And if I attack the hardened shroud with my crowbar, the steel clatter will bring Conrad running across the pier in seconds, while a single slip of the pry tip could crack the internal borosilicate canisters inside.

The tubular key is sitting on Conrad's belt ring—or in Victor Garroway's coat pocket.

I refasten both brass tarp snaps cleanly so the canvas lies flat, tuck the loose corner of the wet green canvas back under the chain loop, and wipe my wet glove prints off the damp fiberglass coaming with my wool scarf before creeping forward along the starboard side deck toward the launch's wheelhouse cabin.

***

The sliding aluminum cabin door isn't locked. I ease it back three inches—just enough to slip sideways into the dark helm station without waking the latch—and pull it nearly shut behind me.

Inside, the cabin smells of stale **Latakia pipe tobacco**, damp vinyl cushions, and cold bilge diesel. Sleet rattles steadily against the tinted windshield glass, drumming a low, cold rhythm over the fiberglass deckhouse.

Under a hooded sliver of flashlight, I sweep the teak chart table beside the helm. Pinned beneath a brass parallel ruler identical to the one my father kept in Portland lies a folded **NOAA Block Island Sound bathymetry chart**. Fresh red grease pencil—the exact same wax stick that circled my mother's name in the 1998 binder—marks a tight `X` over a deep-water trench south-southwest of Block Island, beside a handwritten tidal window: **`00:30 EBB — 200 FT`**.

Beside the parallel ruler sits a brass pipe-tamper coated in dark Latakia resin and an unlocked Harbor Mutual marine underwriting clipboard. I flip the top sheet up with my thumb: blanked-out coastal salvage clearance forms for the *Harbor Bell*, pre-signed at the bottom in thick black fountain ink by Victor Garroway for a midnight "harbor buoy inspection" out of Point Judith.

Next, I inspect the helm console. Twin-diesel throttles sit in neutral, their brass ignition keys pulled—and directly beneath the starter buttons sits an empty **magnetic kill-switch fob slot**. Without Garroway's lanyard fob clipped into that plastic socket, crossing the starter wires behind the dash won't even crank the fuel solenoids. I lift the corner of the rubber deck mat to check the engine-hatch latch—bolted down with two stainless security hex screws so nobody can jump the starter solenoids from the bilge either.

I check the overhead electronics box last.

A modern **marine VHF radio** is bolted into the teak bracket above the compass—its liquid-crystal screen dead, its auxiliary power breaker switched off at the main DC panel, and its **coiled handset microphone cord unplugged** at the jack. Below the helm seat, the heavy steel cuddy safe is bolted straight through the fiberglass stringers and spun locked, hiding the mic and spare keys behind quarter-inch plate.

Garroway didn't leave a single working wire on the pier. Even my own phone, tested one last time against the cracked cabin window, still shows `NO SERVICE` and ticks steadily against the RF jammer.

If I want to reach the mainland before the `00:30` ebb, I'm not getting off Barrow Island in the *Harbor Bell*, and I'm not calling the Coast Guard from its helm. That leaves only the **red battery LED** and directional **microwave dish** I saw blinking atop the **North Ledge Signal Tower**, and the **Customs House** radio room where Garroway and Margot are burning the files.

I check my watch: `5:56 PM`. Back in Portland, Nina is safe under Nora Halvorsen's protection, with no idea I'm cut off out here—and without her biostatistics, I'm staring at coded specimen numbers and assay lot columns I can't read alone. I know maritime bills of lading, crane weights, and tampered locks, but whatever Garroway and Cutler Biotech built out of those twelve patients in 1998 is written in a language only my sister knows how to crack. Every minute I spend trapped on this pier, that paper trail in the Customs House is turning to ash.

I crack the sliding aluminum door, slip quietly back out onto the iced starboard gunwale, and wipe my wet boot smears off the painted fiberglass with my coat sleeve before crouching behind the aft cockpit coaming. Sleet ticks sharp against the stiff canvas tarp over my mother's steel crate, and the black bay swells slap hollow against the transom.

Thirty paces up the shore causeway, a heavy metallic *clang* echoes down the Coal Pier as the rusted iron side door of the **Disinfection Steam Plant** bangs wide open against its brick stop, spilling pale yellow worklight across the snow-crusted gravel and the rusted iron mooring bollards.

A high-powered halogen searchlight flares blinding white through the driving sleet, sweeping across the iced granite slipway rails straight toward the *Harbor Bell*'s stern.
