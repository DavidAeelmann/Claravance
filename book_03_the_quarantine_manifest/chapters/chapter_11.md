## Chapter 11

The narrow wooden service stairs behind the Customs House furnace creak once under my left boot heel, then hold silent as I shift my weight to the outer stringer against the brick chimney wall.

At the top of the flight, a warped pine pantry door stands ajar by two inches, opening onto a dim rear corridor that smells sharply of **burning bond paper**, **birch woodsmoke**, and **Victor Garroway's sweet Latakia tobacco**. Down the hall to the left, orange firelight flickers beneath the closed oak door of the Chief Surgeon's Office, where the heavy cross-cut shredder chews through another sheaf of paper before dropping back into a low electrical hum.

I press my back flat against the pantry shelves—lined with dusty nineteenth-century apothecary jars and salt-stained ledger trays—and listen for twenty seconds. Through the thick oak panels down the hall, a cast-iron stove door clangs shut, followed by the soft scrape of a brass poker against embers. Nobody is standing in the corridor.

Directly across the narrow hall from the pantry sits a solid paneled oak door fitted with a tarnished marine-brass mortise escutcheon—identical to the faceplate on the tin-clad fire door in Ward C.

My father's brass key sits cold between my bare fingers.

I step quietly across the dusty pine floorboards, slide the heavy notched blade into the keyhole, and brace my thumb against the bow stamped **`M`**.

For half a second the rusted ward tumblers resist against my split right index knuckle. Then the brass key turns clean through one full rotation, throwing back the heavy deadbolt with a soft, oiled *click*.

**Master.**

My father didn't just carry a spare key to a single basement morgue wing in 1998. He carried the **station master key** for all of **Quarantine Station No. 4**—and Victor Garroway never knew whether a dead surveyor's daughters still kept it in Portland.

I ease the heavy oak door inward, slip inside with my navy duffel, and pull the latch quiet behind me before turning the thumb-turn on the inside brass mortise plate.

***

The **Customs House archival file room** is windowless, unheated, and lined wall to wall with gray four-drawer steel filing cabinets and stacked bankers' boxes bound in frayed cotton twine. The air smells of dry rag bond, rusted paperclips, and cold plaster. Half the cabinet drawers stand pulled open an inch, their brass label frames empty where Garroway's crew stripped decades of port-clearance ledgers before daylight.

No woodstove here. No shredder. Everything queued inside this room was pulled out of storage by Garroway's team at `3:30 AM` to be hauled down to the Chief Surgeon's stove in batches once the paper trail from the main office finishes burning.

I pull my wool scarf tight around my neck, click on my heavy aluminum flashlight with my thumb cupped over the lens, and check my watch: `8:22 PM`. Four hours until the `00:30` ebb.

Three open steel transit boxes sit directly on the center oak sorting table, their lids propped back like Garroway and Conrad already sorted them by burn priority.

I open the top box first and pull out a thick black-bound **Cutler Biotech — Institutional Revenue & Assay Summary (`1999–2026`)**, cross-referenced to **Kiefer-Hall Fiduciary** quarterly bond audits.

My eyes track down the cumulative revenue column on the final summary sheet, and my chest goes tight against my wet coat.

In Vermont eleven days ago, Simone Cutler poisoned Arthur Cutler to steal **`$38,000,000`** in Class-A voting shares before Friday at five. I thought thirty-eight million was the whole empire.

It was only the piece the family fought over.

Under my pinched flashlight beam, **`CutlerCor`'s cumulative gross revenue since 1999 totals `$984,400,000`**—nearly **one billion dollars** built on a single synthetic cardiac compound, billed quarter after quarter to regional surgical centers from Boston to Chicago. Stamped across every annual FDA recertification and formulation appendix in the binder—in `2014`, `2019`, and `2023`—is the same baseline reference code: **`NC-1998 COHORT / PRIMARY BENCHMARK: #12-C (CALDER, E.M.)`**.

Every time Cutler Biotech reformulated *CutlerCor*, every time the FDA demanded stability benchmarks, they tested the batch against the pathology blocks and plasma aliquots sitting at `4°C` in Ward C. Three hundred dollars a vial in hospital billing, multiplied across twenty-seven years of cardiac bypasses, all anchored to one twenty-nine-year-old woman's tissue lot from Block Island Sound.

They didn't keep my mother's crate on Barrow Island out of sentiment or carelessness. They couldn't destroy the evidence because **the evidence was the product**.

Until Nina and I handed the *Caretaker's Ledger* to AUSA Nora Halvorsen at St. Johnsbury ER. Once federal prosecutors had the `B.I.Q.S.-04` notary seal, keeping those crates on the island became worth less than the risk of getting caught with them—so Garroway came here before dawn to burn the paper and sink the last crate in the Block Island trench.

I pull out my phone, snap three sharp, silent photographs of the billion-dollar revenue sheet, and reach into the second transit box.

Inside sits a fat red accordion folder stamped **`COMMERCIAL STREET WHARF — WAREHOUSE #4 (OCT 2023)`**.

I flip the elastic strap off the cover. Paperclipped to the inside flap is Conrad Vale's signed salvage manifest—proving the fourteen-hundredweight "cotton yarn" crates I flagged in Portland were Ward C's replacement compressors, rotated through Warehouse #4 while the submarine power cable was laid across Narragansett Bay. Beside it sit photocopies of my own `2023` Harbor Mutual audit memos, underlined in Garroway's thick black fountain ink, alongside his internal memo ordering my lockbox framing and the `$42,000` defamation suit to bury my audit before federal marine investigators ever looked at the `B.I.Q.S.-04` stamp.

And clipped to the back of the 2023 rotation log sits a single carbon **contract-lab shipment voucher** dated **`November 2, 2023`**.

My thumb freezes on the paper edge.

During the 2023 rotation, while the six containment crates sat in Portland between compressor swaps, Garroway's courier shipped a set of **duplicate paraffin tissue blocks** to an outside mainland contract laboratory for FDA stability recertification. No patient names appear on the voucher—only coded **lab lot numbers** paired with cryptic two-letter tissue suffixes and a stamped **`GLP ARCHIVE RETENTION`** box checked at the bottom margin.

I stare at the grid under my 10x loupe, feeling the same helplessness from the Steam Plant coal loft tighten around my ribs. Every lot code ends in a dash and a two-digit batch suffix, matching the same alphanumeric pattern I saw scribbled in pencil down the margin of my father's 1998 assay appendix. I can't decode which lot number belongs to Elena Calder, or whether the contract lab ever returned those duplicates to Barrow Island. Only Nina's biostatistics can match those `2023` lot codes back to the `1998` assay columns in my duffel. I snap two quick photos of the shipment voucher anyway, hoping the camera lens catches what my eyes can't read.

***

A floorboard creaks sharply in the front hallway outside.

I kill my flashlight instantly, holding my breath in the pitch dark behind the oak sorting table while the brass doorknob rattles once from the corridor side.

"Victor," Margot Kiefer-Hall's voice snaps through the thick oak panel, sharp enough to make my shoulders flinch. "I know you locked it from the inside or pocketed the ring. We are not burning the 1998 charter instruments until I read every page."

"Back to the stove, Margot," Garroway calls back from down the hall, his tone mild and unhurried. "Conrad is finishing the pier clearance. We'll clear the file room together in twenty minutes."

"I'm not leaving this island without those trust pages," Margot mutters against the door seam before her heels click angrily away down the pine corridor toward the front parlor, followed by the heavy *thunk* of the Chief Surgeon's Office door closing.

I wait ten slow counts, click my hooded flashlight back on, and drop to one knee beside the **third transit box** on the floor.

Sitting right on top, bound in stiff blue legal backing and stamped **`KIEFER-HALL FIDUCIARY — CHARTER TRUST INSTRUMENT (M/V NORTHERN CROSS, NOV 1998)`**, is the exact packet Margot Kiefer-Hall skipped half a million in federal bail to recover.

I skim the operative clauses under my 10x loupe. The trust instrument is drafted with surgical Boston precision: Kiefer-Hall Fiduciary is listed strictly as a **passive administrative trustee** holding offshore escrow funds on instructions from **Victor Garroway** and **Jonas Cutler**. If Margot hands this blue-backed instrument to AUSA Nora Halvorsen first, she trades Garroway for a cooperation deal and walks away as an unwitting paper trustee.

Then I lift the blue-backed trust folder—and uncover a slender gray **annuity disbursement ledger (`2003–2025`)** clipped directly beneath it.

I flip it open to the three survivor entries I saw stamped **`DISCHARGED — K-H TRUST ANNUITY`** in my father's binder an hour ago. Twenty-two years of quarterly hush-money wires out of **State Street Escrow `#884-19B`**—the exact same Boston escrow account Margot used in 2020 to pay off rural Vermont coroners for Arthur Cutler—to keep the three *Northern Cross* survivors silent.

And on every single annual disbursement authorization from `2003` through `2025`, right above the State Street bank seal, sits the unmistakable slanted fountain-ink signature of **Margot Kiefer-Hall**.

She wasn't a passive bystander. She paid the survivors herself for twenty-two years, signing off on every hush-money check long after her grandfather retired. If Halvorsen sees the blue charter instruments alone, Margot gets a cooperation deal; if Halvorsen sees the gray annuity ledger beside them, Margot dies in a federal cell.

I slide the blue-backed 1998 charter instruments, the 2003–2025 survivor annuity ledger, the billion-dollar revenue summary, and my 2023 Commercial Street Wharf framing file into the dry oilskin liner of my duffel. Every document weighs heavy against my bruised hip—not enough to stop a twin-diesel launch on its own, and useless unless I get off this rock alive or reach the North Ledge repeater. My watch glows `8:58 PM`. Time is running thin before Garroway and Margot come back down this dark hall with a heavy crate dolly.

As I reach to the bottom of the third transit box to check the last loose manila folder before leaving—dated the exact month of my sophomore year of college when my father died—my flashlight beam catches a faded crimson rubber stamp across its tab:

**`B.I.Q.S.-04 — COLD STORAGE UPGRADE (NOV 2014).`**
