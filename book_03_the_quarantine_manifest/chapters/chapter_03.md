## Chapter 3

The CO₂ hits the `4°C` ceiling like a busted steam main, roaring down through four brass nozzles in a blinding blast of dry-ice snow and freezing white fog that turbulent-mixes the entire vault in seconds.

I drop to one knee beside the incinerator plinth, shielding my eyes with my forearm while hooking the curved claw of the crowbar into the narrow gap of the cast-iron clean-out hatch. Visibility drops to zero in two heartbeats. Carbon dioxide doesn't wait politely at floor level when it leaves high-pressure ceiling nozzles—it churns the room into a freezing, sour-tasting whiteout that strips the oxygen out of the air while your chest still thinks it is pulling breath.

Already my head feels light, my temples pulsing hard against the wool collar of my coat. My ears pop from the sudden pressure surge inside the masonry vault. White frost rimes the yellow hydraulic hand-truck and the open door of Locker 4 as freezing vapor swirls past my face.

I brace my left shoulder against the whitewashed brick, plant both lug soles on the wet concrete, and haul backward with everything left in my back.

The rusted cast-iron hinges shriek. Lime mortar flakes down in gray sheets as the heavy hatch swings wide enough for my shoulders. Behind it yawns a pitch-black brick firebox smelling of century-old coal clinker, wet salt, and rotting kelp.

A fresh blast of CO₂ hisses off the stainless-steel lockers behind me, sweeping across my heels and stinging the backs of my eyes. I suck one shallow, burning breath through my wool scarf, shove my navy canvas duffel—heavy with the 1998 binder—through the hatch opening, and dive in after it.

My knees hit wet brick and freezing salt water.

I twist onto my hip inside the narrow brick incinerator chamber, grab the inside iron handle of the hatch, and haul the heavy door shut against the masonry frame. The broken exterior dog latch won't catch, so I grab a loose soot-blackened half-brick from the flooded firebox floor and jam the stone wedge tight between the inner door lip and the brick jamb, keeping my multi-tool safe in my coat pocket.

Then I press my gloved palm along the seam, packing wet coal soot and lime dust into the widest gap to slow the draft. Through the last finger-width crack before the iron door seats home, I see the red `4°C` digits of the digital manifold glowing faintly through the dense whiteout above Locker 4. Whoever pulled that red handle outside expects me to suffocate quietly on the drain grate within three minutes, leaving my father's 1998 binder right where they can wipe the table clean after the gas clears.

Outside in the vault, the CO₂ nozzles keep roaring for another twenty seconds, then taper to a wet hiss as the high-pressure tank bleeds empty. A faint metallic click echoes from the thermostat relay as the `4°C` compressors cycle back up under the strain of the discharge.

Inside the incinerator, the air is stagnant, metallic, and freezing—but breathable. My chest heaves against the wool lining of my coat as I wait for the dizziness behind my eyes to clear. My fingertips numb fast inside the damp leather gloves, and the wet wool over my knees already feels like sheet ice against the brick.

I click my flashlight back on and shine it around the curved brick chamber, watching black bay water glisten between the rusted bars beneath my boots.

***

I am crouched inside a domed nineteenth-century ward incinerator no wider than a shipping crate.

Beneath my boots, a heavy cast-iron floor grate covers the mouth of the **saltwater tidal drain culvert** sloping downward through the granite seawall toward the bay. Black Narragansett Bay water is already surging up through the iron bars with every outside swell, soaking my boot laces and sending freezing spray over my shins.

Ten minutes until `3:58 PM` low water, when the flood tide turns the riprap channel into a boiling rip and cuts off the Narragansett mail skiff.

I aim the flashlight beam straight up.

Directly above the brick firebox, a narrow vertical exhaust flue rises into total darkness—built to carry coal smoke and infectious ward ash up through the building's core so typhus and cholera spores wouldn't drift across the quarantine yard. Two iron damper rods cross the throat overhead, crusted with orange scale, and above them the brick shaft narrows to a tight rectangular chimney lined with slick soot and crumbling lime mortar.

My father used to talk about old coastal quarantine stations when I was a kid—Barrow Island, Gallops Island, House Island up in Casco Bay. He told me the nineteenth-century masons built them like stone chimneys inside brick shells so nothing living or dead could leave a contagious ward without passing through fire or salt water. He never told me that his own wife's specimen crate sat behind one of those brick walls while he sat at our kitchen table pretending she was buried under harbor water.

Right now, salt water is winning.

A cold swell slaps the outer riprap, and black bay water rises over the ankles of my boots before receding with a hollow, sucking rattle down the granite culvert. If the tide climbs past the firebox lip before I clear the throat, the culvert will seal me under the floor with CO₂ pooling on the other side of the hatch.

I zip my wool coat all the way to my throat, cinch the canvas duffel strap diagonally across my chest so the weight of the 1998 binder rides flat between my shoulder blades, and slide the heavy aluminum flashlight into my breast pocket with the lens pointed upward. Then I thread the crowbar vertically through the duffel's exterior webbing strap so the steel shaft rides flat along the canvas rather than locking my hips against the narrow brick walls.

No room to swing my arms. Only enough width to brace my back against the north brick course and walk my rubber lug soles up the south wall.

I reach up, test the lowest iron damper rod with my gloved right hand, and pull.

Orange scale rains into my eyes. I blink hard against the grit, test the rod a second time, and feel it flex slightly before biting solid into the masonry. I haul my boots off the flooded floor grate, press my shoulder blades hard against the wet brick behind me, and wedge my knees into the soot.

My right hand clamps onto the damper edge. Beneath the cold leather glove, the tight pink scar across my right index knuckle—barely three days free of its Vermont sutures—splits open against the rough iron, stinging sharp and hot as warm blood wets the wool lining.

I ignore the sting, set my heels against the opposite brick course, and push upward.

***

Inch by inch, I chimney up the dark brick shaft.

Cold sleet water drips from somewhere high above, turning the century-old soot into black grease under my rubber soles. Every time I shift my shoulders to gain height, the duffel strap saws across my collarbone, the hard leather spine of the 1998 binder digs between my shoulder blades, and the steel crowbar clanks softly against the mortar joints behind my shoulder.

Halfway up, I freeze with my boots wedged across the damp brick.

From somewhere outside the foundation wall comes a muffled metallic *clack*—someone testing the exterior slide-bolt on the Ward C bulkhead—followed by the brisk, receding *tick-thud* of a steel toe-tap heading back down the granite steps toward the boat slip. He isn't staying to watch the vault. He's walking toward the water with ten minutes left before low water turns the channel.

If he pays off the mail skiff—or sinks it—I don't get off this island tonight.

I shove off the wet brick harder, ignoring the raw scrape along my wrists and forearms where the lime mortar chews through my wet wool cuffs. My thighs quiver from holding my weight jammed across the narrow span while my lungs suck cold, lime-dusted air. Every muscle in my back remembers Vermont—dragging myself up a chimney chase in a burning lodge—except here there is no furnace warmth, only wet brick and the smell of dead bay kelp.

Below me, a heavy wave slams into the seaward mouth of the tidal culvert. The echo booms up the brick chimney like a cannon shot, followed by the rush of salt water surging higher into the incinerator firebox I just left.

My wool sleeves are plastered with black soot grease. My breath comes in short, ragged bursts that fog the upward beam of the flashlight in my coat pocket.

Then the beam catches a square **cast-iron clean-out grate** set into the east wall of the flue—the ash-and-soot access door for the first-floor **Contagious Ward sluice room**. Beyond the iron lattice, I can see a faint gray slice of shuttered daylight leaking through from the upper ward, along with the pale gleam of porcelain tile.

I wedge my hips tight between the brick walls, reach up with my bleeding right hand, and test the iron grate. Two rusted thumb-turns hold the frame shut from the room side, but the mortar around the lower sill has rotted to wet sand. If I can pry the bottom edge inward with the crowbar, the whole frame will pop into the sluice room, and I can drop the upper damper plate behind me before the CO₂ below ever works its way up the flue.

I exhale slowly, brace my left boot heel onto a narrow cast-iron mortar shelf jutting from the brick seam, and reach over my shoulder to unclip the crowbar from the duffel webbing. Soot-stained sleet trickles down my collar, biting cold against the back of my neck. My left forearm burns from pressing against the rough lime courses, and the leather strap of the duffel cuts deeper into my shoulder with every shallow breath.

The instant my full weight shifts onto my left heel, the corroded iron shelf shears clean out of the wet mortar and tumbles clattering down into the black water below.

My boot shoots into empty air. My hip slams hard against the soot-slick brick, jarring the duffel strap against my ribs as my gloved fingers grab blindly at the iron bars of the sluice-room grate twenty feet above the flooded culvert and the rising bay surf.
