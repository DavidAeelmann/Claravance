## Chapter 9

The iron ladder rungs leading up to the **Steam Plant** coal loft are slick with damp anthracite dust and condensation.

I climb quietly with my navy duffel strapped tight across my chest, keeping my bruised left hip clear of the steel rail until I pull myself onto the rough timber planks of the upper gallery. Above my head, sleet hammers the corrugated-iron roof in a steady, deafening roar that turns the dark loft into a hollow drum. Below, the black iron bellies of the 1890s sterilization retorts sit silent in the gloom.

I crawl into the sheltered corner behind the brick chimney breast where no flashlight beam from the lower aisle can sweep me from the doorway, unbuckle my duffel, and pull out the dry **spare wool sweater** from the oilskin liner. My teeth are chattering hard enough to ache. Before I touch a single page of paper, I peel off my soaked wool coat and boots, wring out my freezing wool socks over the loft edge, and pull the dry sweater over my damp thermal layer. Ten minutes out of the wind with dry wool against my chest stops the violent shivering before hypothermia can lock my fingers.

My watch glows `6:48 PM`. Twenty minutes until Conrad's next round, if his perimeter circuit runs on the half-hour.

I pull the 1998 binder out of the oilskin liner, tuck my damp leather gloves inside my coat to keep them from freezing stiff, and hold my flashlight pinched between my chin and shoulder with my loupe in my right hand. I open the cracked leather cover past the front page I already photographed in Ward C.

Inside the spine, three folded sheets and a clipped carbon appendix sit tucked behind the primary `M/V NORTHERN CROSS` manifest—papers that stayed bound inside the black leather after my father's firm folded, until Garroway pulled the binder out of storage for tonight's purge.

I smooth the first yellowed sheet over my thigh, anchoring the curled corners with my flashlight and my elbow.

It is the **November 19, 1998 Clinical Passenger & Cohort Transfer Log**, typed on plain unwatermarked bond paper and stapled to a Providence port-clearance slip. Twelve congenital cardiac patients boarded the *M/V Northern Cross* out of Providence under a private **Kiefer-Hall Fiduciary** medical grant—every one of them uninsured, desperate for surgical intervention after an **FDA clinical hold in 1997** stalled Cutler Biotech's experimental potassium-channel compound on the mainland. The names read like a cross-section of New England mill towns: a machinist's wife from Pawtucket, a retired schoolteacher from Fall River, a twenty-nine-year-old mother from Portland who had just delivered her second daughter.

Beside nine of the twelve names—ending with **`#12-C: CALDER, ELENA M.`**—sits a typed timestamp of cardiac arrest and a crate allocation number from `01` to `06`. Nine fatal QT-interval spikes. Thirty-six hours of offshore agony off Block Island, logged with the cold precision of a port-customs tally sheet.

Then my loupe catches the three remaining lines near the bottom of the column. Unlike the others, they carry no crate number and no time of death. Stamped in faded blue ink across all three entries is a single administrative disposition:

**`DISCHARGED — K-H TRUST ANNUITY.`**

Three survivors. Paid off through a Kiefer-Hall Fiduciary trust so they would never speak about what happened aboard that ship. No dollar figures sit beside their names here, only a Boston trust docket number and a recurring annual payout code. I trace the faint blue ink with my thumb, filing the phrase away beside Margot Kiefer-Hall's pale face under the Customs House lantern.

I pull the folded, yellowed **1998 `C.M.C.` carbon sheet** from my inner coat pocket and lay it edge to edge against the binder's top manifest. The carbon transfer lines up millimeter for millimeter—except the carbon copy I pulled out of Nina's Burlington lockbox stops cleanly above the blue annuity stamps and the handwritten death timestamps. Whoever tore the carbon out wanted Garroway's countersignature and the crate count preserved, without leaving the names of the survivors on a loose page.

***

I turn to the second folded sheet tucked into the binder's oilskin gusset pocket.

It is a **1998 Marine Casualty & Hull Inspection Addendum**, stamped `U.S. COAST GUARD — SECTOR PROVIDENCE (DUPLICATE)` on the top margin, though the bottom signature block carries no Coast Guard sector seal—only Victor Garroway's bold fountain-ink approval for a **`$24,000,000`** Harbor Mutual hull-and-loss payout on the scuttled *M/V Northern Cross*.

Halfway down the casualty column sits a single-line injury report dated `November 19, 1998 — 23:40 HRS`, cross-referenced to a Providence marine inspector's license number:

**`DECK OFFICER / INSPECTOR: VALE, CONRAD — CRUSHED RIGHT FOREFOOT (DAVIT CABLE PARTING DURING NIGHT OFFLOAD).`**

I stare at the name under my loupe until the letters blur slightly in the cold.

**Conrad Vale.**

A Coast Guard marine inspector who moonlighted as the *Northern Cross*'s deck officer—which explains how a vessel carrying twelve unapproved cardiac trial patients and falsified lifeboat manifests cleared a federal hull inspection out of Providence in 1998. A snapped davit cable crushed his right forefoot on the night they offloaded those six crates onto Barrow Island, leaving him with a permanent orthopedic steel toe-tap on his right dress boot.

Twenty-eight years of *tick-thud, tick-thud* on stone risers and iron grating. Twenty-eight years of making physical reality match whatever Victor Garroway wrote on a Harbor Mutual claim form.

I flip back to the primary manifest page—the one Garroway circled in red grease pencil—and look past my mother's name to the date-and-time stamps beside my father's **`C.M.C. — AUDIT 1998`** seal.

I stare at the ink until my eyes sting.

The audit stamp on the cover sheet is dated **`November 17, 1998 — 14:00 HRS`**. Two full days *before* the *Northern Cross* sailed out past Block Island. Two days before nine people died of QT-interval spikes.

I know how my father worked at his oak drafting table on Custom House Wharf. I spent half my childhood watching him line up his brass parallel ruler and test his ink nib on scrap paper before he ever touched a survey form. He never stamped a blank sheet by accident, and he never missed a date stamp in thirty years of marine surveying. He knowingly certified false cargo and passenger paperwork two days before the ship left Providence—because Victor Garroway offered him a clinical berth for my dying mother in exchange for a clean `Calder Maritime & Cargo` survey stamp. My father thought he was signing off on an insurance or regulatory dodge to save his wife's life.

Then I tilt the flashlight beam to the narrow white margin directly beside **`SPECIMEN CRATE 06`**.

Written later—not in crisp mechanical ink, but in shaky, pressing graphite in my father's unmistakable square draftsman's print—are eight words that cut deeper into the paper than any stamp:

**`I DID NOT KNOW WHAT WAS IN 06.`**

***

The sleet hammers harder against the corrugated iron above my head, drowning out every sound below the loft floor.

I sit in the dark coal loft with my back pressed against cold brick, staring at those eight graphite words until my eyes sting from the salt draft. My father wasn't clean. He took Garroway's bargain before anyone died, believing paper fraud was a small price for keeping his wife alive for his three-year-old daughter and his three-week-old baby. And on the night of the offload, when he opened the transfer log in Ward C and saw Elena's name beside `Crate 06`, it was already too late.

If he went to the Coast Guard, his own `November 17` signature put him in federal prison as a co-conspirator in a twenty-four-million-dollar scuttling—and put Nina and me into state foster care. So he wrote those eight words in the margin, hid the carbon sheet and his master brass key in a rusted lockbox, and carried the weight in silence every time we walked the Eastern Promenade seawall together.

Down below in the boiler room, a loose metal shutter rattles once in the bay gale, snapping me back to the cold planks under my boots. I check my watch: `7:19 PM`. No time left to sit in the dark with dead paper.

I force myself to turn to the final appendix clipped behind the casualty sheet, hoping for something concrete I can use tonight.

Instead, three pages of dense biostatistics tables stare back at me under the flashlight beam. Column after column of **QT-interval variance logs**, **potassium-channel assay benchmarks**, and coded specimen lot numbers paired with donor IDs like **`12-C / LV`** and **`04-A / RV`**. Handwritten pencil annotations run down the right-hand margin—concentration ratios, stability curves, baseline tolerances, and lot cross-references that mean nothing to a marine fraud auditor.

I trace the coded sample IDs with my bare thumb, a cold, helpless anger settling behind my ribs. Half the lot columns carry recent hand-penciled dates from `2023` beside the original `1998` donor numbers, as if someone kept pulling those same baseline curves out of cold storage for a quarter-century.

I know ships. I know crane weights, forged bills of lading, and tampered mortise locks. I don't know cardiac assays or biostatistics.

Nina does.

In Portland, safe inside AUSA Nora Halvorsen's witness detail, Nina spent six years reading clinical spreadsheets just like these. If she were sitting beside me in this coal loft for five minutes, she could tell me what those lot codes mean—where the reference blocks went, and why Garroway kept them cold for twenty-eight years. Alone on this rock with zero cell bars, those columns are a locked door without a key.

I close the black leather binder gently, slide it back inside the dry oilskin liner of my duffel, and pull my damp gloves and wool coat back on over the dry sweater. My watch reads `7:28 PM`. Every muscle in my back protests as I cinch the duffel strap across my chest and crawl toward the loft's north gable louver, keeping my knees off the squeaking joists.

Through the rusted iron slats, the sleet-swept courtyard lies dark and empty between Ward C and the pier. Fifty paces north, an orange glow flickers behind the shuttered first-floor windows of the **Administration & Customs House**—warm light pulsing like a stoked furnace.

Thick black smoke—carrying the sharp stench of burning bond paper and sweet pipe smoke—pours out of the Customs House brick chimney into the night wind. Whatever Victor Garroway and Margot Kiefer-Hall are feeding into that stove, they aren't waiting for the `00:30` ebb to finish the purge.
