## Chapter 24

Gray Friday dawn breaks over Blackthorne Ridge in a flat wash of freezing sleet and wet pine smoke.

By six-thirty, two county pumper trucks have knocked down the worst of the carriage barn fire, leaving the rear kitchen wing a blackened skeleton of steaming rafters against the snow. Red and blue cruiser strobes pulse steadily across the turnaround, turning the churned ice into bruised purple slush. The sharp smell of wet charcoal and cold diesel clings to every wool coat in the clearing.

I sit on the open tailgate of an Essex County ambulance beside my sabotaged Honda Civic—which a state tow rig hauled up to the turnout twenty minutes ago, its clipped distributor wire dangling loose beneath the popped hood—wrapped in an olive wool blanket while a paramedic cleans the split across my right index finger. My bruised left shoulder aches under the wool, yet every time I glance toward the adjacent stretcher, **Maya** is sitting upright in a clean navy fleece, sipping hot black coffee from a paper cup with her ribs freshly taped. She hasn't let the paper cup out of her left hand since the medics wrapped her chest, as if holding onto something warm proves she's no longer hiding in a freezing attic wall.

Down by the wrought-iron gates, two Vermont State Police troopers unfasten Hollis Greer from the iron spear, clap steel handcuffs over his uninjured right wrist, and load him onto a rolling backboard. His soot-smeared jaw stays locked shut, though his eyes never leave Maya until the ambulance doors swing closed.

Across the plowed apron, **Celeste Vane** sits in the back seat of a state cruiser with her pale wrists cuffed in front of her cashmere turtleneck. A state fire marshal carries the black leather *VaneCor* medical case off the passenger seat of Sloane's SUV in a clear evidence bag, right behind a pair of forensic techs rolling **Arthur Vane's** salt-packed body bag out of the collapsed stone icehouse on a plastic sled.

Time of death pending toxicology, yet the state medical examiner's preliminary freeze-chart puts Arthur dead at least forty-eight hours—hours ahead of Friday's five-o'clock survivorship deadline.

A second cruiser pulls up behind the tow rig, and **Deputy Enoch Pike** steps out of the rear door in county custody, picked up down the logging grade without a coat. His heavy boots drag through the slush as a trooper reads him his rights beside the gate.

"Ms. Vance," a tall woman in a charcoal wool overcoat says, stepping up to the ambulance tailgate with an evidence clipboard. **Assistant U.S. Attorney Nora Halvorsen**, dispatched out of Burlington after Sloane's compliance call triggered a multi-agency response. "The troopers tell me your sister has the original financial ledger from the house. We'll clear your ribs at the hospital first, then take formal chain of custody once my evidence courier arrives."

Maya steps down from the stretcher beside me, keeping the oilskin-wrapped **Caretaker's Ledger** buttoned tight beneath her navy fleece. She looks at me once—silent, steady—waiting for my nod before climbing into the warm ambulance cab.

***

Two hours later, **St. Johnsbury Hospital** smells of antiseptic bleach, damp wool coats, and scorched vending-machine coffee.

At **8:30 AM**, the ER triage bay is quiet behind thin blue privacy curtains. Three neat black nylon sutures close the split across my right index finger, and a clean gauze square covers the scrape along my temple. The thin paper sheet beneath my legs crinkles every time I shift, and my wool socks are drying stiffly beside the radiator grille. Maya sits on the edge of the rolling chair beside my gurney, holding a thick, warm **photocopy packet** of the **Caretaker's Ledger** that Halvorsen's paralegal just ran off at the hospital records desk while we wait for the federal courier to seal the original calfskin book buttoned inside her fleece.

The privacy curtain rings rattle sharply on their aluminum track.

**Sloane Mercer-Hall** steps into the narrow bay. Someone loaned her a dry tan trench coat over her soot-stained trousers, and a small ice pack rests briefly against the plum bruise along her left cheekbone before she drops it into her coat pocket. Even in a borrowed coat with ash smudged into her collar, she carries the quiet authority of a woman who bills by the quarter-hour. Outside in the bright linoleum corridor, a uniformed state trooper stands with his shoulder braced against the doorframe, waiting to escort her to a material-witness interview.

"You look remarkably intact for someone who climbed out of a burning coal chute," Sloane says, keeping her voice pitched low enough that it won't carry past the curtain.

"I had a good guide," I say, pulling the olive wool blanket tighter around my shoulders. "And your SUV still needs a new ignition relay."

Sloane's mouth curves fractionally, without humor. She glances once at Maya's taped ribs, then pulls a folded cream-bond **wire authorization slip** from her trench pocket and lays it on the steel tray table beside my elbow. The heavy bond paper smells faintly of dry ink and Boston espresso.

"Your **forty-five-thousand-dollar** field contract cleared compliance at seven-thirty," Sloane says smoothly. "You produced Arthur Vane's corpse prior to five o'clock, which preserves the thirty-eight-million-dollar bondholder clawback. My firm honors its paper, Clara. Every cent is already in transit to Portland."

"I didn't do it for State Street," I say.

"I know why you did it," Sloane replies. She steps half a pace closer to the gurney, lowering her voice until it barely rises above the hum of the triage heart monitor. "And that brings us to the second signature on page forty-four. Halvorsen hasn't unsealed the federal grand jury packet yet. If you and your sister sign a joint supplemental **consulting addendum** with Mercer-Hall Fiduciary right now—routing the original Caretaker's Ledger through our Boston compliance counsel for internal audit before the U.S. Attorney takes formal custody—I'll wire a **one-hundred-thousand-dollar** retainer bonus to your Portland account before noon."

Maya's ink-stained fingers tighten on the edge of the photocopy packet in her lap. She doesn't look up at Sloane.

I look at the crisp hundred-thousand-dollar figure in my head. Three years ago, after Harbor Mutual buried me in civil court, that kind of money would have wiped out every debt I carried and bought me a clean office on Commercial Street. I wouldn't have spent two winters living out of a silver Honda Civic.

Yet Maya is sitting two feet away with taped ribs and ink-stained knuckles because she walked away from her life at twenty-one rather than sign off on dead children.

"You co-signed Escrow Account `#884-19B` in October 2020," I say flatly. "You paid Hollis Greer a hundred and forty-five thousand dollars to bury three dead children under fake hypothermia autopsies. If we hand that original ledger to your compliance counsel first, page forty-four will vanish into a shredder before lunch."

Sloane doesn't blink. "If that ledger hits a public docket unredacted, Vane Biotech stock craters by morning, federal regulators freeze Mercer-Hall's institutional bond pool, and forty pension funds lose their shirts. Take the hundred thousand, Clara. Your sister gets a clean name, and nobody has to spend three years in federal depositions."

***

I reach past the wire slip on the steel tray table and push the blue privacy curtain wide open.

**AUSA Nora Halvorsen** stands just outside the bay in the fluorescent corridor beside a federal evidence courier holding a brushed-steel transit case and a clipboard stacked with triplicate chain-of-custody receipts.

"Ms. Halvorsen," I call out, my voice carrying clean down the hallway.

Sloane's jaw locks white beneath the plum bruise on her cheekbone, though she doesn't turn around or step back. She keeps her hands folded calmly inside the pockets of her borrowed trench coat while the state trooper beside the doorframe shifts his weight onto his heels.

Maya stands up from the rolling chair, ignoring the sharp wince across her taped ribs, and walks straight past Sloane into the corridor. She unbuttons her navy fleece, draws the oilskin-wrapped **Caretaker's Ledger** clear, and places the original calfskin book directly into Halvorsen's gloved hands. Beside it, I drop two sealed hospital biohazard bags onto the courier's clipboard—the wrapped **crystal brandy stopper** and the **half-charred *nitroglycerin* rescue-spray canister** from my coat pocket.

"Original paper, unbroken chain of custody," I tell Halvorsen from the gurney. "Page forty-four has the October 2020 escrow authorization. My sister's whistleblower immunity agreement goes on the record before she gives her formal statement."

"Agreed," Halvorsen says. She unwraps a corner of the oilskin just enough to verify the embossed calfskin spine, then slides the ledger into the foam-lined steel transit case. Both twin brass clasps snap shut with a flat metallic click, and her black ballpoint scratches briskly across the yellow receipt copy. "Your sister is under federal protection as of eight o'clock this morning, Ms. Vance. And we'll keep your forty-five-thousand-dollar Mercer-Hall contract invoice on file as a verified investigative expense—nobody's clawing back your earned fee."

She tears off the yellow carbon receipt, stamps the federal courier's evidence log, and lays my copy beside the wire slip on the steel tray table.

Sloane adjusts the collar of her borrowed trench coat, smooths her silver-streaked hair back from her forehead, and steps calmly into the corridor beside the waiting state trooper. She doesn't look angry. She looks like an attorney who has already calculated her next three bail motions and knows which Boston judge sits on the weekend rotation.

Before climbing into the back of the state cruiser idling outside the glass ER doors, Sloane pauses briefly beside the edge of my pulled-back curtain. A cold gust of wet sleet and ambulance diesel slips in from the covered bay. Her gray eyes drop straight to the thick **photocopy packet** of the Caretaker's Ledger resting across Maya's lap.

"Enjoy your forty-five thousand, Clara," Sloane says quietly, her breath misting faintly in the draft off the automatic doors. "You think Arthur Vane was the top of that ledger? Look at the notarized seal on the back cover. Your father's maritime firm audited the first shipment."

She turns cleanly on her heel and walks out through the sliding glass doors toward the idling cruiser, leaving the sharp smell of expensive espresso and dry paper hanging in the bleach-heavy air.

Beside my gurney, Maya slowly flips the warm photocopy packet over to the final sheet, exposing the faint circular impression of an embossed notary seal pressed deep into the calfskin backing.
