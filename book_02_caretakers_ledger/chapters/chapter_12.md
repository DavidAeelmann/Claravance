# Chapter 12

Raw gasoline fumes thicken under the icehouse threshold, sharp enough to water my eyes and coat the back of my tongue in the pitch dark.

I click my penlight on, grip the cold aluminum barrel between my teeth, and lunge over the collapsed salt sacks to the rear granite wall. The rusted iron grate of the coal chute sits flush against the dark brickwork, its lower hinge pin crusted thick with orange scale and damp lime. Above the latch, a bent iron cotter pin holds the bar fast.

I flip out the heavy needle-nose pliers on my steel multi-tool, yank my right glove off with my teeth so I can feel the steel, and clamp the jaws onto the rusted cotter pin.

My left wrist screams as I twist. The iron pin groans, flakes of red rust showering onto my wet coat sleeves, and refuses to turn. Outside in the yard, a porch door slams shut in the distance—Simone is already heading back from the lodge kitchen with matches or a lighter, and the smell of gasoline under the sill grows stronger by the second.

I switch grips, bracing my right boot against the damp stone beside the chute, and haul back on the pliers with both hands. Salt grit crunches under my heel.

The cotter pin snaps clean at the neck.

I jam the flat screwdriver blade behind the iron latch bar and lever it upward until the frozen scale cracks. The rusted grate shrieks on dry hinges, swinging inward toward me just wide enough to clear my shoulders. A wave of stale, warm coal dust and furnace oil drifts up from the dark brick throat—the exact opposite of the freezing granite vault behind me. Behind my back, Arthur's blue poly tarp rustles faintly in the sudden draft.

Outside, fast boots crunch across the crusted snow toward the icehouse door, followed by the metallic click of a brass Zippo or kitchen matchbox sliding open.

I pocket the multi-tool, click my penlight off so no stray beam leaks through the threshold crack, and haul myself waist-high onto the rough brick lip of the chute. My hipbone scrapes hard against the granite lintel as I feed my coat hem and shoulders headfirst into the narrow, soot-choked shaft. Thick winter wool binds against the rough mortar on both sides, forcing me to exhale all the air from my chest just to inch forward. I reach back with one bare, freezing hand and pull the heavy iron grate shut behind my heels.

The grate settles against the brick frame with a dull clack.

A second later, a match flares yellow-orange through the crack beneath the timber door behind me. A hollow *whoosh* shakes the plank wall as gasoline catches across the dry cedar shingles, sending the sharp stink of burning pitch and scorched paint racing up toward the rafters. Heat radiates against the soles of my boots.

I kick my snow-caked boots free of the rusted iron grate and let gravity slide me headfirst into the dark, soot-choked brick throat.

***

The chute angles steeply downward through the stone foundation, barely wide enough for my winter coat and elbows.

Dry coal soot coats the rough-mortared brick, puffing into my nostrils and stinging the back of my throat with every shallow breath. Near the top, frost clings to the mortar joints, scraping icy grit against my cheekbones; lower down, the brickwork turns slick with warm, greasy coal tar. I brace my palms against the side bricks and jam the rubber toes of my snow boots against the upper wall to keep from dropping blind into the dark. Lime grit and dried mortar crumbs rain down inside my collar. Behind me, thin gray smoke from the burning icehouse door begins to curl lazily down the draft, carrying the acrid bite of hot petrol and roasting cedar. I press my chin into my wool collar to filter the sting out of my lungs, blinking soot tears out of my eyes.

Farther below, the steady mechanical thrum of the sub-basement oil furnace vibrates right through the brickwork under my ribs, loud enough to drown out any footsteps on the cellar stairs.

I slide down one slow body-length at a time, feeling the chute narrow where the foundation stones meet the basement ceiling joists. My coat buttons catch on a protruding brick corner, tear free with a faint pop of thread, and clatter down into the dark ahead of me. Then my outstretched fingers hit solid, unyielding cast iron.

The chute ends dead against a sloped metal baffle—the heavy **ash-pit damper** sealing the coal shaft off from the boiler vault.

I shift onto my left hip in the tight brick tube, fish the penlight out of my pocket with numb fingers, and shield the lens with my palm before clicking it on. The dim red-filtered glow through my fingers shows a thick slab of soot-blackened cast iron wedged tight across the lower throat. No handle or latch sits on this side of the plate, and the lower seam is packed tight with gray furnace clinker and rust scale.

I click the light off so I don't drain the battery and lay my ear flat against the cool iron baffle.

Beyond the metal slab, the oil burner hums with a low, pressurized roar, accompanied by the rhythmic hiss of water circulating up the East Attic riser. No footsteps sound on the concrete floor—not yet—but the boiler room air feels open and hollow on the far side of the damper. I pull my heavy **brass skeleton key** from my pocket and tap the center of the iron plate once, lightly, listening to the pitch.

A dull, dead thud. Heavy iron, backed by solid weight.

I tap the upper left corner next. Same dead thud. Then I tap the upper right corner, right where the brick side-wall meets the iron frame.

The metal rings back with a sharp, hollow *clink*—a recessed void sitting right behind the corner lip.

Nina's schematic note flashes back through my head: *COAL CHUTE TO BOILER — NO LOCK ON FLAP.* It isn't locked from the outside. It's held by a gravity counterweight pin recessed in the brick side-slot, designed to trip whenever coal hits the lip.

I reach up with the tip of my multi-tool's flat screwdriver, feeling along the right mortar joint until the steel blade drops into a narrow vertical slot coated in hardened grease and coal dust. My bare right fingers numb fast against the cold metal as I trace the groove. Near the top of the notch sits a thumb-thick iron pin, crusted tight with fused ash and old furnace scale. I flip out the multi-tool's awl and scrape the grit away bit by bit, grit pattering softly onto the iron baffle beneath my chin, until the pin moves a fraction inside its housing.

Then I hook the flat screwdriver tip behind the pin, brace my left elbow against the brick wall, and pry it sideways.

***

The iron counterweight drops inside its sleeve with a heavy metallic *clang* that echoes loud enough to rattle my teeth.

The cast-iron baffle slides upward in its greased steel tracks below my chest, dumping a cascade of dry coal dust and clinkers onto the concrete floor of the boiler vault—and flooding my face with hot, diesel-scented air.

I freeze, gripping the brick lip with both hands and hooking my boot heels hard into the mortar behind me as the heavy iron plate locks high in its tracks. Dust motes swirl in a shaft of bare yellow bulb light from the far end of the cellar stairs.

Below me, a bright white flashlight beam sweeps sharply across the concrete floor near the furnace jacket. Wet Vibram boot soles squeak once against the damp cement as **Hollis Greer** pivots toward the ash-pit wall, the twin barrels of his shotgun gleaming dull blue in the glare. His heavy canvas chore coat smells faintly of wet wool and stove oil. The sweetish tang of wintergreen smokeless tobacco drifts upward, cutting straight through the furnace diesel.

He didn't come down here to fix the furnace. He's sweeping the basement while Simone torches the icehouse, making sure I didn't slip back in through the cellar bulkhead or hide behind the twin oil drums in the corner.

Behind my heels, black petrol smoke rolls thicker down the chute, spilling past my cheek in a thin gray ribbon toward the boiler room lights. A chunk of loose mortar breaks off beneath my left boot toe and clatters loud down the iron baffle onto the floor.

Hollis's flashlight beam snaps straight up toward the open coal-chute mouth, blinding me for half a second. Dust and black smoke shimmer inside the white cone of light.

"Don't move," Hollis barks, his thumb clicking the shotgun safety off as he steps around the cast-iron boiler casing. "Come out of there feet first!"

I can't crawl backward—thick black petrol smoke is already choking the upper chute behind my heels, and the cedar planks of the icehouse are crackling loud enough to hear down the brick throat. Going forward drops me straight onto the concrete floor in front of a twelve-gauge barrel.

Then, just outside the chute mouth behind the boiler casing, a dark cedar access panel recessed into the brick **pipe-chase junction** pops inward an inch, its brass catch silent in the furnace roar.

Two thin, gloved hands shoot out of the black gap, clamp hard onto the wool collar of my coat, and haul me sideways with desperate, wiry strength. Whoever's inside smells faintly of pine turpentine, damp wool, and cold sweat.

I don't fight the pull. I tuck my elbows in and roll sideways off the iron lip just as the narrow space between the brick flue and the hot copper risers swallows me whole. My shoulder slams against a warm copper pipe, knocking the breath out of my lungs, and my boots scrape clear of the chute lip by a fraction.

An ear-splitting **shotgun blast** detonates inside the boiler vault below.

Buckshot tears into the cast-iron ash baffle and shatters the brickwork at the mouth of the coal chute, spraying hot stone chips and acrid cordite smoke across my boots as the wooden pipe-chase panel slams shut in my face.

A cold, ink-stained hand clamps tight across my mouth in the pitch dark, pressing me flat against the warm copper pipe while Hollis's heavy boots charge across the concrete floor beneath us, kicking through spilled clinkers and ash.
