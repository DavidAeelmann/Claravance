# Chapter 14

Under the shielded cone of my penlight, the **Caretaker's Ledger** smells like damp calfskin, iron gall ink, and six years of bought silence.

Maya sits cross-legged beside me on the wool blankets, holding the open spine steady with her left palm while her right hand presses lightly against her taped ribs. Every time the boiler surges two floors below, the copper riser beside my shoulder ticks and hums, keeping the narrow brick crawlspace blisteringly warm.

"Start at twenty-twenty," Maya whispers, flipping back two leaves from Page 44. "The first three *VaneCor* pediatric trial deaths happened in Burlington and St. Johnsbury between August and October. All three children had undiagnosed arrhythmia markers in their baseline EKGs, and all three died within seventy-two hours of receiving the escalated dose."

I run my bare forefinger down the ruled ink columns, cross-referencing the dates against the two shredded *VaneCor* manifests still tucked inside my breast pocket. The batch codes—*VC-09-412* and *VC-09-418*—match line for line. Beside each patient's initials sits a neat, right-handed entry in Arthur Vane's fountain-pen scrawl: a rural town name, a county medical examiner's initials, and a round-number disbursement—`$145,000.00`, `$180,000.00`, `$210,000.00`—routed through **State Street Escrow Account #884-19B** in Boston.

Between 2021 and 2024, four more pediatric cases appear in the back half of the book—all reclassified as accidental drownings or winter exposure, every single one cleared by the same two rural examiners on Hollis's courier route.

At the bottom of every single page, Arthur Vane's signature sits on the left line, and **Sloane Mercer-Hall's** crisp black signature sits on the right.

"Sloane's firm underwrote the thirty-eight-million-dollar institutional bond that kept *Vane Biotech* solvent after the FDA delayed full approval," Maya says quietly, tracing a penciled margin note beside a 2023 transfer. "If those deaths had hit the clinical registry, the bondholders would have pulled their capital overnight and *Mercer-Hall Fiduciary* would have eaten the federal clawback. Six years ago, I pulled the raw EKG variance logs off the Burlington server before I understood who was covering it up. Three days later, someone torched my apartment while I was at the laundromat."

"And the survivorship clause," I murmur, pulling out the three-page covenant I lifted from Celeste's jewelry drawer. "Arthur's voting shares only vest to Celeste if he survives past five o'clock tomorrow afternoon. If he's certified dead before five, the thirty-eight million snaps back to Sloane's institutional bondholders."

"Which means Sloane doesn't want justice," Maya says, her dark eyes flat in the dim light. "She needs Arthur's corpse found before five o'clock tomorrow so she can hand her bondholders their thirty-eight million—and she needs this ledger burned before anyone connects her escrow account to the dead kids."

I close the calfskin cover gently and tie the hemp twine back around the oiled canvas. Every piece of the last thirty-six hours clicks into place with ugly symmetry: the $15,000 advance wired before I even crossed the state line, the rush to beat a Friday storm, the brass skeleton key Sloane dropped into my palm at Becky's Diner. My head throbs from coal soot, freezing drafts, and thirty hours without real sleep. Sloane didn't hire me because I was a good forensic auditor; she hired me because my Maine PI license was freshly restored, I was hungry enough to ignore a rushed NDA, and she figured I'd stumble over Arthur's body in the dark while she cleaned up the rest. And if Hollis killed me before Friday afternoon, nobody in Portland or Boston would look twice at a dead private investigator with an expired bond history.

***

By nine o'clock, the cramped heat inside the chimney crawlspace turns suffocating, thick with dry lime dust and wool sweat.

Maya unlatches the false cedar louver an inch, listens to the dead quiet of the third floor, and slides the panel wide. We crawl back out into **Room 4 — Attic East**, keeping clear of the bare floorboards near the center joist where Hollis's weight groaned above me yesterday dusk. The room still smells of pine turpentine and stale blood from Maya's gauze, though the radiator valve hisses softly against the cold gable wall. I hand Maya three dry saltines from the box she brought out, and we sit with our backs to the cold plaster while she chews slowly.

I stretch my stiff legs against the baseboard, then walk softly across to the third-floor corridor door and press my palm against the oak panel. Cold air leaks around the strike plate, carrying the faint, bitter stink of burned cedar from the yard below. The snapped-off steel key tip I pushed out of the cylinder this afternoon still sits in my coat pocket, leaving the upper stairwell deadbolted from our attic side.

I return to the frosted east gable window, where Maya is scraping a thumbnail-sized peephole through the interior rime ice with the edge of a tarnished copper penny.

"Wait," I say softly, crouching beside her in the dark. "Tuesday night—after Arthur collapsed in the study—how did Hollis stage the snowmobiles without leaving tracks back to the house?"

"He used the wind," Maya whispers, pressing her cheek near the cold glass. "The blizzard was dropping two inches an hour by midnight. I couldn't get down past the second-floor landing with my cracked ribs, so I watched the woodshed bay from this window between one and two in the morning."

She taps the frosted pane with one trembling, ink-stained knuckle.

"Hollis backed his Ford F-250 out of the carriage barn without headlights, dragged Arthur's body out the kitchen larder hatch on a blue poly tarp, and packed him in rock salt inside the icehouse. Then he and Celeste walked out to the three Arctic Cats with a brass hand-pump and two empty red jerrycans. They didn't start the engines once—Celeste held a shaded flashlight under her coat while Hollis opened the brass fuel petcocks and drained every drop of gas out of the tanks."

The final piece of the woodshed staging locks into place with cold, mechanical precision. That explains why the snowmobiles had clean spark plugs, zero melted ice troughs under the tailpipes, and raw gasoline pooled around the brass drain valves. They weren't idled dry in a frantic whiteout departure; Hollis and Celeste manually siphoned the tanks and turned the ignition keys to `ON` so any county sheriff poking around on Friday afternoon would log an abandoned walk-off.

"By five in the morning, the snow filled in every boot print between the woodshed and the porch," Maya finishes quietly. "Only the truck ruts under the iron gate stayed visible—because Hollis drove back up from the county turnaround after paying Pike his retainer."

Below us, the house feels deceptively still. No footsteps echo on the second-floor gallery, and the intercom speaker in the hallway stays dark. Hollis and Celeste are downstairs waiting out the clock—either patching up the burned icehouse door or tearing through my navy duffel in the study.

"We can't wait until morning," I tell Maya, checking the luminous dial on my watch: **9:32 PM**. Less than twenty hours left until Friday's five o'clock deadline. "If Hollis goes through my duffel, he'll find the signed Boston contract. He'll know Sloane sent me."

"He already knows you're not from probate," Maya says, glancing toward the locked third-floor fire door at the end of the hall. "The question is what he does when Sloane realizes you haven't called in."

Before I can answer, a low, rhythmic rumble vibrates up through the granite foundation—not the basement oil burner, and not the wind howling across the spruce ridge.

Twin chains clatter steadily against frozen gravel, climbing the steep logging road from the county turnaround.

***

Maya and I drop flat onto our stomachs beneath the east gable dormer, keeping our heads below the windowsill as heavy tire chains chew closer through the fresh snow. Frost bites through my wool trousers where my knees press against the cold pine floorboards.

Through the scraped peephole in the frost, two bright white LED beams slice upward through the black spruce trunks, sweeping across the wrought-iron gates and spilling pale blue glare across the Great Hall's high clerestory windows below us.

First comes **Enoch Pike's** dented county snowplow pickup, its amber roof strobe switched off so it doesn't announce itself across the valley.

Right behind Pike's bumper, idling smoothly on heavy studded tires, rolls a **black armored SUV** with Massachusetts plates. Its suspension settles stiffly onto the packed snow of the forecourt, and exhaust steams white in the subzero air.

"Sloane," I whisper, my stomach knotting tight as the SUV slides to a stop beside the porch steps.

The passenger door opens. A tall woman in a tailored camel hair coat steps out onto the packed snow, clutching a leather briefcase in one gloved hand while Pike climbs down from his plow cab with a heavy flashlight. Even from three stories up, I recognize the silver-streaked bob and the crisp, unhurried posture from State Street. She doesn't look around for my Civic, and she doesn't glance toward the woodshed. She walks straight for the porch like she already knows the layout by heart.

Down on the covered hemlock porch, the heavy oak front door swings inward, spilling yellow hearth light across the snow.

**Hollis Greer** steps onto the top plank with his shotgun cradled across his forearm, his wool chore coat smeared black with icehouse soot. Behind his shoulder, Celeste's pale silhouette appears briefly at the edge of the foyer light, her blonde knot gleaming pale against the dark oak paneling. Hollis doesn't raise the barrel at Pike, and he doesn't look surprised to see the Massachusetts plates. He just steps aside and holds the heavy oak door wide as **Sloane Mercer-Hall** climbs the porch steps and disappears straight inside the Great Hall.

Behind her, Pike kills the SUV's headlights, climbs back into his idling plow cab to stay out of the biting wind, and plunges the forecourt back into dark falling snow. Only the dim yellow glow from the Great Hall's high clerestory glass paints a pale rectangle across the hood of Sloane's truck.

Maya's hand tightens around the oilskin-wrapped **Caretaker's Ledger** against her chest. Below our feet, muffled voices rise faintly through the old floor registers as Sloane's heels click across the flagstone hearth—coming straight to collect Arthur's frozen body and burn the only book that can send her to federal prison.
