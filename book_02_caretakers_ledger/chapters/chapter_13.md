# Chapter 13

Below the floorboards of the pipe-chase junction, Hollis kicks through a pile of broken brick and furnace clinkers, coughing hard as black petrol smoke pours out of the shattered coal-chute mouth into the boiler vault.

"Goddamn it," Hollis snarls, his heavy boots crunching over spilled ash. "Simone! Get down here—she's in the chute!"

No answer reaches the basement over the roar of the oil burner. The hand clamped across my mouth stays rigid, ink-stained knuckles pressing my upper lip against my teeth. In the narrow cedar shaft behind the boiler casing, the warm copper riser radiates steady heat against my bruised shoulder blade. Whoever pulled me off that iron lip is pressed chest-to-back against me in the dark, breathing in shallow, uneven hitches that whistle faintly through clenched teeth. A sharp smell of pine turpentine and dried wool clings to her sweater sleeve.

Below us, Hollis's flashlight beam sweeps the concrete floor around the coal-chute opening. Wood scrapes as he drags an empty oil drum closer to peer up into the smoking brick throat, then curses again as thick black smoke drives him back. Heavy boots stomp across the concrete toward the cellar stairs, taking the wooden steps two at a time as he goes to check the burning icehouse outside.

The cellar door slams shut at the top of the flight, rattling the latch.

The hand lifts slowly off my mouth. Thin, cold fingers catch my coat sleeve, tug once—a sharp, urgent pull upward—and start climbing the steel plumbing straps bolted into the brick shaft.

I don't ask questions yet. My lungs ache from coal soot and cordite, and my bare right hand is shaking from the cold. I catch the lowest iron strap, haul myself up behind the narrow silhouette in the pitch dark, and climb rung by rung past the kitchen joists where the smell of salt brine leaks faintly through the larder wall. We pass the second-floor linen closet hatch, pausing for two heartbeats while muffled boots cross the gallery outside, then climb all the way up into the blistering warmth of the third-floor chimney stack.

A wooden latch clicks quietly overhead. My guide hauls herself into a narrow, blanket-lined recess behind **Room 4 — Attic East**, then reaches down with a sharp intake of breath to grip my wrist and pull me up beside her.

The false cedar louver slides shut behind my heels, sealing us inside a cramped, suffocatingly warm crawlspace wedged between the brick flue and the attic eaves. Stacked wool blankets muffle the radiator's metallic tick, and the air smells sharply of pine turpentine and old paper.

***

A small, red-filtered penlight clicks on, painting the rough pine rafters and stacked wool blankets in dim crimson.

I stare across the narrow, soot-dusted floorboards at **Nina**.

For three seconds neither of us breathes. My mind refuses to square the sister I spent six years hunting through county morgue logs and cold-case alerts with the woman huddled three feet away. She looks older than twenty-seven—hollow-cheeked, her dark hair chopped jaggedly at her collar and tucked behind one ear, her gray wool sweater damp with sweat and stained at the left cuff with dried **Noodler's Prussian Blue** ink. A strip of yellowed medical tape wraps tight around her ribs beneath her open flannel shirt. Every breath she draws stops short of a full inhale, and her scraped knuckles look raw from the brickwork.

"Nina," I manage, the name scraping out of a throat raw with coal dust.

I reach out before I even think, my fingers catching her cold wool sleeve just to prove she isn't a trick of carbon monoxide and exhaustion. She flinches sharply as my elbow brushes her ribs, biting down on a gasp, then grabs my forearm with both hands—holding on so tight her nails dig through my coat.

"Six years," I whisper, my eyes burning suddenly in the soot-dark air. "Not a postcard. Not a call on your birthday. I thought you were dead in a ditch off Route 7."

"I couldn't," Nina says, her voice dry and papery, worn thin from days of silence. She lets out a shaky breath that turns into a wet cough she smothers against her sleeve. "If I called Portland, they'd have watched your apartment next. Look at me, Tessa—I'm sorry."

Her eyes drop to my left wrist where the torn coat cuff rides back, exposing the thin pink Salt Point scar. Her thumb brushes the raised line gently. "What happened in Maine?"

"A ferry dock," I say, squeezing her freezing, ink-stained hand. Her bones feel fragile beneath the wool, like a bird's wing. "I cleared the judgment. I came up here the second Margot showed me the ledger scan."

Nina eases herself back against the warm brick chimney, wincing as her ribs press against the mortar. Beside her rolled wool blanket sits a dented aluminum thermos, a half-empty box of saltines, a fountain pen with its cap off, and a heavy rectangular bundle wrapped tight in oiled brown canvas. She unscrews the thermos cap and nudges it toward my knees; lukewarm tap water sloshes inside.

I take two slow swallows, washing the coal grit off my tongue, then hand the steel cup back.

"Wait," I say softly, pulling the folded Guest Ledger page and the glossy Cragmont photograph from my breast pocket. "How did you know Margot would send *me*?"

"I was auditing Arthur's private trust as *Lena Talbot*," Nina whispers, keeping one ear cocked toward the floorboards of Room 4 outside our louver. "My cover gave me access to *Kiefer-Hall Fiduciary*'s estate-trust archive portal. Two weeks ago, an internal escrow alert popped across my screen when you turned down Graham Kiefer's fifty-thousand-dollar bribe at Salt Point."

"She flagged me," I breathe.

"Right below the alert sat Margot's partner memo flagging you as her top target recruit for December's Vermont audit," Nina says. "The second Arthur collapsed Tuesday night, I knew Margot would dispatch you before Friday's vesting deadline—if I gave her the one piece of bait you couldn't walk past."

"And the margin note," I say, tapping *Don't trust him.* "Why not write my name? Why not tell me where to look?"

"Because if Hollis saw *Tessa* on the lobby Guest Ledger, he'd know *Lena Talbot* was tied to Boston's auditor," Nina says sharply, her eyes bright in the red light. "He would have burned this house down with me in the walls before you ever left Portland. *Don't trust him* looked like a dead-end archivist warning about Arthur—something Margot would tape to your briefing photo without a second thought, while only you would know my cramped, back-slanted cursive."

"You rigged the boiler damper too," I say, nodding toward the scalding copper riser humming beside my knee. "Eighteen gallons of fuel oil in forty-eight hours."

"If I froze to death in the rafters, nobody was ever going to read what's inside this oilskin," Nina says, pulling her knees tighter to her chest as the chill from my wet coat radiates through the tight space. "Hollis cracked two of my ribs Tuesday night when he caught me in the study hallway—I barely made it into the dumbwaiter before he fired."

"Wait—you were inside the study when Arthur died?" I ask, keeping my voice low enough that it barely carries past the copper pipe.

"I was behind the walnut desk photographing the ledger when he walked in at eleven-thirty-eight," Nina whispers, her fingers tightening on her knees. "He had a massive cardiac spasm on the rug and begged Simone for his nitroglycerin spray. She walked over to the apothecary cabinet, pumped a tampered canister twice under his tongue, and washed it down with brandy rimmed with crushed *CutlerCor* powder. She watched the mantel clock until he stopped breathing at eleven-forty—and then she screamed for Hollis."

"The same Hollis who torched your apartment in Burlington?"

"I saw his right hand on the steering wheel of his pickup outside the fire six years ago," Nina says grimly. "Missing the top joint of his ring finger. I wrote *Don't trust him* in the corner of my Tuesday check-in page, pushed a fax over the kitchen phone jack before dawn—right before Hollis ripped the line out—and flipped the ledger sheet back under the blotter so he wouldn't see it. When you arrived at dusk yesterday while he was out padlocking the barn, I slipped down the dumbwaiter, sliced my page out, and glued the Room 4 brass tag over the tear so you'd find the Icehouse map I left in the radiator."

***

Downstairs, two stories below our feet, a heavy timber door slams hard against the Great Hall frame—Hollis coming back inside from the burning icehouse, shouting something muffled at Simone over the crackle of the intercom.

Nina doesn't flinch. She tucks my folded Guest Ledger sheet and the charred voucher corner safely inside the Caretaker's Ledger, then locks her gaze on the **three-page Survivorship Covenant**, the wrapped crystal brandy stopper, and the half-charred silver rescue-spray canister on the blanket between us.

"I just saw Arthur's body under the rock salt in the icehouse," I tell her, keeping my voice low enough that it barely carries past the copper pipe. "Thin socks, swollen gout toe, blue fingernails. And Simone is holed up in the West Wing right now, burning the remaining *CutlerCor* blister packs over a porcelain bowl—right before she torched the icehouse gable thinking I was still inside."

Nina's jaw sets hard, her thumb brushing the edge of the charred canister. "She has until five o'clock tomorrow afternoon. If Arthur isn't certified dead before five, the thirty-eight-million-dollar voting block vests to her instead of the institutional bondholders."

"That's why Margot hired me," I say, tapping the $45,000 contract figure on my mental ledger. "She told me it was a routine ten-day probate inventory. She lied about the timeline, she lied about the road being clear—and Hollis just pulled my navy duffel out of my guest room."

Nina reaches down beside her thermos, unties the hemp twine around the **oiled canvas bundle**, and peels the stiff waterproof cloth back.

Inside sits a thick, calfskin-bound **Caretaker's Ledger**—twin to the maroon Guest Ledger downstairs, its brass corner guards green with age and its spine reinforced with black linen tape. The heavy rag-paper pages feel cold and stiff with damp.

"She didn't just lie about the timeline, Tessa," Nina whispers, her voice dropping so low I have to lean my ear close to her shoulder.

She flips past sixty pages of handwritten farm deliveries, timber leases, and caretaker wages until she reaches a crisp, ink-ruled spread near the back—**Page 44**, dated **October 14, 2020**, two months after Nina vanished from Burlington. I click my white penlight on, shielding the beam with my cupped palm so no light leaks through the cedar louver slats.

My beam catches a fresh blue sticky-note tab Nina left on the margin, right beside six neat columns of wire transfers.

> *CASE #VT-20-114 (PEDIATRIC CARDIAC / HYPOTHERMIA RECLASSIFICATION) — $145,000.00*

> *DISBURSED VIA: STATE STREET ESCROW ACCT #884-19B (BOSTON)*

> *COURIER / CLEARANCE: H. GREER*

I reach into my breast pocket and lay the **charred Cutler Biotech voucher corner**—the one I pulled out of the Great Hall hearth yesterday evening—flat against the ruled margin. The `$145,000.00` payout figure matches to the cent.

My eyes drop past the dollar figures to the bottom authorization block, where two ink signatures certify the off-book disbursement.

Arthur Cutler's jagged, heavy fountain-pen scrawl sits on the left line.

On the right line, written in crisp, slanted black ink above the printed title *Fiduciary Managing Partner* and dated *10/14/2020*, sits the unmistakable signature of **Margot Kiefer-Hall**.

"She wasn't trying to expose Arthur," Nina breathes against the hot brick wall. "Margot co-signed the hush-money account six years ago. She sent you up here to find Arthur's corpse for her bondholders—and burn this book before anyone finds her name on page forty-four."
