# Chapter 13

Below the floorboards of the pipe-chase junction, Hollis kicks through a pile of broken brick and furnace clinkers, coughing hard as black petrol smoke pours out of the shattered coal-chute mouth into the boiler vault.

"Goddamn it," Hollis snarls, his heavy boots crunching over spilled ash. "Celeste! Get down here—she's in the chute!"

No answer reaches the basement over the roar of the oil burner. The hand clamped across my mouth stays rigid, ink-stained knuckles pressing my upper lip against my teeth. In the narrow cedar shaft behind the boiler casing, the warm copper riser radiates steady heat against my bruised shoulder blade. Whoever pulled me off that iron lip is pressed chest-to-back against me in the dark, breathing in shallow, uneven hitches that whistle faintly through clenched teeth. A sharp smell of pine turpentine and dried wool clings to her sweater sleeve.

Below us, Hollis's flashlight beam sweeps the concrete floor around the coal-chute opening. Wood scrapes as he drags an empty oil drum closer to peer up into the smoking brick throat, then curses again as thick black smoke drives him back. Heavy boots stomp across the concrete toward the cellar stairs, taking the wooden steps two at a time as he goes to check the burning icehouse outside.

The cellar door slams shut at the top of the flight, rattling the latch.

The hand lifts slowly off my mouth. Thin, cold fingers catch my coat sleeve, tug once—a sharp, urgent pull upward—and start climbing the steel plumbing straps bolted into the brick shaft.

I don't ask questions yet. My lungs ache from coal soot and cordite, and my bare right hand is shaking from the cold. I catch the lowest iron strap, haul myself up behind the narrow silhouette in the pitch dark, and climb rung by rung past the kitchen joists where the smell of salt brine leaks faintly through the larder wall. We pass the second-floor linen closet hatch, pausing for two heartbeats while muffled boots cross the gallery outside, then climb all the way up into the blistering warmth of the third-floor chimney stack.

A wooden latch clicks quietly overhead. My guide hauls herself into a narrow, blanket-lined recess behind **Room 4 — Attic East**, then reaches down with a sharp intake of breath to grip my wrist and pull me up beside her.

The false cedar louver slides shut behind my heels, sealing us inside a cramped, suffocatingly warm crawlspace wedged between the brick flue and the attic eaves. Stacked wool blankets muffle the radiator's metallic tick, and the air smells sharply of pine turpentine and old paper.

***

A small, red-filtered penlight clicks on, painting the rough pine rafters and stacked wool blankets in dim crimson.

I stare across the narrow, soot-dusted floorboards at **Maya**.

She looks older than twenty-seven—hollow-cheeked, her dark hair chopped jaggedly at her collar and tucked behind one ear, her gray wool sweater damp with sweat and stained at the left cuff with dried **Noodler's Prussian Blue** ink. A strip of yellowed medical tape wraps tight around her ribs beneath her open flannel shirt. Every breath she draws stops short of a full inhale, and her scraped knuckles look raw from the brickwork.

For three seconds neither of us moves. The last time I saw my sister alive, she was twenty-one years old in a Burlington coffee shop, sliding a spare apartment key across a sticky Formica table before vanishing off the grid. Now she's sitting three feet away in a dark attic crawlspace, smelling of pine turpentine, cold sweat, and chimney dust.

"Your left wrist," Maya whispers, her eyes dropping to the pink healing scar peeking out beneath my torn coat cuff. Her voice sounds dry and papery, worn thin from days of silence. "What happened in Maine?"

"A ferry dock," I say, my throat raw with coal dust as I reach out and catch her cold, ink-stained hand in mine. Her bones feel fragile beneath the wool sleeve, like a bird's wing. "I'm okay. I cleared the judgment, Maya. I came up here the second Sloane showed me the ledger scan."

Maya exhales a short, bitter sound through her nose and eases herself back against the warm brick chimney, wincing as her taped ribs press against the mortar. The collar of her open flannel shirt shifts, exposing the pale, crescent-shaped scar above her right collarbone from the summer we both fell off the dock pilings in Casco Bay. Beside her rolled wool blanket sits a dented aluminum thermos, a half-empty box of saltines, a fountain pen with its cap off, and a heavy rectangular bundle wrapped tight in oiled brown canvas. She unscrews the thermos cap and nudges it toward my knees; lukewarm tap water sloshes inside.

I take two slow swallows, washing the coal grit off my tongue, then hand the steel cup back.

"I didn't think she'd send you this fast," Maya says quietly, keeping one ear cocked toward the floorboards of Room 4 outside our louver. "I sent that cropped guest-ledger scan to *Mercer-Hall Fiduciary*'s overnight fax line Tuesday night after Arthur collapsed. I knew Sloane wouldn't recognize the slant of my `m`—or why I wrote *Don't trust him.* on the margin—but I knew her compliance desk would flag the Blackthorne Lodge header and pull your Maine file."

"Why *Don't trust him.*?" I ask, pulling the folded Guest Ledger page and the glossy Blackthorne photograph from my breast pocket. "You meant Hollis?"

"I meant Hollis, and I meant anyone who answers the door up here," Maya says. "When I heard your Civic crunch up the logging road Wednesday dusk, Hollis was already in the Great Hall burning the duplicate clinical manifests with lamp kerosene. He sliced the top sheet out of the Guest Ledger with his skinning knife because my alias—*Mara Abbott*—was still wet on the bottom line. Then your headlights hit the front gate."

She reaches out with her left hand and taps the **brass `ROOM 4 — ATTIC EAST` tag** resting in my palm.

"Hollis ducked into the study for his shotgun," Maya whispers. "I had ninety seconds. I dropped down the pantry dumbwaiter, grabbed the hide glue from the butler's shelf, and pressed that brass tag straight onto the tacky spine so you'd know where to look if Hollis locked you in. Then I climbed back up the shaft before he threw the third-floor deadbolt."

"You rigged the boiler damper too," I say, nodding toward the scalding copper riser humming beside my knee. "Eighteen gallons of fuel oil in forty-eight hours, all pushed straight up into Room 4."

"I had to," Maya says, pulling her knees tighter to her chest as the chill from my wet coat radiates through the tight space. "If I froze to death in the rafters, nobody was ever going to read what's inside this oilskin. Hollis broke two of my ribs Tuesday night when he caught me in the study hallway—I barely made it into the dumbwaiter before he fired."

***

Downstairs, two stories below our feet, a heavy timber door slams hard against the Great Hall frame—Hollis coming back inside from the burning icehouse, shouting something muffled at Celeste over the crackle of the intercom.

Maya doesn't flinch. Her gaze stays locked on the folded **three-page Survivorship Covenant** I've laid beside the Guest Ledger sheet on the blanket between us, along with the wrapped crystal brandy stopper and the half-charred silver rescue-spray canister from Celeste's vanity.

"Arthur died at eleven-forty Tuesday night," I tell her, keeping my voice low enough that it barely carries past the copper pipe. "I just saw his body under the rock salt in the icehouse. Thin socks, swollen gout toe, blue fingernails. Celeste swapped his cardiac rescue spray with *VaneCor* capsules—she's alive in the West Wing right now, burning the blister packs over a porcelain bowl. And she just torched the icehouse door thinking I was still inside."

Maya's jaw sets hard, her thumb brushing the edge of the charred canister. "She has until five o'clock tomorrow afternoon. If Arthur isn't certified dead before five, the thirty-eight-million-dollar voting block vests to her instead of the institutional bondholders."

"That's why Sloane hired me," I say, tapping the $45,000 contract figure on my mental ledger. "She told me it was a routine ten-day probate inventory. She lied about the timeline, she lied about the road being clear—and Hollis just pulled my navy duffel out of my guest room."

Maya reaches down beside her thermos, unties the hemp twine around the **oiled canvas bundle**, and peels the stiff waterproof cloth back.

Inside sits a thick, calfskin-bound **Caretaker's Ledger**—twin to the maroon Guest Ledger downstairs, its brass corner guards green with age and its spine reinforced with black linen tape. The heavy rag-paper pages smell of cedar chest and old ink.

"She didn't just lie about the timeline, Clara," Maya whispers, her voice dropping so low I have to lean my ear close to her shoulder.

She flips past sixty pages of handwritten farm deliveries, timber leases, and caretaker wages until she reaches a crisp, ink-ruled spread near the back—**Page 44**, dated **October 14, 2020**, two months after Maya vanished from Burlington. I click my white penlight on, shielding the beam with my cupped palm so no light leaks through the cedar louver slats.

My beam catches a fresh *Noodler's Prussian Blue* sticky-note tab Maya left on the margin, right beside six neat columns of wire transfers.

> *CASE #VT-20-114 (PEDIATRIC CARDIAC / HYPOTHERMIA RECLASSIFICATION) — $145,000.00*
> *DISBURSED VIA: STATE STREET ESCROW ACCT #884-19B (BOSTON)*
> *COURIER / CLEARANCE: H. GREER*

I reach into my breast pocket and lay the **charred *Vane Biotech* voucher corner**—the one I pulled out of the study ash-trap yesterday evening—flat against the ruled margin. The `$145,000.00` payout figure matches to the cent.

My eyes drop past the dollar figures to the bottom authorization block, where two ink signatures certify the off-book disbursement.

Arthur Vane's jagged, heavy fountain-pen scrawl sits on the left line.

On the right line, written in crisp, slanted black ink above the printed title *Fiduciary Managing Partner* and dated *10/14/2020*, sits the unmistakable signature of **Sloane Mercer-Hall**.

"She wasn't trying to expose Arthur," Maya breathes against the hot brick wall. "Sloane co-signed the hush-money account six years ago. She sent you up here to find Arthur's corpse for her bondholders—and burn this book before anyone finds her name on page forty-four."
