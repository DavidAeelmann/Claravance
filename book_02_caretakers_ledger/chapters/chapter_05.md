# Chapter 5

Hollis marches me up the main oak staircase at a brisk clip, the battery lantern swinging shadows across the dark portraits on the second-floor landing. He opens the second door on the west side of the gallery—a cold guest room with twin four-poster beds and shuttered windows—and jerks his chin toward the cedar closet.

"Drop your bag there," he says, his free hand resting on the shotgun grip. "I'm going down to bleed the fuel line on the basement generator so the pipes don't burst overnight. You're going to walk down to your car at the iron gates, grab whatever inventory binders and battery packs you brought from Boston, and bring them back inside before nine-fifteen."

"My Civic is parked outside the gate," I say, setting my navy canvas duffel carefully on the closet floor. I push the zipper tight over Sloane's leather NDA portfolio and pull the closet door halfway shut. "The drive isn't plowed. Walking down and back in snowdrifts takes twenty minutes."

"Then start walking," Hollis replies. "I'll be watching the drive from the front porch once I kick the boiler back on. Stay on the truck ruts and don't wander toward the barn."

He waits until I step back into the gallery corridor, then follows me downstairs to the commercial kitchen. He drapes his heavy waxed parka over a brass peg beside the back door—taking only the shotgun and his wool vest into the cellar stairs—and disappears down the narrow stairwell behind the pantry, his heavy boots thudding against the wooden treads.

I don't waste a second.

The moment the cellar door latches behind him, I step to the waxed parka hanging on the peg and slide two fingers into the right flap pocket. Cold iron keys jingle softly against wintergreen tobacco tins. My fingertips close around the familiar heavy bow of Sloane's brass skeleton key.

I lift it clean out of his pocket, drop it back into my own left coat pocket, and step out the rear kitchen porch door into the freezing dark.

Cold wind cuts straight through my sweater sleeves. Behind me, deep beneath the kitchen floorboards, the diesel generator coughs twice, catches with a low mechanical roar, and settles into a steady thrum. Two weak amber coach lamps flicker alive on the front porch corner, throwing long, uneven spruce shadows across the upper turnaround.

Hollis will be up from the cellar and out on that front porch in two minutes.

I don't take the main drive. Instead, I step off the kitchen porch into the deep snowbank along the timber eaves, crouching low until the dark bulk of the lodge shields me from the front porch windows.

Fifty paces back in the black spruce stands the timber carriage barn.

***

I move fast along the shadowed treeline, my snow boots sinking halfway to the shins in dry powder where the wind scoured the drift. Pine needles catch in my collar, sharp and resinous against my neck.

Up close, the carriage barn looms wide and silent, its weathered cedar siding dark against the snowpack. Two heavy double doors face the main drive, secured from the outside by a thick steel hasp and a heavy brass padlock—exactly as I saw when I walked up from the gates at dusk.

I don't touch the front padlock. If Hollis walks onto the front porch and shines his halogen beam down the drive, he'll see fresh boot prints right under the barn doors.

I circle around the blind north gable where the spruce boughs hang lowest against the timber wall. Halfway down the side, a narrow wooden service door sits recessed under a sagging snow overhang, its iron thumb-latch crusted with frost.

I pull my steel multi-tool, snap out the thin awl blade, and slide it into the narrow gap between the door jamb and the frame. Not a deadbolt—just a rusted spring-bolt latch frozen stiff in its keeper. I lean my shoulder against the cedar planks to take the tension off the bolt and lever the awl upward.

The frost cracks with a sharp snap. The door swings inward an inch.

I slip inside and pull the cedar slab shut behind me, plunging into pitch blackness that smells strongly of hot motor oil, wet rubber, and melted snow dripping onto concrete.

I click my penlight on, cupping three fingers tightly over the lens.

A matte-charcoal Ford F-250 crew cab pickup fills the center of the concrete bay. Heavy mud-terrain tires—waffle-tread, packed with fresh white snow in the deep sipes—match the inbound ruts under the iron gates. I lay one bare palm flat against the matte hood grille.

Warm. Not lukewarm—radiating dry, metallic heat straight through my skin. The truck hasn't been parked for two hours. Hollis didn't drive back up at four-thirty; he parked here less than ninety minutes ago.

I step quietly along the concrete floor beside the driver's door. Through the tinted glass, a half-empty plastic bottle of wintergreen spit sits in the center console beside a heavy pair of bolt cutters with fresh silver scrapes along the jaws. Beside the timber workbench against the west wall hangs a coiled rubber siphon hose and a brass hand-pump, filling the cold bay with the sharp tang of raw ninety-one octane gasoline.

The siphoned snowmobile fuel.

I move past the cab to the truck bed. A stiff canvas tarp lies bunched over a low cargo load, tied down with frayed nylon cord. I untuck one corner of the tarp without untying the knots and aim my narrow penlight beam underneath.

Two things sit side by side on the ridged bedliner.

First, a woman's fitted white-and-navy *Moncler* down coat, quilted and spotless, sits folded neatly over a pair of small fur-trimmed winter boots. I lean close for one second. Beneath the cold nylon hangs a faint, unmistakable trace of compounded lavender cuticle oil.

Arthur's second wife, Celeste Vane, didn't ride a snowmobile into a blizzard. Her winter coat sits dry in Hollis Greer's pickup.

Beside the coat sits a heavy plastic milk crate packed tight with cross-cut paper strips—thousands of confetti-sized ribbons spilling out of a busted commercial shredder bag. I pinch two wider strips near the crate rim where the blades jammed and left half a line of print intact.

`...VANECOR PEDIATRIC TRIAL — AUTOPSY TRANSFER...`

`...DEATH CODE: ACCIDENTAL HYPOTHERMIA (RURAL CORONER)...`

My stomach knots tight against my ribs. I drop the two paper strips into my inside breast pocket beside the charred *Vane Biotech* payout voucher, tuck the canvas tarp back down over the crate, and slip out the side service door before Hollis steps onto the front porch.

***

Ten minutes later, I reach the wrought-iron gates at the bottom of the drive, breathing hard in the subzero air.

Behind me up the ridge, the two amber coach lamps glow faintly on the lodge's front porch. Hollis's broad silhouette stands motionless beside the top steps, the red tip of his lantern resting against the railing as he watches the dark drive. He thinks I stayed on the truck ruts the whole way down.

I slip through the narrow pedestrian gap beside the fieldstone pillar into the dark spruce turnout where I left my 2014 silver Honda Civic.

The turnout sits quiet and dark under heavy boughs. No other tire tracks show in the snow around my bumper—Wait.

I stop two paces behind the rear quarter panel and crouch low, shielding my penlight against my palm.

Boot prints cut straight across the turnout from the iron gate pillars to my driver's-side fender. Large, deep Vibram lug-sole prints, size eleven. Not mine. My treads are narrower, and I walked straight from the driver's door to the gate gap at dusk.

I step forward and sweep the narrow beam down onto my front driver's-side tire.

Cold metal glints loosely in the powder. My heavy steel tire chains lie pooled in a tangled heap around the hubcap, severed cleanly in three places with the crisp, pinched V-cut of heavy bolt cutters. The same silver-scraped jaws sitting on Hollis's console back in the carriage barn.

I rise fast and pop the interior hood release through the unlocked driver's door. When I lift the Civic's hood, the fresh snow crust along the cowl seam cracks cleanly—someone already broke that ice seal within the last hour. A fresh smear of pine resin and gun oil stains the yellow metal prop rod.

The coolant reservoir sits ice-cold to the touch. Above it, the plastic battery terminal cover hangs unclipped. I shine my penlight straight down onto the ignition harness beside the intake manifold.

The main distributor lead—plus the primary starter relay wire—has been snipped cleanly right at the plastic plug, leaving bare copper strands glinting against the black rubber loom.

No spare fuse fixes a cut harness in subzero dark without crimps and wire strippers. My car isn't going down this mountain tonight, and neither am I.

I check the glovebox first. My folded Maine PI bond letter and the $0 Cumberland County satisfaction-of-judgment notice still sit tucked behind the registration sleeve, untouched. Two weeks ago, clearing that judgment felt like the finish line. Now it feels like a ticket into a different locked room. Hollis wasn't looking for my papers—he just wanted my wheels dead before I figured out what's in the woodshed.

I move quietly to the Civic's trunk, pop the latch with the key, and pull out three things I keep packed for Maine winter fieldwork: a spare lithium battery pack for my penlight, a pair of lightweight aluminum snowshoes strapped inside a canvas cover, and my compact encrypted field tablet inside a magnetic weatherproof case. If Hollis tosses my navy duffel upstairs tonight, I don't want him finding a backup drive with my Salt Point notes or photos of the *VaneCor* strips.

I slide the magnetic tablet case high up behind the rear driver's-side wheel well liner where road slush won't hit it, then shoulder the small canvas daypack with the snowshoes and battery brick—making sure the aluminum frames stay hidden inside the bag so Hollis only sees an ordinary binder pack from the porch. I close the trunk gently without slamming the latch, step back through the narrow gap between the fieldstone gate pillars, and start up the truck ruts.

Up on the ridge, a heavy halogen flashlight beam flares bright from the lodge's front porch, sweeping slowly down the snow-packed drive toward the iron gates.
