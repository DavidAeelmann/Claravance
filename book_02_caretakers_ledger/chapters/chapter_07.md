# Chapter 7

"Who's in there?" I whisper, keeping my lips an inch from the cold iron gap while my boot heel presses the soot-swept floor.

A ragged cough catches behind the brick baffle. "Don't—wait—"

Above my head, heavy floorboards groan violently across the commercial kitchen. Not a mouse. Size-eleven Sorel boots crossing from the pantry hallway straight toward the cellar plank door.

I shove the iron ash-pit flap flush with my palm until the counterweight latch drops silently home. "Get back up the shaft," I hiss against the rust. "Boots on the stairs."

Scrape of wool against dry brick. A faint, retreating scuffle inside the chute—and then the cellar door at the top of the wooden stairs swings wide.

I don't hide behind the oil tanks. Only one stair leads up out of this vault, and if Hollis Greer walks down here with a flashlight and catches me crouching behind the boiler, he'll shoot first and tell the county sheriff he mistook me for the attic squatter.

Instead, I step straight into the harsh yellow glare of the caged work bulb, gripping the brass shutoff wheel on Zone 2's cold copper pipe as if I've been fighting a stuck valve for five minutes.

Hollis clatters halfway down the wooden treads, his double-barreled shotgun leveled at my chest, his wool vest unbuttoned over a gray thermal shirt. His eyes snap from my boots to the boiler manifold, then down toward the dark chimney base behind me.

"What the hell are you doing down here at midnight?" he demands, stepping onto the concrete floor.

"Freezing," I snap back, letting genuine anger sharpen my voice. "My guest-room radiator is stone-cold, and the pipes in the wall kept hammering like someone was hitting them with a wrench. You left the cellar unlocked after bleeding the generator, so I came down to crack the Zone 2 valve."

A lie, though the locked mortise bolt wouldn't hold against a good lawyer if he checked his key ring—and right now, Sloane's skeleton key sits deep in my left pocket. Hollis's gaze narrows on the brass wheel under my palm.

"That valve's seized," he says, stepping past the oil tanks toward the furnace jacket. My ribs lock tight as he stops three paces from the brick coal chute, squinting into the shadow behind the boiler.

For one terrible second, I expect him to notice the swept arc in the gray soot or the faint smear of blue ink on the rusty thumb-latch.

Instead, the diesel generator behind him kicks into a higher governor cycle, rattling a loose tin of pipe fittings on the workbench. Hollis glances toward the generator, mutters under his breath, and turns back to me with the shotgun barrels angled at the floor.

"Leave the boiler alone," he orders, gesturing toward the stairs with his chin. "Second floor stays shut down till morning to save fuel. Get upstairs, and if I hear the kitchen door open again before six, I'm locking you in the larder till your Boston boss answers her phone."

I don't argue. I climb the wooden treads ahead of his heavy boots, hear him throw the cellar deadbolt behind me, and march straight back up the service stairs to the west guest room. Once inside, I wedge a fresh gray thread into the upper doorjamb gap before pulling my coat tight around my shoulders.

***

At six-thirty Thursday morning, pale gray dawn leaks around the edges of the bolted timber shutters, cold enough to turn my breath white above the wool blanket.

I didn't sleep an hour. My left wrist aches dully beneath my cuff, and every muscle across my shoulders feels knotted tight from listening to the generator hum through the floorboards. My reset gray thread tamper-trap stayed intact across the doorjamb all night—nobody entered my room while I lay awake—and my navy duffel still sits undisturbed on the closet floor.

At six-forty, heavy knuckles rap twice against the oak panel.

"Kitchen coffee's on," Hollis calls through the wood. "You wanted to start your probate inventory before noon. Master suite's open."

Ten minutes later, I follow him down the gallery corridor carrying a yellow legal pad and a ballpoint pen from my duffel. The double mahogany doors of the master suite stand unlocked now, swinging inward into a sprawling corner bedroom that smells faintly of stale cedar, cold linen, and something floral—sweet and sharp against the chill.

Compounded lavender cuticle oil.

My skin prickles. It's the exact same scent I smelled on the quilted *Moncler* down coat folded inside the bed of Hollis's Ford F-250 last night.

Hollis leans one shoulder against the outer doorjamb, cradling his shotgun across his crooked arm while keeping his eyes fixed on my hands. "Catalog the jewelry drawer, the closet, and the desk," he says flatly. "Don't touch the wall safe behind the headboard—that's for the Boston lawyers."

"Fine by me," I reply, clicking my ballpoint pen as I step onto the pale wool rug.

The master bedroom looks staged for a magazine shoot—or scrubbed clean in a hurry. Arthur Vane's king-sized bed sits made with military-tight hospital corners. Against the east wall stands Celeste Vane's mirrored dressing table, its glass top wiped nearly bare except for a silver hairbrush, a tortoiseshell comb, and a small amber dropper bottle labeled *Compounded Lavender Oil — Rx 4412*.

I log the dressing table on my yellow pad, unscrew the dropper cap for half a second to confirm the scent, and screw it back down while Hollis watches from the door. Next, I step into the walk-in cedar closet. Arthur's tweed jackets and flannel shirts hang evenly spaced on wooden hangers, while Celeste's winter coats leave a clean, empty gap where the white-and-navy *Moncler* down jacket should hang. On the bathroom marble counter beyond the open arch sits an empty cardboard carton for a sublingual *nitroglycerin* rescue spray, its silver blister sleeve missing.

Beside the shuttered bay window sits a small walnut sitting table holding a heavy cut-crystal brandy carafe and a single unwashed tumbler. Someone carried them up from the study—or Arthur and Celeste shared a nightcap right here Tuesday night before everything went sideways.

I step over to the sitting table, keeping my back angled slightly toward Hollis so he only sees my shoulder moving as I jot down *1 crystal decanter, 1 tumbler*.

With my left hand, I lift the heavy crystal stopper an inch out of the carafe neck.

Two things hit me at once. First, a sticky, translucent oil smudge coats the frosted glass grip of the stopper—smelling unmistakably of Celeste's compounded lavender cuticle oil.

Second, when I tilt the unwashed crystal tumbler slightly toward the gray window light, a thick, chalky white ring coats the dry amber residue at the bottom of the glass. Tiny, pulverized flecks of gelatin capsule shell cling to the crust—not dissolved sugar, and not ice melt. Someone crushed a cardiac rescue capsule or sedative pill directly into the bottom of that glass before pouring the brandy.

I set the stopper back down gently without clinking the crystal, my pulse hammering against my ribs.

Arthur Vane suffered from severe gout and heart trouble. His wife wears compounded lavender oil, her spotless winter coat is hidden in Hollis's pickup truck, and someone wearing that exact oil handled this brandy stopper right before a crushed capsule ended up in an unwashed glass.

***

"Keep moving," Hollis mutters from the doorway, shifting his weight against the jamb. "I need to check the front gate plow line before nine."

I nod without looking up, crossing to the tall rosewood chest of drawers flanking Celeste's dressing table. The top three drawers hold folded cashmere sweaters—every sleeve neat, zero suitcases packed, contradicting the idea that she threw clothes in a bag for a midnight ride.

I pull on the small brass drop-handles of the bottom jewelry drawer. Locked.

"This drawer's bolted," I call out, tapping the rosewood lip with my pen. "You got the key on your ring?"

Hollis pushes off the doorframe with an irritable sigh, fishing a small brass key ring from his thermal vest pocket. Just as he takes two steps into the room, the black handheld VHF radio clipped to his hip squawks sharply through static.

*"Hollis—you copy? County plow's down at the lower turnout. Gate's drifted over."*

Hollis freezes, his jaw clenching. He unclips the radio with his scarred right hand, holding the talk button down with his index finger while his eyes stay locked on me. "Copy, Enoch. Stay at the turnaround. I'm coming down in ten."

He tosses the small brass jewelry key onto the bedcover without stepping closer. "Log the watch rolls and leave the folder on the table. Don't leave this floor till I'm back from the gate."

His heavy Sorel boots thunder down the main oak staircase. Twenty seconds later, the rear kitchen porch door slams shut, followed by the muffled crunch of snow under his truck path toward the carriage barn.

I snatch the brass key off the bedcover and jam it into the rosewood drawer lock.

The tiny bolt snaps open. Inside sits a dark green velvet tray lined with empty watch slots—someone already cleared the high-value gold pieces—and a single folded legal printout tucked flat beneath the removable velvet liner.

I lift the corner of the tray and pull the crisp white sheets into the gray window light.

It isn't a jewelry receipt. It's a three-page **Survivorship & Voting-Share Covenant** stamped with *Mercer-Hall Fiduciary* letterhead and signed by both Arthur Vane and Sloane Mercer-Hall. Halfway down page two, a highlighted clause jumps off the paper in sharp black type:

> *In the event Arthur Vane is not legally certified deceased prior to **5:00 PM EST on Friday, December 5**, all Class-A voting shares ($38,000,000 par value) shall vest irrevocably in **Celeste Vane** as surviving spouse.*

My fingers go cold around the paper. I fold the covenant sheets flat into my breast pocket, then cross to the sitting table, wrap the lavender-smudged crystal brandy stopper inside a clean linen handkerchief from my pocket, and drop it into my coat. I leave the empty carafe neck uncovered—Hollis won't notice one missing stopper before he gets back from the gate.

Today is Thursday. Tomorrow at five o'clock, thirty-eight million dollars changes hands—and the woman who wears lavender cuticle oil isn't missing in a Vermont snowbank. She's hiding somewhere on this ridge, waiting out the clock.
