# Chapter 10

I don't try the third-floor stairs. Whoever just snapped a shotgun safety off in the Great Hall is already climbing the main oak flight below, and meeting twelve-gauge buckshot on a narrow pine landing leaves zero room to duck.

I drop to my knees beside the hot radiator in Room 4, twist the brass turnbuckles back, and pull the cedar louver panel wide. Behind me, heavy boots hit the second-floor gallery planks—pausing briefly outside the Master Suite where I left the jewelry drawer unlocked, then turning east toward the narrow attic stairs. Whoever just walked into the lodge knows the upper house layout by heart, and he's sweeping the third floor first.

I squeeze headfirst into the narrow brick pipe chase, turn my shoulders sideways to clear the scalding copper riser, and pull the cedar louver flush behind my heels until the wooden lip catches. Pitch darkness swallows me instantly. The brick flue radiates dry, suffocating heat against my coat sleeves, thick with the smell of old soot, damp wool, and pine turpentine. Below my boots, steel plumbing straps bolted into the mortar drop straight down into the dark shaft like ladder rungs, spaced just wide enough to catch the welt of a winter boot.

I hook one toe onto the top strap, grip the cool iron cold-water pipe beside the hot riser so I don't blister my palms, and lower myself rung by rung into the guts of the house. Condensation beads along the cold iron pipe, wetting my raw knuckles and stinging the healing pink scar across my left wrist. Every breath tastes like chimney dust and hot lime. Halfway down, my coat hem snags on a jagged mortar lip; I yank it free without clicking my penlight and keep climbing down past the second-floor ceiling joists. A copper branch line splits off toward the west gallery through a notched timber beam, humming faintly with circulating hot water from the boiler vault below. Dust rains softly onto my wool collar with each shift of my weight.

A narrow square access hatch—barely wide enough for a plumber's shoulders—breaks the dark plaster wall on my left, secured by a rusted slide bolt.

Above me in the attic, a heavy shoulder slams twice against the locked third-floor fire door. Wood splinters faintly around the strike plate, followed by the rattle of a brass knob violently twisted against a thrown deadbolt. Whoever climbed the stairs—Enoch Pike, or Hollis—just realized the deadbolt is thrown from the inside and won't budge.

I slide the rusted hatch bolt back with my thumb, grit scraping loud in the tight shaft, then push the wooden panel outward an inch and peer into a dim, shuttered linen closet smelling of cedar shavings and dry laundry soap. Empty. I crawl out of the shaft, brush lime dust off my knees, pull the access hatch shut behind my boots, and ease the linen closet door open into the far west end of the second-floor gallery.

***

Down at the east end of the corridor, a muffled curse echoes through the heavy oak stairwell door as a boot kicks the bottom rail. He thinks whoever stole the attic key is still trapped up under the rafters.

I don't head back toward my west guest room—not when Pike or Hollis will sweep the main bedrooms next. Instead, I slip quietly deeper into the shuttered **West Wing**, past three closed guest rooms whose brass handles sit cold and dust-coated. My boots make zero sound on the faded wool runner rug.

Near the end of the corridor, the air changes.

A thin ribbon of dry warmth leaks beneath the final door, carrying a sharp, acrid chemical stink—scorched aluminum blister foil and melting plastic—layered cleanly underneath a familiar sweet floral note.

*Compounded lavender cuticle oil.*

I stop outside the final double doors of the West Wing parlor suite. No overhead lamp burns inside, yet a faint orange flicker pulses behind the keyhole, accompanied by the soft hiss of a butane lighter clicking shut and the rustle of heavy paper.

I wrap my fingers around the brass lever, press it down until the latch clears the strike plate without a click, and push the door open half an inch.

Inside, the parlor suite's heavy timber shutters are bolted tight, turning the room into a dark box lit only by a small blue-and-amber flame burning inside a porcelain washbasin on the mahogany vanity. Beside the door, a small electric oil-filled radiator ticks softly, plugged into the generator circuit to keep this single hidden suite livable while the rest of the wing freezes. A stack of unread probate mail and two unopened bottled waters sit on the bedside table beside an unmade cot.

A woman stands over the basin in the flickering glow.

She's mid-thirties, slender, wearing a black cashmere turtleneck and dark wool trousers—matching the folded sweaters in Celeste Vane's bedroom closet, minus the spotless *Moncler* down coat sitting in the bed of Hollis's pickup. Her blonde hair is pinned up in a tight, messy knot, and her pale fingers glisten faintly in the firelight as she uses a pair of silver eyebrow tweezers to hold a foil blister strip of *VaneCor* cardiac capsules over the flame. A leather weekend valise sits half-packed on the stripped daybed behind her, stuffed with gold watch rolls from the Master Suite's empty velvet tray and two thick bundles of bearer bonds.

Black smoke curls off the melting plastic, dropping greasy ash into the porcelain bowl beside an empty silver *nitroglycerin* rescue-spray canister—its label half-charred, its metal nozzle still intact—a small amber apothecary bottle of compounded lavender oil sitting uncapped by the faucet, and an unwashed crystal brandy tumbler coated at the base with dried amber dregs and chalky white flecks of crushed gelatin capsule shell.

Celeste Vane isn't frozen in a Vermont snowbank. She isn't even hiding from Hollis. She's standing right here in the West Wing, methodically erasing the clinical evidence of how Arthur Vane died Tuesday night while waiting for five o'clock tomorrow.

I slide my right hand into my coat pocket, my fingers closing around the steel frame of my multi-tool, and push the parlor door wide enough to step inside.

The old floorboards beneath the edge of the threshold groan once.

Celeste spins around, dropping the silver tweezers with a sharp metallic clatter into the porcelain basin. She snatches a heavy brass candlestick off the vanity, raising it chest-high as her eyes lock onto mine in the dying blue light of the burning blister pack.

"Don't move," she whispers, her voice shaking with sharp, cornered fury rather than fear. "How did you get past the third-floor door?"

"Same way I found your *Moncler* coat in Hollis's truck bed," I say, keeping my weight balanced on the balls of my boots. "And the thirty-eight-million-dollar covenant under your jewelry tray. You've got until five o'clock tomorrow afternoon before those shares slip through your fingers, Celeste. Where's the rest of the *VaneCor* lot?"

***

Celeste's knuckles tighten around the brass candlestick. She doesn't step back toward the shuttered window; instead, her gaze flicks past my shoulder toward the dark hallway—waiting for the heavy boots at the east end of the gallery to finish breaking down the attic door.

"You're not a probate clerk," she says, her voice flat and tight as she recovers her composure. "Sloane sent you up here to void the survivorship clause. She's been trying to claw back those voting shares since October."

"Sloane sent me because someone faxed a guest ledger to Boston with my sister's handwriting on it," I counter, taking one slow step inside the room to block her path to the corridor. "Arthur died at eleven-forty Tuesday night. You swapped his cardiac spray, and you put crushed capsules in his brandy."

"Prove it," she snaps, raising the candlestick higher until the heavy pewter base gleams in the dying ember of the porcelain bowl. "Nobody's getting off this ridge before Saturday, and by Friday night—"

I lunge half a step sideways toward the vanity—not at Celeste, at the half-charred silver rescue-spray canister sitting on the porcelain rim beside the smoking tweezers. My left hand snatches the cool metal cylinder off the porcelain and seals it inside a zip-top plastic evidence baggie from my coat pocket alongside the wrapped crystal brandy stopper. Acrid blister-foil smoke stings my nostrils as I pivot away from the basin.

Celeste swings the brass candlestick hard at my shoulder. I duck under the arc, the heavy metal whistling past my ear and clipping the mahogany mirror frame with a sharp crack of splintered veneer. Before she can bring it back around, I catch her wrist with my right hand and pin her forearm against the edge of the vanity. Her skin feels ice-cold beneath the cashmere cuff, slick with lavender oil, and her pulse hammers fast against my thumb.

"Arthur couldn't walk fifty feet without his walnut cane," I tell her, holding her wrist off the porcelain bowl. "He never touched a snowmobile Tuesday night. You and Hollis siphoned the tanks and dragged him out to the icehouse."

Her jaw tightens, a muscle jumping beneath her cheekbone, and she tries to drive her free elbow toward my ribs. I shift my hip into her side to block the blow, the sharp smell of burned foil clinging to her cashmere sleeve.

Then a sharp, metallic squawk cuts the room in half.

Bolted into the plaster wall beside the vanity hangs a brass-plated house intercom panel, its small amber LED glowing steady in the dark. Static hisses through the speaker grille, followed by the unmistakable, gravelly crackle of Hollis Greer's voice transmitting from downstairs in the study—he just got back inside from the lower gate.

*"Celeste—pick up,"* Hollis barks through the speaker, breathing hard like he just ran up the kitchen steps. *"Pike met me at the gate and we checked the turnout. I found fresh snowshoe tracks leading back from the county road turnaround and a hidden tablet rig behind her Civic's wheel well, Celeste. Check her room—she's not from the fiduciary office."*

The speaker clicks dead.

In the sudden silence, Celeste wrenches her wrist free from my grip, her pale face breaking into a tight, vicious smile. She doesn't look toward the door anymore. She reaches sideways with her left hand and slams her palm straight down onto the red **CALL / ALARM** button on the brass intercom box.

A piercing buzzer erupts through the corridor outside—loud enough to rattle the glass in the vanity mirror—just as heavy boots thunder back down the third-floor stairs toward the second-floor gallery.
