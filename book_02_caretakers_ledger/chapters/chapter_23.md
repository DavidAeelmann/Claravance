## Chapter 23

Ten days after the fire on Blackthorne Ridge, December rain hits Portland Harbor sideways off the Casco Bay chop.

I walk the granite seawall along Commercial Street with a paper bag of groceries tucked under my left arm and a cardboard carrier of black coffee balanced in my right hand. Diesel exhaust drifts across the wet cobblestones from the morning island ferry idling at the pier. Overhead, two herring gulls wheel into the salt wind, screaming over the clatter of lobster traps stacked beside the bait shed.

Three days ago, the **forty-five-thousand-dollar** wire from Mercer-Hall Fiduciary cleared into my Portland checking account without a single compliance hold. Yesterday, an urgent-care nurse snipped the three black nylon sutures out of my right index finger, leaving a tight pink seam across the knuckle that still pulls whenever I curl my hand around a steering wheel. My silver Honda Civic sits parked two blocks up the hill with a new distributor harness, fresh winter tires, and zero snow chains in the trunk.

Two nights ago, **AUSA Nora Halvorsen** faxed the signed federal **protective witness immunity order** to my lawyer's office on Middle Street, formally clearing **Maya Vance** to return to every bank ledger and lease agreement in New England without fear of *Vane Biotech* retaliation.

I turn up the steep brick slope toward the Old Eastern Promenade, climbing past three-story clapboard triple-deckers with peeling white trim and salt-frosted storm windows. My bruised left shoulder aches under the damp wool of my coat, yet the heavy lead weight that lived behind my ribs for six years—the constant low math of wondering whether my sister was alive under a highway overpass or buried in unmarked pine dirt—is finally gone.

Inside my third-floor apartment, the cast-iron radiator by the front window hisses and clanks against the cold draft.

The narrow hallway feels warm and lived-in, carrying the faint toastiness of rye bread from the toaster beside a fresh roll of athletic tape on the bathroom sink. Maya's laced leather boots—scrubbed clean of Vermont mud and soot—stand neatly aligned beside my sneakers on the rubber boot tray. On the brass key hook beside the door hangs a spare set of house keys she hasn't held since college.

I set the paper grocery bag on the butcher-block counter, peel off my wet coat, and check the half-ajar door of the spare bedroom.

For six years, that room held nothing except cardboard bankers' boxes from my father's estate, a folding aluminum cot, and a radiator that rattled through every January gale. Now a real pine bed sits under the east window with crisp flannel sheets, and Maya sleeps curled beneath a thick gray wool quilt with her dark hair spread across a clean cotton pillowcase. Her breathing comes slow and even, without the sharp catch that followed every rib fracture in the Blackthorne attic.

I pull the bedroom door closer to keep the kitchen light off her face, nudge my damp scarf onto the radiator valve to dry, and carry two steaming paper cups of black coffee over to the scarred pine table by the bay window.

***

On the pine tabletop lies the thick **photocopy packet** of the **Caretaker's Ledger**, weighted down by my brass desk lamp and a jeweler's ten-power folding loupe.

Since Halvorsen's evidence courier sealed the original calfskin book inside that brushed-steel transit case in St. Johnsbury, I haven't read past page forty-four again. The Vermont grand jury already has Arthur Vane's salt-packed autopsy, Hollis Greer's forged hypothermia certificates, Celeste Vane's *nitroglycerin* tampering, and Sloane Mercer-Hall's co-signed October 2020 escrow authorization. Federal marshals denied bail for Hollis and Celeste at Monday's arraignment in Burlington. Sloane posted a half-million-dollar surety bond through a Boston law partner and vanished behind three appellate motions before noon.

Yet Sloane's parting line at the St. Johnsbury ER doors has sat in the back of my skull like a pebble inside a boot.

*You think Arthur Vane was the top of that ledger? Look at the notarized seal on the back cover. Your father's maritime firm audited the first shipment.*

I sit down on the wooden chair, click the brass desk lamp on, and angle the shade low so the warm halogen beam rakes almost flat across the final sheet of the photocopy packet.

Halvorsen's paralegal ran the copier on high contrast at the hospital records desk, which washed out half the background grain—yet the heavy circular impression pressed into the original calfskin back cover still catches the raking light in faint gray arcs.

I unfold the steel loupe, brace my elbows on the pine grain, and lean close over the page.

At first glance, the arc looks like a standard Massachusetts corporate notary ring—serrated outer border, sans-serif block capitals, a two-digit year code notched above the center line. Under ten-power glass, the outer ring resolves letter by letter where the original steel embosser bit deep into the leather:

`QUARANTINE STATION NO. 4 — BARROW ISLAND, R.I.`

My grip tightens around the cold brass rim of the loupe.

Barrow Island isn't in Vermont. It sits three miles off the Rhode Island coast in lower Narragansett Bay—a windswept granite reef holding a defunct turn-of-the-century maritime quarantine hospital that the state decommissioned in the late eighties and sold off to a private shell trust.

I reach across the table to the bottom shelf of my bookcase, pull out a battered blue **Harbor Mutual** civil discovery binder from three years ago, and flip past the burnt warehouse photographs from Portland's Commercial Street wharf. Halfway through the cargo manifests, tabbed in faded yellow plastic, sits the original **salvage bill of lading** that got me blackballed by every insurance underwriter in Maine.

Three years ago, when the Commercial Street warehouse burned to the waterline, Harbor Mutual's forensic adjuster swore the manifest held nothing except surplus textile looms and damp wool batting. When I refused to sign the payout clearance because the pallet weights didn't match the harbor master's crane logs—crated refrigeration compressors billed as dry cotton yarn, offloaded at low tide without a customs seal—the firm buried me under a fraudulent-interference suit. They stripped my license, froze my escrow bond, and wiped out every dime I owned before the charred pilings cooled.

I lay the yellowed Harbor Mutual manifest flat beside the ledger photocopy and shift the loupe from one sheet to the other. The paper still carries the faint, sour tang of wet wharf ash.

There, stamped in faint violet ink along the lower-left margin of the Portland warehouse manifest, is the exact same octagonal port-clearance code: `B.I.Q.S.-04`.

Barrow Island Quarantine Station.

I swing the desk lamp closer, squinting through the glass at the faded inner ring of the notary seal on the Caretaker's Ledger cover. Beneath the Rhode Island station name, crookedly over-stamped in tiny serif capitals, sit three letters and a registration number that stop the breath in my chest:

`V.M.C. — AUDIT 1998.`

**Vance Maritime & Cargo.**

My father's old marine surveying firm—the same three-man office on Custom House Wharf that went under when he died of a coronary in my second year of college. I still keep his brass parallel ruler and cracked leather logbook in the hall closet. He spent twenty years auditing cold-storage cargo manifests up and down the Atlantic coast, and his signature sat on the opening shipment before Arthur Vane ever bought a single pine acre on Blackthorne Ridge.

***

Wooden floorboards creak behind me in the narrow hallway.

I set the loupe down beside the Harbor Mutual binder without looking up right away, waiting for the cold knot between my shoulder blades to loosen.

**Maya** steps into the kitchen wearing one of my oversized flannel shirts over gray sweatpants, her left arm tucked lightly across the white athletic tape wrapping her ribs. Dark circles still shadow the skin beneath her eyes, yet the hunted set around her jaw has softened since St. Johnsbury. She carries a small, dented **steel lockbox** under her good arm—its corners scraped raw down to bright metal, its hinged lid held shut by a **rusted brass padlock** thick with white sea-salt corrosion.

"The coffee's still hot," I say, nudging the second paper cup across the pine table toward her.

Maya doesn't sit down right away. She looks from the opened Harbor Mutual binder to the raking desk lamp angled across the ledger photocopy, then straight at my face.

"You found the Barrow Island stamp," she says quietly.

"My father audited the first shipment in ninety-eight," I say, tapping the faint `V.M.C.` ring with the tip of my scarred index finger. "Same station code that sat on the Harbor Mutual warehouse manifest before the wharf burned in Portland. Sloane wasn't bluffing at the hospital."

Maya sets the dented steel box onto the pine table between our coffee cups. The rusted brass padlock hits the wood with a dry metallic thud, leaving a faint scatter of gray salt grit across the grain. Smells of wet iron, mildew, and old basement concrete rise off the dented lid.

"When Hollis torched my Burlington apartment six years ago," Maya says, pulling out the opposite wooden chair and easing herself down carefully to favor her ribs, "I didn't grab just the *VaneCor* trial binders. I had a rented storage locker out on Shelburne Road that Arthur didn't know about. Troopers cleared the seal on it yesterday morning."

She reaches into her flannel shirt pocket, pulls out a tiny **tarnished brass key** tied to a frayed length of red baker's twine, and lays it on top of the lockbox lid.

"I told you in the St. Johnsbury ER that I ran because of the pediatric fatalities," Maya says, sliding the heavy steel box toward my hands until its front edge bumps the Harbor Mutual binder. "That was half of it. Look at the notary ring again, Clara. I didn't disappear six years ago just because of Vane Biotech."

She keeps two fingers pressed against the rusted brass padlock, holding my eyes steady across the steam rising off the black coffee.

"I disappeared because the man who framed you in Portland was looking for what's inside this box."

Outside the bay window, the morning ferry horn blows a long, low blast across the gray harbor chop, shaking the frosted glass in its wooden sash. I wrap my scarred right hand around the cold brass padlock, feel the rough salt crust bite into my palm, and reach for the tarnished key beside my thumb.
