# Chapter 4

I don't reach for my multi-tool. Twin twelve-gauge muzzles sit an inch from frosted glass, and men holding shotguns in the dark don't wait for explanations.

I rise slowly from behind the butcher block with both empty palms raised beside my ears. The halogen beam blinds me for two heartbeats before dipping toward the maple island. I reach into my left coat pocket with two fingers, lift Margot's heavy brass skeleton key by the bow, and lay it flat on the butcher block.

"Boston fiduciary office," I say, keeping my voice flat and dry, the same clerk cadence I used to bore insurance adjusters in Portland. "Margot Kiefer-Hall sent me up to catalog the estate before the county probate clerk arrives. Check the leather portfolio in my duffel if you want."

The man outside doesn't lower the barrel. Behind the glass, his silhouette shifts sideways as a heavy key ring jingles against his hip. A bolt snaps back inside the rear kitchen door. Cold air rushes across my ankles as he kicks the timber slab inward and steps over the threshold, filling the doorway in a snow-crusted waxed parka and size-eleven Vibram lug boots.

Up close, he smells strongly of pine resin, lamp kerosene, and wintergreen smokeless tobacco. Deep weathered lines bracket his mouth beneath a salt-and-pepper beard trimmed short against his jaw. He's pushing fifty, broad through the shoulders, and his left thumb rests steady across the shotgun's top tang safety.

"I'm Hollis Greer," he says, his eyes flicking from my wet snow boots to the brass key on the island. "Winter caretaker. Who gave you a master key to the front mortise?"

"Margot Kiefer-Hall. State Street." I nod toward the Great Hall archway without dropping my hands. "My navy duffel is behind the study curtains. I hid it when the stairs locked overhead."

Hollis's jaw tightens. He spits a thin dark stream of tobacco juice into the stainless kitchen sink beside the door, then steps past me—keeping the shotgun muzzle angled toward my knees—and snatches the brass skeleton key off the butcher block with his gloved right hand.

"You shouldn't be in here," he mutters, shoving the key into his parka pocket. "Generator threw a fuel line an hour ago, and some local trespasser broke into the east gable earlier today. I locked the third-floor fire door from the landing to pen him up and snapped a broken key tip into the ward until I could fetch my twelve-gauge from the barn, then went down the back stairs and padlocked the front storm door so nobody else wandered in."

A smooth lie. Too smooth. If he locked the front storm door to keep people out, he did it *after* I was already standing at the lectern—and he didn't explain why his heavy boots were still creaking on the rafters before I even crossed the Great Hall.

"I'm not here to argue with your security," I say, letting a trace of tired irritation leak into my voice. "I'm a records clerk on a flat ten-day fee. If there's a trespasser in the attic, call the sheriff from your truck. Otherwise, point me to the guest wing and show me the three snowmobiles so I can log the property status and sleep."

Hollis studies my face in the pale snow-glare from the door. For a second, I think he's going to march me straight out to the porch at gunpoint. Instead, he clicks the shotgun safety on with a sharp metallic snick and rests the barrels across his crooked left forearm.

"Show me that Boston paperwork first," he says. "Then I'll show you the woodshed."

***

Ten minutes later, Hollis stands beside the Great Hall hearth while a single battery lantern he pulled from his coat illuminates the leather *Kiefer-Hall Fiduciary* portfolio in my lap. He doesn't touch the hearth ash. He doesn't glance at the sliced guest ledger on the lectern either, though the faint smell of lamp kerosene still hangs thick around the fireplace stones.

He reads Margot's signed estate-archiving authorization twice, his gloved thumb smudging the embossed State Street seal, then hands the folder back without a word.

"Ten days," he says, his voice dropping low. "You stay out of the third floor until I clear that attic lock in daylight, and you keep clear of the carriage barn. Snow's too deep by the woodshed anyway."

"My briefing says Arthur Cutler, his wife, and the caretaker left three snowmobiles idling by the woodshed Tuesday night," I say, zipping the portfolio back inside my navy duffel. I sling the bag over my shoulder—no chance I'm leaving Margot's contract out of my sight again—and follow him back toward the kitchen. "Wait. If you're the caretaker, why did the Boston trustees think you walked out with them?"

Hollis pauses at the kitchen threshold, his broad back blocking the porch light. "Because I took my pickup down to the county line Tuesday afternoon to buy shearling blankets before the whiteout hit. Bridge washed out below the ridge, so I couldn't get back up until an hour ago. Somebody else took those sleds—or tried to."

Another clean cover story. He even accounted for the fresh mud-terrain truck ruts I saw under the iron gates when I arrived at dusk.

"Then I still need serial numbers off the sleds for the fiduciary inventory," I tell him, stepping onto the back porch ahead of his lantern. "Five minutes in the cold, and I'm out of your hair."

Freezing air hits my face like dry needles, sharp and still beneath a heavy pewter sky. The snow has stopped falling, leaving the ridge buried under deep white drifts that swallow every sound past the porch eaves.

Hollis leads me twenty paces behind the kitchen wing toward a low timber woodshed crouched beneath snow-laden black spruce. Beside the woodshed's double doors, three sleek black-and-silver Arctic Cat snowmobiles sit lined up side by side in the drift, their windshields crusted with Tuesday's frozen sleet.

Hollis holds the battery lantern high, throwing stark shadows across the cowlings. "There they are. Keys still in the ignitions. Tanks bone-dry when I checked them at five."

I step carefully along the narrow packed footpath beside the lead sled, keeping my eyes on the snow beneath the rear bumpers rather than the handlebars.

The briefing packet claimed the trio left all three machines idling in the storm until the fuel ran out. An Arctic Cat two-stroke idling for two or three hours in subzero weather pumps scorching exhaust straight downward onto the snowpack. It carves deep, glazed ice troughs beneath every tailpipe and fouls the plugs with black oil soot.

I crouch beside the first snowmobile's rear flap, pretending to read the stamped chassis plate near the track tunnel.

Beneath the metal exhaust outlet, the Tuesday snowpack sits completely unbroken. Smooth, dry powder, crisp and powdery right up to the steel baffle. Not a millimeter of meltwater crust. Not a single speck of dark carbon soot on the white drift.

I rise and move to the middle sled, leaning across the seat as if checking the handlebar odometer. The key sits turned to `ON`, just as Hollis said, and the small digital hour-meter sits frozen on its last memory tick—unchanged from the laminated grease-stained maintenance sheet clipped inside the open woodshed bay door.

Then I drop my left hand near the lower cowling seam, right above the brass fuel petcock and reserve drain line.

Sharp, raw gasoline vapor stings my nostrils. Not burnt exhaust. Unburned ninety-one octane liquid, pooled wet around the brass drain valve where someone slipped a rubber siphon hose over the fitting and drained the tanks dry into the three red plastic jerrycans stacked beside the woodshed cordwood rack.

I straighten slowly, wiping a flake of snow off my sleeve so Hollis doesn't see my jaw clench. Nobody rode these sleds. Nobody left them idling in a blizzard either. Someone drained them by hand in the dark to sell a story to Boston.

***

"Got your numbers?" Hollis asks. His breath plumes thick in the lantern beam as he steps past the third sled toward the woodshed's heavy timber doors.

"Last two digits on the third chassis are iced over," I say, stalling while my eyes sweep the snowline past the woodshed corner.

Fifty paces behind us in the trees, the dark gable of the carriage barn looms silent behind its padlocked doors. Closer—barely twenty paces up the granite slope behind the kitchen wing—a squat, half-buried stone structure squats under a mounded white drift, its heavy timber door facing away from the porch light.

*ICEHOUSE — 1904.*

"Leave it till morning," Hollis snaps, shifting the shotgun under his left arm so he can reach the frozen brass padlock hanging on the woodshed hasp. "I'm locking the sled covers down before the wind picks back up."

He tugs at the frozen padlock shackle with his gloved right hand. The wet leather palm slips against the iced brass. Muttering under his breath, Hollis hooks the stiff cuff of his right glove between his teeth and yanks his bare hand free into the freezing air.

He grips the brass padlock body hard to twist the keyway clear of ice.

The lantern light catches the back of his bare right hand squarely against the dark timber.

His index finger wraps the shackle. His middle finger braces the brass collar. His pinky curls underneath. Between his middle finger and pinky, the right ring finger ends abruptly at the middle knuckle—a pale, puckered stump where the distal tip was sheared off years ago.

My throat goes dry. Three oily finger pads on the third-floor fire door deadbolt plate. Index, middle, and pinky, pressed in pine resin and gun oil.

Hollis Greer didn't lock the third-floor door from the inside to trap a trespasser. He was *up* there in the dark when I walked into the Great Hall, listening to me cross the flagstones, before he slipped down an exterior route and circled back to the kitchen porch with his shotgun.

The padlock clicks open. Hollis shoves the glove back over his scarred hand and turns toward me, the battery lantern swinging low across the packed snow between the woodshed and the stone icehouse.

"Inside," he says, gesturing with the shotgun barrel toward the kitchen steps. "I'll show you the guest room on the second floor. Keep your door shut till dawn."

I nod once, pivoting back toward the porch—and stop half a stride short.

Ten paces to my left, right at the edge of the narrow footpath leading up toward the half-buried stone icehouse, a single vivid drop of liquid sits frozen like dark glass against the stark white crust.

Dark blue. Archival fountain ink, untouched by Tuesday's snow.

Someone walked toward that icehouse *after* the storm stopped—bleeding ink from a cracked nib or a stained cuff—and Hollis Greer's lantern beam just swept straight past it without seeing a thing.
