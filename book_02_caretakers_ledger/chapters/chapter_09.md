# Chapter 9

The climb back up the spruce ridge takes two hours through drifting powder, every step heavier than the last. Twice I pause behind granite boulders to catch my breath while Pike's orange plow grinds slowly up the logging spur below, its amber roof strobe sweeping pale flashes across the treetops. Sweat cools fast against my ribs beneath my wool sweater, chilling me down to the bone whenever I stop moving.

By two-fifteen, I reach the timberline behind the lodge's carriage barn. Hollis's F-250 is gone from the turnaround, leaving fresh mud-terrain ruts curving down toward the iron gates to meet Pike. Nobody stands on the front porch. No smoke rises from the Great Hall chimney now.

I unbuckle my snowshoes beside the woodshed, stuff them back into the canvas daypack, and slip inside the rear kitchen porch without knocking.

The kitchen sits dead silent. Hollis's waxed parka is gone from the wall peg, and the swinging baize door leading into the Great Hall stands wide open—he didn't bother bolting it when he drove down to meet Pike. As I cross toward the Great Hall archway, I spot the wall telephone jack beside the pantry door. Its ivory faceplate hangs pried off the plaster by one screw, two copper wires ripped clean out of the terminal block with fresh brass pliers marks.

Whoever pushed that fax through before dawn Wednesday didn't get cut off by a storm limb. Someone inside the house tore the jack out of the wall after the transmission hit Boston.

I cross the flagstones quietly, climb the main oak staircase two treads at a time, and stop by the west guest room just long enough to ditch the bulky daypack in my closet. My navy duffel sits undisturbed on the cedar floor, and my gray thread tamper-trap still sits intact in the upper doorjamb. Nobody checked my room while I was on the ridge.

I turn east down the second-floor gallery toward the narrow pine stairs leading up to the third-floor fire door. My boots leave dark wet prints on the bare treads.

At the top landing, the heavy oak-and-iron fire door still smells of pine resin and gun oil.

I crouch beside the threshold, tear a stiff cardboard backing sheet off my yellow legal pad, and slide it flat through the narrow gap beneath the door until only two corners remain on my side. Then I pop the thin steel awl from my multi-tool and feed it gently into the oiled keyhole, angling the tip upward to catch the snapped-off steel blade wedged inside the warded cylinder.

Hard steel clicks against the broken key tip. I brace my left shoulder against the oak jamb and push steadily with the awl.

Once. Twice.

The jagged steel key tip slides backward out of the brass wards and drops with a sharp metallic clink onto the cardboard sheet below.

I pull the legal pad backing slowly back toward me under the door, pocket the broken key tip, and slide Margot's brass skeleton key into the cleared cylinder. The deadbolt throws with a heavy click. I step into the attic corridor, immediately turn the skeleton key from the inside, and throw the heavy wrought-iron slide bolt mounted on the attic side of the jamb so Hollis's master key can't open it from the stairwell while I search Room 4.

***

The third-floor attic corridor stretches narrow and dim beneath steep timber rafters, the air dry and stifling from a radiator valve hissing somewhere at the far east end.

Four paneled doors line the corridor, two on each side. I test the first three knobs quickly—unlocked, empty storage rooms stacked with sheet-draped wicker furniture, moth-eaten wool rugs, and rusted steamer trunks. Every cast-iron radiator along the west wall sits ice-cold.

At the far east end of the gable, a heavy oak door stands closed with a tarnished brass plate screwed above the knob: **4**.

I test the brass handle. Locked—though no key blocks the cylinder here. I slip Margot's skeleton key into the slot, turn the wards until the latch clicks, and push the door inward.

Warm air rushes out over my face, carrying the sharp, resinous bite of pine turpentine mixed with wool, stale sweat, and dried blood.

I step inside and close the door behind me without latching it, clicking my penlight on to sweep the low-sloped ceiling.

**Room 4 — Attic East** is smaller than the guest rooms downstairs, tucked directly under the steep east gable. A narrow iron cot stands stripped to its ticking mattress against the north wall. Beneath the frosted dormer window—its exterior timber shutters cracked just enough to let thin gray daylight bleed across the floorboards—sits an old oak draftsman's table.

From the dormer glass, anyone standing at this desk has a clear, unobstructed sightline straight down onto the carriage barn roof, the woodshed snowmobile bay, and the narrow footpath leading to the stone icehouse.

On the blotter lies a small glass bottle of *Noodler's Prussian Blue* waterproof archival ink, its cap screwed crooked, beside a black resin fountain pen with a fine steel nib stained with the exact ink Nina bought by the bottle in college.

My throat goes tight. She always tilted her notebooks forty degrees clockwise in school so her left wrist wouldn't drag through wet ink, and she favored fine steel nibs because ballpoints skipped on slick paper.

Beside the ink bottle sits a cracked ceramic mug holding cold black tea, a roll of surgical adhesive tape, and three crumpled squares of sterile gauze stiff with dried rust-brown blood. Under the blotter edge sticks a laminated Boston estate-archivist badge made out to **LENA TALBOT — CRAGMONT ARCHIVE**, its corner creased from being shoved into a boot or sleeve. The photo on the badge is blurry and low-contrast, stamped over a generic winter parka, yet the jawline beneath the knit cap stops my breath cold.

Someone living up here took a bad cut or rib injury within the last forty-eight hours—and used the lodge's heat to survive while Hollis thought he locked a trespasser out.

I pull the shallow center drawer of the draftsman's table open an inch. No ledger inside—only a battered brass compass, two sharpened drafting pencils, and a folded receipt from a Burlington outdoor outfitter dated ten days ago for thermal base layers and waterproof matches.

I turn toward the cast-iron radiator bolted along the south chimney wall.

Heat radiates off the painted iron fins in shimmering waves, blistering hot to the touch—fed straight up from that wide-open manual damper in the sub-basement boiler vault.

Directly behind the hot copper riser pipe, set flush into the dark cedar wainscoting, sits a tall vertical louver panel secured not by nails, but by two small brass turnbuckles. A faint crescent smudge of dark blue ink stains the lower cedar slat where someone's left palm pushed it shut in a hurry.

I twist the brass latches open and pull the cedar panel outward.

***

A narrow, pitch-black pipe chase opens behind the chimney brick, running vertically down past the second floor toward the kitchen pantry dumbwaiter—and horizontally beneath the low attic eaves.

I aim my penlight into the cramped crawlspace.

Thick wool blankets lie folded into a narrow sleeping berth along the warm brick flue, cushioned over old fiberglass batting. Beside the blankets sit an empty stainless thermos, a half-eaten sleeve of saltines, and a heavy brass flashlight wrapped in black electrical tape so only a pinhole beam escapes. Steel plumbing straps bolted along the brick chimney form a crude vertical ladder dropping straight down into the darkness toward the second-floor service chase, while a cold draft rises from somewhere lower down in the house.

No ledger. No person hiding in the berth right now. Whoever lives inside this wall slipped out before I climbed the stairs—maybe down through the dumbwaiter shaft, or out toward the stone icehouse where I heard them whisper last night.

If they bolted out of the coal chute at midnight when Hollis came down the cellar stairs, after pushing that ledger scan through the copper trunk before dawn yesterday, they're still moving inside the house's walls.

I swing the cedar louver shut and turn back toward the blistering-hot radiator beside the draftsman's table.

Something white catches my eye, wedged tightly down inside the narrow gap between the fourth and fifth iron radiator fins where the rising heat dried the paper crisp.

I pull my steel multi-tool, pinch the flat pliers onto the corner of the paper, and ease it gently up out of the hot iron slot without tearing the fibers.

It's a full sheet of heavy cream, red-ruled ledger paper. Along the left margin runs a jagged, straight-blade cut—matching the sliced binding of the maroon guest ledger down on the Great Hall lectern. I pull the tiny triangular scrap from my breast pocket and hold it against the missing bottom-right corner. The torn paper fibers and the tail of Nina's cursive *m* line up edge to edge without a hair's gap.

I smooth the warm paper flat on the draftsman's table beneath the gray window light.

Three guest names sit logged in neat faded ink at the top for Tuesday, December 2: *Arthur Cutler — Master*, *Simone Cutler — Master*, *Lena Talbot — Room 4*. Beneath them, filling the blank lower half of the page in cramped, back-slanted blue cursive, sits a hand-drawn schematic of the **Stone Icehouse** behind the kitchen wing.

Arrows point from the exterior icehouse floor down into a rough rectangle labeled: *A.C. — ROCK SALT / SAWDUST — DIED TUES 11:40 PM*. Beside it, a second arrow points down the back wall of the icehouse into a shaft marked: *COAL CHUTE TO BOILER — NO LOCK ON FLAP.* In the margin sits one final word, underlined twice in heavy ink: *LEDGER IN WALL.*

I stare at the two-word postscript until the ink blurs.

Arthur Cutler didn't vanish on a snowmobile Tuesday night. He died at eleven-forty Tuesday night—and his body is packed under rock salt and sawdust inside the stone icehouse twenty paces behind the kitchen.

I fold the sliced ledger page fast into my inside breast pocket—just as a heavy door slams violently downstairs in the kitchen wing, rattling the floorboards beneath my boots.

Wet boots stride fast across the Great Hall flagstones below, followed by the sharp metallic click of a shotgun safety snapping off and heavy steps turning toward the bottom steps of the main oak staircase.
