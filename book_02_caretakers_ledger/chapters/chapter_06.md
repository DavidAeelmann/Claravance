# Chapter 6

Hollis doesn't ask about my Civic when I step back onto the kitchen porch at nine-fifteen. He just sweeps his halogen beam once across the canvas daypack on my shoulder, grunts, and slides the heavy brass deadbolt across the kitchen door behind my boots.

"Generator's holding steady," he says, hanging his wet parka back on the wall peg without checking the flap pocket. "Stay in the west guest room till daylight. I'm locking the pantry hall from my side, and I sleep light."

I nod once, keep my face tired and blank, and climb the main oak stairs to the second floor.

Inside the cold west guest room, I shut the heavy paneled door and wait three seconds in the dark before pulling my penlight. First check: the cedar closet. My navy duffel sits undisturbed on the pine floorboards, the brass zipper pull still flush against the left seam where I aligned it. Nobody tossed my bag while I was down at the gate.

I slide my canvas daypack with the snowshoes and spare lithium brick onto the top closet shelf behind a stack of spare wool blankets. Next, I inspect the heavy interior timber shutters bolted across the two west windows. Solid oak panels, held tight by wrought-iron slide bolts painted shut with thick layers of old cream enamel. Even if I pried the bolts loose, the drop outside lands straight onto icy granite ledges.

I pull a six-inch strand of dark gray thread from the frayed hem of my coat lining. I wedge one end into the upper doorjamb gap just above the brass hinge and tuck the loose tail flat against the molding—a crude tamper-trap so I'll know if Hollis checks my room while I'm downstairs.

My left wrist aches under the wool cuff, the thin pink ridge of my Salt Point scar throbbing faintly in the damp chill. Two weeks ago, paying off that eighteen-thousand-dollar judgment felt like buying my life back. Back in Portland, I walked out of Becky's Diner thinking the worst thing in my life was a flooded basement audit. Now I'm locked inside a freezing mountain fortress with a sabotaged car, a caretaker who carries bolt cutters, and a dead man's cane sitting dry in the hall mudroom.

I sit on the edge of the stripped four-poster mattress in my coat and boots, listening to the low thrum of the diesel generator vibrating through the floorboards. Unlike Maine, there is no ocean surf or distant harbor buoy out here—only dead winter silence pressing against the granite walls.

At ten-thirty, heavy boots cross the Great Hall flagstones below, followed by the metallic click of a latch near the study archway. Silence settles over the house. At eleven-fifteen, the floorboards stop creaking altogether, and a door latches softly somewhere down near the first-floor caretaker's office.

I wait fifteen more minutes until the glowing hands on my wristwatch read eleven-thirty. I kneel by the threshold to test the hallway air—zero light, zero wintergreen tobacco smell—and ease my door open wide enough to slip out into the dark gallery.

***

The second-floor corridor smells of cold cedar, dry plaster, and old varnish. I hug the inner wall where the oak joists meet the lathe, avoiding the center boards that bow under weight, and slip past the locked double doors of the master suite. No light leaks under the threshold. Across the landing, the third-floor attic stairs sit swallowed in pitch black.

At the east end of the gallery, I ease through the narrow service door and descend the pie-shaped back stairs behind the main chimney stack.

At the bottom, the commercial kitchen sits pitch-black except for a tiny amber pilot LED blinking on the wall intercom panel beside the pantry. Outside the frosted porch pane, moonlight reflects dimly off snowbanks, throwing faint silver stripes across the copper stockpots overhead.

Hollis said he locked the pantry hall from his side. I test the swinging baize door separating the kitchen from the Great Hall—sure enough, a heavy brass deadbolt locks it fast from the hall side. He meant to keep me out of the main floor at night, forgetting—or not caring—that the narrow plank door tucked behind the walk-in larder drops straight down to the sub-basement boiler vault.

I test the cellar knob gently. Locked with an interior mortise bolt. I slide Sloane's brass skeleton key into the oiled keyhole, brace the iron latch with my left thumb so it doesn't snap, and turn the wards. They glide without a scrape.

I ease the heavy plank door open, slip onto the top cellar tread, and pull the door nearly shut behind me, leaving half an inch of clearance so the latch won't click loud in the silent kitchen.

Warm, oily air rises from below, smelling heavily of diesel exhaust, damp concrete, and hot cast iron. The steady mechanical thrum of the backup generator grows louder with every step down the steep wooden stairs.

At the bottom, a narrow concrete vault opens around a massive, soot-streaked cast-iron boiler flanked by two tall black heating-oil tanks. A single caged work bulb hangs low over the diesel generator in the far corner, casting harsh yellow light across copper supply pipes, pressure gauges, and gray block walls.

Nobody sits on the wooden stool beside the boiler workbench. Hollis must have bled the fuel line, flipped the main breaker, and gone back up to bed—or locked himself into the caretaker's wing.

I step quietly onto the concrete floor, checking the far corner of the vault first. No second stair leads out of this cellar—only a sloping concrete bulkhead ramp covered by heavy steel cellar doors bolted shut from the inside with a dropped iron bar. Across the workbench sit two spare brass pressure gauges, a coffee tin of pipe fittings, and a half-empty bottle of lamp kerosene matching the smell from the Great Hall hearth.

My gaze swings back to the twin black heating-oil tanks beside the boiler.

Clipped to the front manifold hangs a grease-stained laminated maintenance clipboard with a stubby carpenter's pencil dangling from twine. I lift the clipboard gently and tilt it toward the caged work bulb.

Tuesday's noon entry, written in blocky, cramped print, lists the primary oil tank sight-glass reading: **142 GAL.** Above it, Monday's entry sits at **145 GAL**—three gallons burned over twenty-four hours of normal winter idling before the storm hit.

I crouch beside the clear glass sight-tube bolted to the side of Tank A and tap the dusty glass lightly with my knuckle so the dark red float settles.

The brass indicator ring sits cleanly at **124 GAL.**

Eighteen gallons of heating oil burned in less than forty-eight hours. Six times the normal winter burn rate, while the guest wing sits cold enough to see my breath.

I frown, staring up at the copper manifold pipes branching off the top of the cast-iron boiler. When Hollis marched me into the west guest room two hours ago, the radiator beneath the shuttered window was stone-cold, its brass valve shut tight. Downstairs in the Great Hall, the flagstones felt chilled enough to numb my toes through wool socks. Even the study felt like an icebox.

If the main house thermostats sit turned down near freezing, where did eighteen gallons of fuel oil go since Tuesday's blizzard?

***

I follow the thick copper supply pipes upward along the concrete ceiling joists, tracing each brass shutoff wheel by hand.

Zone 1—*Great Hall & Study*—brass gate valve cranked closed. Zone 2—*West Guest Wing*—closed tight, the copper pipe dead cold under my bare palm. Zone 3—*Master Suite*—barely lukewarm, throttled down to a trickle just enough to keep the pipes from splitting.

Then my fingers touch the fourth copper riser curving up toward the east chimney chase behind the boiler.

I snatch my hand back with a sharp hiss. The bare copper pipe burns blistering hot, radiating waves of dry heat against my knuckles. Above the elbow, a heavy brass manual damper lever sits cranked wide open at full throttle, stenciled in faded black paint along the floor joist: **EAST ATTIC RISER — ROOM 4.**

Someone isn't heating the guest rooms or the Great Hall. Hollis Greer—or whoever set this manual bypass valve—has been pumping eighteen gallons' worth of scalding boiler heat straight up the east chimney chase into the locked third-floor attic for two straight days.

Wait. Not Hollis. Hollis locked the third-floor fire door from the *outside*—or told me he locked a trespasser inside the east gable. Why would Hollis burn eighteen gallons of his own heating oil to keep a trespasser warm? Unless he never checked the manual bypass damper behind the boiler, or someone else slipped down here after Tuesday's storm and cranked the East Attic valve wide open.

I step past the hot cast-iron furnace jacket toward the back corner of the concrete vault, where the whitewashed brick chimney stack drops straight into the cellar floor. The air back here smells different—cold limestone dampness cutting through the diesel fumes, mixed with fine gray coal dust.

Set low into the brick base—barely waist-high—sits a heavy vertical cast-iron damper plate riding inside greased steel side tracks, held in place by a thick iron counterweight lever. Above the frame, a slanted brick shaft angles steeply upward through the rear foundation wall toward the hillside behind the kitchen wing.

*Coal & Ash Chute.* The dotted red line from Arthur's 1904 survey map.

I step closer, crouching to check the soot-dusted concrete floor beneath the iron damper lip. Most of the basement dust sits thick and undisturbed—Wait.

Right below the bottom edge of the iron damper, the fine gray soot has been swept clean in a narrow smear, as if the sliding plate was hoisted up recently. A fresh smudge of dark-blue fountain ink stains the rusty iron counterweight handle, barely an inch from a pale scrape in the soot where someone's boot heel slipped.

Before I can reach for the lever, the heavy iron damper plate clanks softly from the inside of the brick chute.

I freeze, my hand hovering an inch above my multi-tool.

The iron plate slides upward a fraction of an inch in its greased tracks. A rush of freezing mountain air whistles straight down the brick shaft into the warm boiler room, carrying the sharp scent of pine needles, snow, and wet wool.

Then a faint, ragged whisper slides through the narrow iron gap in the dark:

"Clara? Don't turn your flashlight on."
