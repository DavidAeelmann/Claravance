# Chapter 8

At eight-fifteen, Hollis's Ford F-250 rumbles back up the drive from the iron gates and disappears behind the carriage barn. Five minutes later, the side barn door thuds shut, and his heavy boots crunch past the woodshed toward the kitchen porch.

He thinks I'm still upstairs in the Master Suite cataloging Arthur's tweed jackets.

I don't wait for him to climb the main stairs. I slip out of the Master Suite with the $38,000,000 covenant and the wrapped crystal stopper tucked deep inside my coat, sprint back to the west guest room, and grab my canvas daypack off the top closet shelf. My navy duffel stays right where I left it on the cedar floor, zipper aligned flush against the seam. Before I leave, I press my palm against the cast-iron radiator under the shuttered window. Still stone-cold—all that heating oil is still pouring straight up the East Attic riser.

I descend the narrow service stairs behind the chimney stack, ease through the silent kitchen before Hollis reaches the second floor, and slip out the rear porch door into the blinding morning glare.

Cold sunlight needles my eyes off the fresh drifts. Out past the woodshed, I strap the lightweight aluminum snowshoes over my winter boots, tighten the nylon heel bindings until they bite into my ankles, and angle sharply into the black spruce timberline *east* of the main drive so my tracks won't cross the packed truck ruts.

Spruce boughs dump dry powder down my collar every time my shoulder brushes a low branch. The air tastes like iron and frost. Ten minutes down the slope, the timber opens briefly near the stone boundary wall above the wrought-iron gates. I pause behind a wide hemlock trunk and peer down into the spruce turnout where my 2014 silver Honda Civic sits buried to the rocker panels.

Fresh waffle-tread tire tracks from Hollis's F-250 curve in beside the gate pillars and back out again—stopped just long enough for a handoff through the window. No new boot prints circle my Civic's rear bumper. My magnetic encrypted tablet case is still tucked out of sight behind the driver's-side rear wheel well liner.

I push off into the trees again before the wind erases my balance. By nine-thirty, my thighs burn from breaking trail through knee-deep drifts, and cold sweat cools along my spine under the heavy wool coat. Halfway down the mountain, I cross a frozen granite brook where gray ice groans under the snowshoe webbing, then follow the timbered ridge parallel to the unplowed logging spur.

Two hours of hard climbing and sliding down the grade brings me out above the lower county line, where the private Cragmont track meets the salted town road.

Ahead, through a clearing in the bare maples, a bright orange-and-black county plow truck sits idling crosswise across the snow-packed turnaround, its heavy steel V-blade angled like a roadblock between two frozen culverts.

***

I unbuckle the snowshoes in the ditch beside the county road, stuff them back into the canvas daypack, and step onto the salted gravel shoulder. Diesel exhaust hangs thick and oily in the cold air, rattling from the plow truck's vertical stack.

Before I reach the front bumper, my eyes scan the rear bed of the county truck. No orange highway-department detour sawhorses sit strapped to the rack. No sand-and-salt spreader sits loaded in the hopper either—just two heavy steel ballast blocks and a battered thermos rolling across empty steel. If the state closed a washed-out bridge three bends down, this truck isn't carrying a single barricade sign to prove it.

The driver's door swings open before I reach the fender.

A wiry man in his early fifties steps down onto the running board, wearing an Essex County Sheriff's reserve fleece over Carhartt bibs and a faded knit watch cap. He chews a cinnamon toothpick in the corner of his mouth, his pale eyes dropping straight to the snow-caked knees of my wool trousers.

"You're the Boston clerk Hollis mentioned on the radio," he says, resting one gloved hand on the open cab door. "Deputy Enoch Pike. County road's closed past this turnaround, miss. You shouldn't be hiking down here alone."

"Tessa Calder," I say, stopping just outside his arm's reach while keeping my breathing steady. "And I'm not hiking for fun. Arthur Cutler's cellar boiler is burning through fuel oil, my car's ignition harness was snipped outside your gate last night, and I need a tow truck out of St. Johnsbury before noon."

Pike shifts the cinnamon toothpick to the other cheek and spits lightly into the snowbank. "Can't get a wrecker up here even if you paid double. Covered bridge three bends below the county line washed an abutment during Tuesday's thaw-and-freeze. State highway crew dropped barricades at dawn. Nobody's rolling past the bend till Friday night at the earliest."

Another clean lie. Tuesday's storm was a subzero blizzard, not a spring thaw—there wasn't enough meltwater on this ridge to float a pine cone, let alone wash out a granite bridge abutment.

"That's funny," I say, stepping two paces closer to the open cab door as if trying to get out of the wind. "Margot Kiefer-Hall's office called the county highway garage yesterday afternoon and nobody mentioned a washed-out bridge. And your hopper's empty—no detour signs, no salt."

Pike's mouth flattens into a hard line. "County garage doesn't plow private timber spurs, and I dropped the barricades at the bridge two hours ago," he replies smoothly, reaching back into the cab to turn down his heater fan. "Look, Hollis Greer's a friend of mine. He's got his hands full keeping that lodge from freezing while the family's out on the sleds. Walk back up before the wind picks up, and I'll send a wrecker up Friday evening once the crew clears the span."

He leans across the steering wheel to pull the door shut.

Right above his knit cap, clipped behind the driver's sun visor, sits a thick white legal envelope. The unsealed flap faces outward toward the windshield, held down by a blob of dark brown pine-resin wax—still fresh enough to catch the morning glare.

Pressed squarely into the cooled wax sits a distinct, three-pad grease smudge—index, middle, and pinky, with a smooth blank space where a right ring fingertip should be. Even from three paces outside the cab, the uneven thickness of the envelope screams folded cash.

Hollis Greer didn't just radio Pike at the gate this morning. He drove down in his F-250, met Pike at the lower turnout, and paid him off in cash to keep Cragmont Lodge's only road sealed shut until Friday night—right after Simone Cutler's $38,000,000 survivorship deadline clears at five o'clock.

***

"I'd like to use your truck radio to call my Boston office," I say, keeping my tone stubborn and bureaucratic. "My cell's dead on this ridge, and the lodge landline hasn't had a dial tone since Tuesday. If you won't let a wrecker up, at least patch a status call through dispatch."

Pike pulls the cab door half-shut, blocking my view of the visor envelope with his shoulder. "County VHF doesn't patch commercial calls to Boston, miss. And Hollis told me the lodge's copper trunk snapped under an ice limb during the squall. Nothing goes in or out on wire till the power company runs a line truck."

Before I can push back, the black Motorola dispatch console bolted beneath his dashboard squawks loud through the cold air.

Static hisses, followed by the clipped, nasal voice of an Essex County dispatcher.

*"Essex Dispatch to Unit Fourteen—Pike, you copy? Automated trunk audit just flagged a copper line-short on the old Cragmont Spur from early Wednesday morning. Someone pushed a fax carrier through to a Boston six-one-seven exchange before dawn, then the line dead-shorted mid-upload. You near the gate?"*

Pike freezes with one boot on the steel running board. His face drains of color.

I don't move a muscle either, staring past his shoulder at the glowing green LED on the radio face.

Cragmont Lodge's landline wasn't snapped by an ice limb during Tuesday night's blizzard. Someone inside that "offline" house pushed a fax carrier out through the copper trunk before dawn Wednesday—and got cut off mid-upload when someone else inside the lodge ripped the wire straight out of the wall.

Whoever is hiding in that east attic pipe chase isn't a random squatter. They're trying to push those pages out before Friday at five o'clock.

Pike grabs the coiled handset. He turns his broad back toward me as he keys the plastic mic button hard.

"Unit Fourteen copies, Dispatch," he says, his voice dropping tight and rough. "Must be a short in the junction box from the storm. I'm at the turnaround now—heading up to the iron gate to meet Greer."

He switches channels with a sharp click of his thumb, keys the mic again, and barks into the cab: *"Hollis—pick up. County just flagged a fax carrier on your landline from Wednesday morning. Meet me at the lower gate right now."*

He slams the handset back onto the dashboard cradle and glares down at me from the cab step, his right hand dropping instinctively toward the heavy leather snap holster on his belt.

"Get in the passenger side," Pike snaps, no trace of friendly small-town deputy left in his voice. "Now. Hollis and I are going to find out who's messing with the lines up there."

I step backward toward the snowy ditch, my right hand slipping into my coat pocket around the steel multi-tool. If I climb into that cab, Pike drives me straight back to Hollis with a story about me snooping around the county line—and Hollis will know I hiked down here while he thought I was in the Master Suite. Worse, if Hollis is driving down the ridge to meet Pike at the gate right now, the lodge upstairs is wide open for the next twenty minutes.

"No thanks," I tell him, pivoting toward the steep snowy cutbank where my snowshoe trail enters the spruce. "I'll take my own trail back."

I scramble up the frozen bank into the trees before his boots hit the gravel, hearing his truck gear grind into low range behind me as he swings the heavy plow blade around toward the lodge gate. Once the spruce boughs swallow me, I buckle my snowshoes tight and start climbing hard.
