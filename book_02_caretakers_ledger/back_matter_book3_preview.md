# Sneak Peek: The Quarantine Manifest
### A Clara Vance Thriller — Book 3 by David Seelmann

## Chapter 1

Salt spray freezes along the rusted iron gunwale of the Narragansett mail skiff, coating my wool sleeves in a thin crust of gray rime.

Ahead through the December sleet, **Barrow Island** rises out of lower Narragansett Bay like the humped spine of a drowned whale. No ferry dock welcomes visitors here. Only a jagged granite breakwater and a decommissioned nineteenth-century brick quarantine hospital sit above the black swell, their slated roofs furred with white gull droppings and dead salt marsh vine.

Behind the wheelhouse glass, the boatman shifts his throttle into neutral while the hull bumps against rotted oak pilings.

"Forty minutes, lady," he calls out through the rusted megaphone slot, spitting tobacco juice into the bilge. "Tide turns at four. Once the south current hits that riprap channel, I don't hold the stern line for the Coast Guard, let alone a Portland insurance clerk."

"Forty minutes is plenty," I say, adjusting the strap of my faded navy canvas duffel across my left shoulder.

Beneath my right glove, the pink scar seam across my index knuckle pulls tight against the cold leather—my souvenir from a burning mountain ridge in Vermont twelve days ago. Under my left cuff sits the thinner scar from Salt Point. My checking account back in Portland still holds forty-two thousand dollars of Mercer-Hall Fiduciary's money, and my sister **Maya** is currently locked inside my third-floor apartment on the Eastern Promenade with two federal marshals parked downstairs on Congress Street.

Safe. Exonerated. Breathing clean harbor air.

I should be sitting beside her at my kitchen table drinking hot coffee.

Instead, I reach into my right coat pocket and wrap my gloved fingers around the two objects Maya and I pulled out of her rusted Burlington lockbox forty-eight hours ago.

The first is a heavy **mortise ward key** cast in tarnished marine brass, its bow stamped with the same octagonal port-clearance code that sat on the back cover of Arthur Vane's calfskin ledger:

`B.I.Q.S.-04 — WARD C.`

The second is a folded carbon sheet torn from my late father's **1998 Vance Maritime & Cargo** survey logbook. Across the bottom of that yellowed sheet—certifying the offload of six refrigerated steel containment crates into Barrow Island's decommissioned basement morgue—sits a countersignature in thick black fountain ink that I have stared at in civil court nightmares for three years:

**Victor Haldane.**

Chief marine underwriter at **Harbor Mutual Insurance**. The exact man who testified under oath that I falsified the Commercial Street warehouse crane logs in Portland, stripped my investigator's license, and buried me under an eighteen-thousand-dollar judgment to make sure I never audited that wharf fire again.

I step off the pitching bow onto the slick granite seawall, my rubber lug soles biting into wet kelp and frozen barnacles. Behind me, the mail skiff backs twenty yards off the pilings, its diesel exhaust hanging blue and sour over the chop.

The island smells of dead eelgrass, wet brick, and cold iron rust.

According to Rhode Island state land records, **Quarantine Station No. 4** was shuttered in 1984 and sold to a Providence maritime preservation trust that went defunct twelve years ago. The wrought-iron perimeter fence is bowed inward under decades of hurricane surf, its padlock rusted solid into a lump of orange scale.

Except nobody walks through the front gate.

I crouch beside the concrete steps leading up from the boat slip toward the hospital's boarded portico.

Yesterday's sleet storm left a clean skin of gray slush across the granite risers. Pressed deep into the center of every step—cutting straight through the frozen slush toward the basement coal bulkhead on the leeward side of the brick ward—runs a fresh trail of rubber wheel ruts.

Dual pneumatic tires. Hand-truck gauge.

And beside the left tire track, stamped crisp into the wet salt crust, sits the heel print of a men's dress boot with a custom steel toe-tap.

My pulse beats slow and steady against my collarbones as I stand up.

The island isn't abandoned. Someone wheeled a heavy load up from the boat slip after yesterday's sleet stopped—less than twelve hours ago.

I stay off the concrete steps, keeping my boots on the frozen marsh grass along the foundation wall until I reach the leeward corner of **Ward C**. Above me, three stories of red brick and arched window openings stare out over the bay, every sash sealed from the inside with corrugated steel storm shutters bolted into the masonry.

Down at ground level, recessed into a granite well beneath the old contagious-ward autopsy wing, sits a pair of sloped iron bulkhead doors.

Wait.

I stop at the top of the granite steps and drop into a crouch without touching the handrail.

The iron bulkhead isn't rusted shut. A brand-new stainless-steel padlock hangs open through the brass hasp, its shackle greased with fresh white lithium lubricant that hasn't even collected salt grit yet.

From somewhere deep beneath the brick foundation comes a low, rhythmic hum—not the diesel roar of an estate backup generator, but the smooth, sixty-cycle purr of a modern **submerged submarine cable transformer**. Someone is pulling commercial three-phase power across the bay floor into a building that hasn't existed on the municipal grid since the Reagan administration.

I draw my steel multi-tool from my pocket, thumb the heavy flashlight tube into my left palm, and ease the right-hand bulkhead leaf up by an inch.

The greased hinges don't squeak.

Warm air drifts up the stone stairwell—carrying the sharp, unmistakable scent of **medical formalin**, **wet cedar crate shavings**, and **expensive Turkish pipe tobacco**.

Victor Haldane's tobacco. The same sweet, cloying Latakia blend that filled the Harbor Mutual conference room the afternoon he handed me my termination papers.

I descend the twelve granite steps in total silence, keeping my weight on the outside edges of my boots where the stone meets the brick wall. At the bottom of the well stands a heavy oak fire door sheathed in riveted galvanized tin. Directly below the brass lever sits an antique mortise keyhole—its faceplate freshly wiped clean of verdigris.

I slip my father's tarnished **WARD C** brass key out of my coat pocket and slide the bit into the lock.

The wards align with a soft, oiled click.

I depress the lever and push the tin-clad door open a crack.

Inside runs a long, whitewashed brick vault lit by low-wattage amber bulkhead cages. Along the left wall stand four stainless-steel refrigeration lockers wired into a digital temperature manifold reading **four degrees Celsius**. Across the center of the concrete floor, sitting on a yellow hydraulic hand-truck beside a crowbar and a pile of damp pine slats, is a freshly pried shipping crate stenciled with the faded crest of the **Portland Custom House Wharf**.

And resting open on a steel stainless autopsy table under the center amber bulb is a black leather cargo manifest binder—its spine stamped with the gold-leaf emblem of **Vance Maritime & Cargo, 1998**.

I take two silent steps across the concrete drain grate, pull my phone out to photograph the open page, and freeze.

The manifest sheet isn't listing refrigeration compressors or pharmaceutical vials. It's a bonded passenger-and-specimen transfer log from a vessel that supposedly sank off Block Island twenty-six years ago—and the final line item, written in my father's neat, square draftsman's print, has been circled twice in fresh red grease pencil.

Before I can read the name inside the red circle, the amber bulkhead lights snap off, plunging the brick vault into pitch darkness.

Behind me at the top of the granite stairwell, the heavy iron bulkhead doors slam down over the concrete curb, and a steel slide-bolt grates shut from the outside.
