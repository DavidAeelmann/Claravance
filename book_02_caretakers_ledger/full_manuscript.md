# The Caretaker's Ledger
*A Clara Vance Thriller (Book 2) — By David Seelmann*

---

# Chapter 1

Tire chains bite hard into packed mountain ice, rattling up through the floorboards of my 2014 silver Honda Civic with every switchback.

Outside my salted windshield, the Northeast Kingdom of Vermont looks nothing like the Maine coast. There is no open water here, no foghorns groaning across a harbor channel, and no tide clock counting down the hours. Instead, towering black spruce crowd both edges of the unplowed logging road, their boughs bent low under fresh snow that swallows headlights ten paces ahead.

On the passenger seat beside me sits my faded navy canvas duffel, zipped tight over forty-five thousand dollars' worth of new nondisclosure paperwork from Mercer-Hall Fiduciary. Tucked into the cup holder, resting atop the folded copy of my restored Maine private investigator bond, is the heavy brass skeleton key Sloane Mercer-Hall slid across the table at Becky's Diner yesterday morning.

Even inside the heated cab, the key smells faintly of cold iron and pine resin.

My left wrist still itches beneath my wool cuff where the Salt Point stitches came out, a thin pink ridge against pale skin. Twenty-four hours ago, I had a ten o'clock interview lined up on Congress Street for a daylight desk job auditing flooded boat hulls. Safe work. Clean chain-of-custody. Zero locked rooms.

Then Sloane flipped a glossy photograph over on the diner Formica and showed me three words written in my younger sister's slanted, left-leaning cursive in the corner of a mountain guest ledger:

*Don't trust him.*

Six years. Seventy-two months since Maya walked out of a winter internship in Burlington and vanished off every bank grid, tax roll, and credit bureau in New England. I ruined my savings and half my twenties chasing cold tips across three states before the Harbor Mutual lawsuit buried me for good. I told myself I had accepted the silence.

One ink stroke proved I was lying.

Ahead, through a break in the timber, wrought-iron gates loom out of the drifting snow. They match the photograph in my coat pocket—tall forged spears flanked by rough fieldstone pillars, guarding the private drive to Blackthorne Lodge. According to Sloane's briefing packet, pharmaceutical heir Arthur Vane, his second wife, and their live-in winter caretaker walked out of the lodge during Tuesday night's blizzard and left three running snowmobiles idling by the woodshed. Nobody has filed a missing-persons report yet. The family trustees in Boston want the house inventoried and cleared of private papers before the county sheriff asks why the snowmobiles ran out of gas.

I ease my foot onto the brake pedal and bring the Civic to a halt in front of the iron gates.

My headlights sweep across the drift piled against the lower crossbars.

I don't reach for the gate latch. I sit still behind the wheel, heater fan clicking softly in the dash, staring down at the snow between the stone pillars.

Sloane told me the private road had been drifted shut since Tuesday night's storm. She said the state plow stops at the county line three miles back, and nobody from the Boston office had been able to reach the mountain.

Yet two deep, wide tire ruts cut straight through the powder beneath the iron gates—fresh enough that the waffle tread of heavy mud-terrain tires hasn't filled in with blowing snow.

Someone drove a heavy truck up this mountain within the last hour. And judging by the single set of tracks pointing uphill, they haven't driven back down.

***

I kill the Civic's headlights, leave the car tucked in the dark spruce turnout outside the pillars, and slip through the narrow pedestrian gap beside the stone post with my duffel slung over one shoulder.

Cold air bites into my lungs like crushed glass, dry and thin, smelling of cedar needles and woodsmoke. Wait—not old soot from a cold flue. Fresh birch smoke, sharp and resinous, drifting down from the ridge above.

My boots crunch softly in the truck ruts as I climb the final bend of the drive.

Blackthorne Lodge rises against the gray twilight sky like a stone fortress wedged into the granite mountainside. Steep timber gables sag under thick white drifts. Every window across the three-story front elevation stands dark, heavy interior timber shutters latched tight behind frosted panes. The turnaround in front of the wide stone porch is empty—no pickup truck in sight—Wait.

I follow the fresh tire ruts with my eyes. They don't stop at the front steps. They curve past the main house toward a timber carriage barn fifty paces back in the trees, where a pair of double barn doors stands padlocked from the outside, fresh snow kicked loose around the threshold.

Whoever drove up here parked their truck inside the barn and locked it behind them.

I step onto the covered porch, keeping my weight near the stone wall where the hemlock planks don't creak. In my right pocket, my fingers wrap around my steel multi-tool. In my left hand, I hold the heavy brass skeleton key from Sloane's briefcase.

I slide the resin-stained bit into the oversized iron mortise lock on the front oak door.

The wards turn without a scrape. Oiled recently.

I push the heavy door open a crack and slip inside, easing the latch shut behind my heel until the bolt clicks home.

The great hall is cavernous and dim, lit only by pale snow-glare filtering through high clerestory glass near the rafters. Mounted stag heads stare down from dark timber walls above a massive fieldstone fireplace. Across the room, a wide oak staircase climbs toward a shadowed balcony.

The air inside isn't freezing. It's warm.

I walk slowly toward the hearth. No flames burn on the iron grate, but a thick bed of gray birch ash glows dull orange at the center, radiating steady heat across the flagstone floor. Beneath the birch smoke hangs a second smell—greasy, chemical, and unmistakable to anyone who ever investigated an arson claim.

Lamp kerosene. And burnt bond paper.

Someone used the hearth to ash a stack of files less than twenty minutes ago.

To my right, beside the archway leading to the study, stands a slanted oak reception lectern—the old-fashioned kind built for nineteenth-century hunting clubs. Resting open across its brass lip is a thick, maroon-leather guest ledger.

My pulse beats hard against my collarbones as I cross the flagstones.

I recognize the cream, red-ruled paper from the cropped scan taped to the back of Sloane's photograph. Same faded header print. Same column lines for *Date*, *Guest*, and *Room*.

Except when I look down at the open ledger under the gray clerestory light, my breath catches in my throat.

The top sheet hasn't been turned. It has been sliced clean out along the leather spine with a straight-edged blade, leaving a jagged fringe of cream paper still glued into the binding.

Only the bottom right corner of the missing page tore loose before the blade finished its cut—a tiny triangular scrap pinned beneath a heavy brass desk weight. On that scrap sits the tail of a single cursive letter *m* in dark blue ink, slanting left. Maya's ink.

And resting directly on top of the sliced binding, where the wet glue is still tacky to the touch, sits a freshly stamped brass room tag on a steel ring.

**ROOM 4 — ATTIC EAST.**

Above my head, directly across the third-floor rafters where the attic corridor runs, a heavy floorboard groans under the slow, deliberate shift of a boot.

Then a key turns in the upper stairwell lock, sealing me inside the hall.

# Chapter 2

The key turns twice in the upper stairwell lock, iron scraping dry brass, and then the third-floor rafters fall silent.

I don't move. My boots stay planted on the cold flagstones beside the oak lectern while my lungs pull in thin, kerosene-tainted air. Overhead, the heavy floorboard doesn't groan again. Whoever stands on the attic landing isn't pacing. They're listening.

Wait. If they locked the stairwell door from above, they didn't lock me *into* the Great Hall—they locked themselves *out* of it, or sealed the third floor against me. Either way, the front oak door behind my back is still my way out. I pivot slowly toward the porch threshold, keeping my heels off the stone so my snow-damp treads don't squeak.

My left hand still grips Sloane's brass skeleton key. My right fingers close around the cold steel frame of my multi-tool inside my coat pocket. Before I cross the hall, I glance back at the lectern. If I leave the torn corner scrap and the brass room tag sitting on the wet glue, anyone who comes downstairs will know I stood right here and read their work. I peel the freshly stamped **ROOM 4 — ATTIC EAST** tag off the tacky binding—a thin strand of hide glue stretches and snaps—and slip both the brass tag and the tiny triangular scrap of Maya's ink into my inside breast pocket.

I cross the flagstones toward the front oak door and test the heavy iron thumb-turn. It slides smoothly. I pull the inward-swinging oak slab open half a hand's breadth—and freeze.

Beyond the oak threshold sits the heavy timber-and-iron storm door I passed on the porch, designed to swing outward against mountain drifts. Right now, its iron latch bar is jammed tight from the exterior porch side. Through the narrow crack between the two doors, freezing wind whistles across my knuckles, carrying fresh birch smoke and the unmistakable metallic click of a steel padlock hasp swinging against wood.

Someone didn't just lock the third-floor stairs. While I stood staring at the lectern, a second person—or the man who parked that truck in the carriage barn—slipped onto the porch and padlocked the outer storm door behind me. I shove my shoulder against the inner oak door just to check if the storm latch has play, though the iron frame holds solid without giving a hair.

I ease the inner oak door shut and throw the thumb-turn back into place before the wind rattles the glass and announces where I'm standing.

Trapped inside. Just like Salt Point, except there is no ocean glass to smash here—only thick granite walls and heavy interior timber shutters bolted tight over every ground-floor window. Even the high clerestory panes near the rafters sit too far above the stone floor to reach without a rigging ladder.

I shift the strap of my navy duffel higher on my shoulder, feeling the stiff leather spine of Sloane's nondisclosure portfolio press against my ribs. Four walls. Two staircases—one grand oak flight rising straight across the Great Hall to the second-floor gallery, and whatever service stairs hide behind the kitchen archway.

If the person upstairs wanted me dead in the next thirty seconds, they'd be coming down those stairs with a rifle. Instead, they locked the top door. Why lock yourself upstairs unless you're guarding something up there—or buying time to clear out through another exit?

I cross the Great Hall along the shadowed wall beneath the mounted stag heads, avoiding the dull orange glow of the birch embers on the hearth. The flagstones give way to dark hardwood planks beside the study archway, and to my left, a narrow paneled door stands ajar beneath the curve of the main staircase.

The hall mudroom.

I push the door wide with my elbow, holding my breath against the stale smell of wet wool, cedar mothballs, and dried road salt.

***

No overhead bulb burns in the mudroom, so I pull the small penlight from my coat pocket and cup my fingers over the lens to keep the beam narrow and low.

The space is cramped. Iron hooks line the cedar wall. Three heavy waxed-canvas parkas hang undisturbed along the pegs, their brass snaps dull with tarnish. Beneath the bench, pairs of rubber chore boots sit neatly aligned on a galvanized drip tray. The paneling smells faintly of damp wool and cedar oil. Nobody has tracked snow in here for days.

My light sweeps the corner umbrella stand—and stops.

A heavy walnut walking cane fitted with a custom, oversized rubber ferrule leans upright against the cedar wainscoting. The dark wood handle is worn pale along the *left* side of the curve where a palm leaned hard against it, and a brass collar halfway down the shaft bears an engraved monogram: *A.V.* Beside it sits a small brass tin of orthopedic heel wax, its lid crusted with dried yellow balm.

Beside the cane sits a pair of tall, shearling-lined leather snow boots. I crouch and run one bare knuckle across the leather toes and rubber treads. Bone-dry. Not a speck of melted snow, salt crust, or pine needle sits trapped in the lug pattern. I straighten slowly, clicking the penlight off in my palm.

Sloane's briefing packet claimed pharmaceutical heir Arthur Vane, his second wife, and their winter caretaker walked out into Tuesday night's blizzard and rode three snowmobiles off the ridge. Maybe Arthur didn't ride a snowmobile. Maybe he only walked to the woodshed—except even on flat floorboards, a man who wears down the walnut grip of a custom orthopedic cane doesn't hike into a whiteout wearing thin house slippers while leaving his shearling snow boots dry in the mudroom.

I step back out into the dim Great Hall and test the study archway next. The tall mahogany doors stand closed, their brass lever handles cold under my thumb. Locked. I crouch and peer through the keyhole—pitch black, no key resting on the inside, and no draft moving beneath the threshold.

Across the hall, the wide oak staircase climbs into shadow toward the second-floor balcony. Above that, invisible from the flagstones, sits the third-floor landing where the key turned.

If I wait down here in the dark, whoever is upstairs controls the high ground, the phone lines, and every exterior shutter. My chest feels tight, the familiar cold knot sitting behind my sternum—the same trap I swore off in Portland yesterday morning before Sloane slid Maya's cursive across the Formica. I can't walk out. The outer storm door is padlocked from the porch, and Maya's ink is still fresh on that torn corner in my pocket.

I sling my duffel off my shoulder and tuck it deep behind the heavy velvet drapes flanking the study archway where it won't slow me down on the stairs. My boots hug the wall stringer where oak treads carry the most support as I start up the main staircase.

Every step tastes like dust and cold varnish. Halfway up, the muffled hum of a diesel generator vibrates faintly through the floorboards beneath my soles—a steady, low thrum coming from somewhere deep below the kitchen wing. That explains why the hall felt warm despite the blizzard outside. Somebody is burning fuel to keep the pipes from freezing, or to keep one specific wing livable.

I reach the second-floor gallery landing. A wide corridor stretches left and right, lined with dark oil portraits and closed bedroom doors. No light leaks under any of them. At the far east end of the gallery, a narrower enclosed staircase angles sharply upward behind a wrought-iron handrail.

No carpet covers those upper treads. Just bare pine boards leading straight up to a heavy oak-and-iron fire door at the top.

I climb slowly, placing the ball of each boot near the riser so the old pine doesn't pop under my weight. When I reach the top landing, the smell hits me before my hand touches the brass hardware—fresh lamp kerosene mixed with sharp pine resin and heavy gun oil.

***

I shield my penlight between two fingers and flick the beam onto the fire door's mortise deadbolt plate.

The brass escutcheon gleams clean against the dark oak, wiped recently enough that no dust coats the bevel. Fresh oil glistens in the keyhole slot—the same slickness I felt when Sloane's skeleton key slid into the front porch lock without a scrape.

Then I tilt the narrow beam sideways across the polished brass plate just above the cylinder.

A dull, greasy smudge breaks the shine. Not a smeared palm print, and not the clean vertical wipe of a rag. Whoever braced their hand against this plate while turning the key left a distinct, three-pad impression in pine resin and gun oil: index finger, middle finger, and pinky.

Where the ring finger pad should press against the brass, there is only a smooth, uninterrupted sheen of clean metal.

I hold the light steady for two seconds, tracing the spacing of the three oily ridges. Left-handed writers smudge ink from the heel of the palm; right-handed mechanics brace the cylinder with their right thumb on the knob and their right fingertips spread flat against the upper plate. The missing pad belongs to a right ring finger—either bandaged stiff, or missing its tip entirely.

I click the penlight off and slip it back into my coat. Then I pull Sloane's brass skeleton key from my left pocket. The front door took it without a fight, oiled by the same hand. If Blackthorne Lodge runs on old nineteenth-century mortise wards, one master bit might throw this upper deadbolt too.

I slide the brass bit gently into the oiled keyhole. It stops halfway in—blocked from the far side by the steel tip of a key still sitting in the cylinder.

Whoever locked this door left their key inside the lock.

I lean close, pressing my ear directly against the cold oak panel, holding my breath until the blood thumps loud in my eardrums.

On the far side of the door, pine floorboards creak—slow, heavy, deliberate weight shifting away from the threshold toward the east gable. Not a woman's light step. Heavy winter boots, the exact same slow cadence I heard over the Great Hall rafters ten minutes ago.

Then the deep, steady vibration of the basement generator beneath my boots stutters twice—and dies completely.

The hall plunges into dead, suffocating silence. No furnace hum. No distant relay click. Even the faint draft along the floorboards stops as the blower fans coast to a halt.

Before I can pull back from the oak panel, a sharp metallic hiss erupts inside the plaster wall right beside my left shoulder—a taut steel cable racing downward through a hidden wooden shaft at full drop.

# Chapter 3

The dumbwaiter cable slams against the wooden guide rollers somewhere below the second floor, then stops with a dull thud of hemp rope slackening inside the wall.

I pull back from the third-floor fire door and descend the bare pine stairs without turning on my penlight. Darkness wraps the gallery corridor thick enough to press against my eyelids. No furnace blower hums beneath the floorboards now. Without the generator, the lodge settles into the cold creak of timber rafters contracting in the subzero drop outside.

At the east end of the gallery, a narrow service door stands half-latched beside a linen press. I push it open with my knuckles. Steep, pie-shaped back stairs spiral downward behind the main chimney stack, smelling strongly of cold bacon grease, damp plaster, and stale coffee grounds.

I take the treads sideways, one hand trailing the rough plaster wall to keep my balance. If the person behind the third-floor fire door didn't just drop that dumbwaiter car by accident—if somebody else is riding the shafts or sending cargo down from the attic—they're moving faster than I am.

At the bottom of the service flight, a swinging baize door opens into a cavernous commercial kitchen. Pale snow-glare leaks weakly through a small frosted pane set high in the rear porch door, enough to outline a long butcher-block island, copper stockpots hanging from an iron rack, and a double-door stainless refrigerator standing dead and silent.

To my right sits the butler's pantry.

The narrow wooden door to the dumbwaiter shaft hangs unlatched, a thin draft of warm air rising out of the dark vertical box. I pull my penlight, cup three fingers around the bezel, and shine a sliver of light down into the maple car resting at waist height.

Empty—almost.

In the back corner of the wooden tray sits a small, uncapped amber glass bottle with a stiff hog-bristle brush resting across its rim. I lean close without touching the glass. Sharp, animal-protein stink rises off the amber liquid—hot hide glue, still faintly warm from being carried against someone's coat pocket.

Beside the bottle lies a fresh crescent smudge of dark-blue fountain ink on the maple lip, no wider than a fingernail. I reach higher into the dark shaft and run two fingers along the hemp hoist rope. Warmth lingers on the braided fibers, and a faint smear of pine turpentine clings to the wood guide rail where someone braced a hand on the descent.

My pulse beats hard behind my ears. The man in heavy boots on the third-floor rafters locked the stairwell door *after* I walked into the Great Hall. Yet the `ROOM 4 — ATTIC EAST` brass tag was pressed onto wet hide glue *before* I crossed the flagstones—and whoever carried that glue bottle didn't walk down the grand staircase. They dropped straight down this service shaft.

Two people are moving inside Blackthorne Lodge. One of them is wearing heavy winter boots and burning paper in the hearth. The other is using the walls—and left me a key tag written in Maya's ink.

I click the penlight off and ease the pantry door shut until the wooden latch catches.

***

I cross the dark kitchen back toward the Great Hall archway, keeping the butcher block between my ribs and the frosted rear porch window.

Back in the main hall, the birch embers in the fieldstone hearth have dimmed to a faint cherry crust beneath gray ash. No boots sound overhead. I check the heavy velvet drapes beside the study archway first; my navy duffel still sits tucked behind the folds, untouched.

I pull Sloane's heavy brass skeleton key from my left pocket and kneel beside the locked mahogany doors of Arthur's study. If the same nineteenth-century mortise wards run through the ground floor, the master bit should throw this bolt too.

The brass wards slide in cleanly. No key blocks the far side here. I turn my wrist slowly, bracing the mahogany handle with my forearm so the bolt doesn't snap loud across the flagstones, and ease the door inward.

Stale cigarillo smoke hits my face. Clove tobacco, bitter and dry, hangs trapped inside a room sealed since Tuesday's storm. I step inside, close the door until the latch whispers shut, and flick my penlight low across the carpet.

Arthur's walnut desk dominates the center of the room. Its green leather blotter sits swept clean except for a heavy square brass paperweight—an exact twin to the one pinning Maya's torn ledger corner out in the Great Hall. Tall built-in bookshelves line the wall behind the desk, packed tight with bound medical journals and thick county land-survey binders. Against the east plaster hangs a framed, hand-inked topographical survey of the Blackthorne ridge.

I cross to the map, shielding the penlight beam against the glass. The heavy oak frame sits slightly crooked on its brass wall cleats, leaving a pale dust-free shadow along the plaster where someone swung it aside recently—probably to reach a recessed wall safe hidden behind the backing board. I don't pry the frame open now. No time to crack a dial safe with someone breathing upstairs.

Instead, I trace the survey's crisp surveyor's ink: the main lodge, the carriage barn fifty paces northwest in the spruce, the woodshed bay, and a half-buried granite rectangle tucked into the hillside directly behind the kitchen wing, labeled **ICEHOUSE — 1904**. A dotted red line runs straight from the back wall of that icehouse into the lodge's sub-basement boiler room.

*Coal & Ash Chute.*

I memorize the layout, then drop to one knee beside the small cast-iron hearth set into the study's north wall. Cold soot, untouched for weeks. Whoever burned bond paper tonight didn't use the study grate—they used the massive fieldstone fireplace out in the Great Hall.

I step back to the walnut desk and try the center drawer. Locked. I pull my steel multi-tool, pop the narrow flathead blade, and slide it into the brass gap above the lip just far enough to feel the bolt resistance. No give. Heavy mortise lock, not a cheap desk catch.

On top of the mahogany apothecary cabinet against the far wall sits a small brass tray holding three loose items: a silver cigarillo cutter, a crumpled Boston pharmacy receipt dated Monday, and a half-empty prescription vial of *Colchicine*—gout medication prescribed to Arthur Vane. The cabinet doors below stand bolted with a miniature brass padlock, the wood around the hasp scarred by fresh pry marks.

Acute gout in his left foot. Dry shearling boots in the mudroom. A custom walnut cane left behind in the stand.

Arthur Vane never walked out into Tuesday night's blizzard.

I slip out of the study, relock the mahogany door with the skeleton key so nobody spots a broken latch, and move silently toward the Great Hall hearth.

***

The fieldstone fireplace still radiates dry heat against my shins.

I kneel on the slate apron, ignoring the grit biting through my wool trousers, and wrap my gloved hand around the iron handle of the small brass ash shovel leaning against the stone jamb. The glowing birch coals sit mounded in the center of the grate, ringed by pale gray ash and curled black ribbons of burnt paper.

Most of the stack reduced to weightless carbon. Even a breath stirs the flakes into gray dust.

I slip the flat blade of the shovel carefully beneath the cooler ash along the *left* edge of the grate, where the air draft from the chimney flue pushes unburned corners against the firebrick. I lift a shallow scoop onto the cold hearth slate and spread the warm gray powder apart with the tip of my multi-tool.

Two blackened edges crumble at a touch. Beneath them lies a folded corner of heavy bond paper, singed brown along the crease where tight folding starved the flame of oxygen.

I pinch the unburned corner between my thumb and forefinger and tilt it toward the faint orange glow of the coals, clicking my penlight on for one second.

Stamped purple ink crosses the top margin: **VANE BIOTECH — CLINICAL AUDIT & DISBURSEMENT VOUCHER.** Below the header sits a partial ledger column—*Batch VC-09 / Pediatric Mortality Transfer*—and a handwritten dollar figure cut off halfway through the digits: **$145,000.00**.

The rest of the sheet ends in brittle charcoal.

I click the penlight off and fold the scorched voucher corner inside my breast pocket beside Maya's torn ledger scrap and the **ROOM 4** brass tag.

Someone drove a heavy mud-terrain pickup up this mountain within the last hour, parked inside the carriage barn, padlocked the outer front storm door behind me, and burned *Vane Biotech* clinical payout vouchers in this hearth using lamp kerosene. Meanwhile, a second person—carrying warm hide glue in the pantry dumbwaiter—sliced Maya's page out of the guest ledger, left me a directional tag for **ROOM 4 — ATTIC EAST**, and dropped back down the wall before the generator died.

Wait. If the person in heavy boots locked the third-floor fire door from *above* while I stood at the lectern, how did they get downstairs to padlock the front storm door? Unless the man who parked in the barn and padlocked the storm door *wasn't* the person on the third floor—or the person upstairs climbed down an exterior fire escape or service stair after sealing the attic.

No. Stop guessing and check the kitchen exit.

I slip quietly back through the dark archway toward the commercial kitchen. If the front storm door is padlocked from the porch, the rear kitchen porch door might still open from the inside—and if I can reach the carriage barn or my Civic in the spruce turnout, I can grab my spare tire iron and check what's sitting behind those padlocked barn doors.

Halfway across the kitchen flagstones, my boot heel catches a dry grain of spilled kosher salt near the butcher block.

I freeze.

Outside on the rear porch, crisp snow crunches under heavy, deliberate boots. Not climbing stairs. Stepping straight onto the back wooden landing.

A gloved fist slams hard against the frosted glass pane of the kitchen door, rattling the wood in its frame.

I drop low behind the butcher block—too late. A blinding halogen flashlight beam stabs straight through the frosted pane, pinning my silhouette against the copper pots behind the island.

Through the glass, a broad-shouldered man in a snow-crusted waxed parka raises a double-barreled shotgun until the twin steel muzzles press flat against the frosty pane.

"Put the brass key on the butcher block, lady," he barks through the wood, his voice thick and gravelly, "or I blow the latch off."

# Chapter 4

I don't reach for my multi-tool. Twin twelve-gauge muzzles sit an inch from frosted glass, and men holding shotguns in the dark don't wait for explanations.

I rise slowly from behind the butcher block with both empty palms raised beside my ears. The halogen beam blinds me for two heartbeats before dipping toward the maple island. I reach into my left coat pocket with two fingers, lift Sloane's heavy brass skeleton key by the bow, and lay it flat on the butcher block.

"Boston fiduciary office," I say, keeping my voice flat and dry, the same clerk cadence I used to bore insurance adjusters in Portland. "Sloane Mercer-Hall sent me up to catalog the estate before the county probate clerk arrives. Check the leather portfolio in my duffel if you want."

The man outside doesn't lower the barrel. Behind the glass, his silhouette shifts sideways as a heavy key ring jingles against his hip. A bolt snaps back inside the rear kitchen door. Cold air rushes across my ankles as he kicks the timber slab inward and steps over the threshold, filling the doorway in a snow-crusted waxed parka and size-eleven Sorel lug boots.

Up close, he smells strongly of pine resin, lamp kerosene, and wintergreen smokeless tobacco. Deep weathered lines bracket his mouth beneath a salt-and-pepper beard trimmed short against his jaw. He's pushing fifty, broad through the shoulders, and his left thumb rests steady across the shotgun's top tang safety.

"I'm Hollis Greer," he says, his eyes flicking from my wet snow boots to the brass key on the island. "Winter caretaker. Who gave you a master key to the front mortise?"

"Sloane Mercer-Hall. State Street." I nod toward the Great Hall archway without dropping my hands. "My navy duffel is behind the study curtains. I hid it when the stairs locked overhead."

Hollis's jaw tightens. He spits a thin dark stream of tobacco juice into the stainless kitchen sink beside the door, then steps past me—keeping the shotgun muzzle angled toward my knees—and snatches the brass skeleton key off the butcher block with his gloved right hand.

"You shouldn't be in here," he mutters, shoving the key into his parka pocket. "Generator threw a fuel line an hour ago, and some local trespasser broke into the east gable earlier today. I locked the third-floor fire door from the inside to pen him up until I could fetch my twelve-gauge from the barn, then padlocked the front storm door so nobody else wandered in."

A smooth lie. Too smooth. He didn't lock the third-floor fire door from the *inside* unless he climbed out a third-floor gable window into a snowbank—and if he locked the front storm door to keep people out, he did it *after* I was already standing at the lectern.

"I'm not here to argue with your security," I say, letting a trace of tired irritation leak into my voice. "I'm a records clerk on a flat ten-day fee. If there's a trespasser in the attic, call the sheriff from your truck. Otherwise, point me to the guest wing and show me the three snowmobiles so I can log the property status and sleep."

Hollis studies my face in the pale snow-glare from the door. For a second, I think he's going to march me straight out to the porch at gunpoint. Instead, he clicks the shotgun safety on with a sharp metallic snick and rests the barrels across his crooked left forearm.

"Show me that Boston paperwork first," he says. "Then I'll show you the woodshed."

***

Ten minutes later, Hollis stands beside the Great Hall hearth while a single battery lantern he pulled from his coat illuminates the leather *Mercer-Hall Fiduciary* portfolio in my lap. He doesn't touch the hearth ash. He doesn't glance at the sliced guest ledger on the lectern either, though the faint smell of lamp kerosene still hangs thick around the fireplace stones.

He reads Sloane's signed estate-archiving authorization twice, his gloved thumb smudging the embossed State Street seal, then hands the folder back without a word.

"Ten days," he says, his voice dropping low. "You stay out of the third floor until I clear that attic lock in daylight, and you keep clear of the carriage barn. Snow's too deep by the woodshed anyway."

"My briefing says Arthur Vane, his wife, and the caretaker left three snowmobiles idling by the woodshed Tuesday night," I say, zipping the portfolio back inside my navy duffel. I sling the bag over my shoulder—no chance I'm leaving Sloane's contract out of my sight again—and follow him back toward the kitchen. "Wait. If you're the caretaker, why did the Boston trustees think you walked out with them?"

Hollis pauses at the kitchen threshold, his broad back blocking the porch light. "Because I took my pickup down to the county line Tuesday afternoon to buy shearling blankets before the whiteout hit. Bridge washed out below the ridge, so I couldn't get back up until an hour ago. Somebody else took those sleds—or tried to."

Another clean cover story. He even accounted for the fresh mud-terrain truck ruts I saw under the iron gates in Chapter 1.

"Then I still need serial numbers off the sleds for the fiduciary inventory," I tell him, stepping onto the back porch ahead of his lantern. "Five minutes in the cold, and I'm out of your hair."

Freezing air hits my face like dry needles, sharp and still beneath a heavy pewter sky. The snow has stopped falling, leaving the ridge buried under deep white drifts that swallow every sound past the porch eaves.

Hollis leads me twenty paces behind the kitchen wing toward a low timber woodshed crouched beneath snow-laden black spruce. She sits huddled against the frosted rubber with her pale hands pressed over her ears, staring blankly at the roaring orange flames consuming the carriage barn and the rear kitchen roof. The thirty-eight-million-dollar clock is still ticking toward five o'clock, and she knows nobody is coming to cash it.

Maya steps clear of the woodshed bay, limping slightly against her taped ribs, and stops beside me at the iron gate. Her ink-stained fingers touch my torn wool sleeve—warm, steady, and real in the freezing dark. Buttoned safe beneath her flannel shirt rests the oilskin-wrapped **Caretaker's Ledger**.

"Sloane's still inside the Great Hall," I tell her, nodding toward the front oak door where thick black smoke is already curling out beneath the porch eaves. "Watch Celeste. If she stands up, hit her with Arthur's cane."

I sprint across the turnaround, take the covered hemlock porch steps two at a time, and throw the unlocked oak door wide. Thick, suffocating birch and diesel smoke pours out around my shoulders, stinging my throat raw. Behind the leather armchair, Sloane is slumped sideways on the flagstones, choking weakly into her camel hair sleeve while orange flames lick through the baize kitchen door behind the reception lectern. The heat inside the hall is already blistering. I drop beside her, slice the white nylon ropes around her elbows and ankles with my steel multi-tool, and haul her upright toward the porch air—half-dragging her down the stone steps into the snow just as the rear kitchen roof rafters collapse inward with a shower of orange sparks.

Sloane drops to her knees beside the SUV fender, coughing hard into the freezing air, her silver-streaked hair plastered with soot. She looks once toward the burning lodge, then back at Maya standing beside the iron gate with the oilskin bundle buttoned tight beneath her flannel shirt. Even half-asphyxiated, Sloane's eyes lock straight onto the rectangular outline of the ledger.

"Clara," Maya calls out, nodding down the dark logging road toward the county line.

Through the dense black spruce corridor below us, twin **blue-and-red strobe bars** flash sharply against the snow-laden boughs, climbing steadily up the unplowed grade behind a heavy county plow blade. Diesel sirens echo faintly off the granite ledges, followed by the deep air-horn blast of a pumper truck rounding the lower switchback.

State police cruisers and county fire rigs—drawn up the ridge by the towering column of diesel smoke visible from the state highway, Pike's abandoned county plow strobe, and the abrupt dead-man cutoff of Sloane's Boston satellite tracker.

Maya slides her cold, ink-stained hand into mine, her knuckles raw from the brick chute yet steady against my split finger. Behind us, Hollis slumps silent against the forged iron spear, while Celeste stares blankly at the burning timber gables. Snowflakes hiss softly against the cooling hood of Sloane's disabled SUV, and the scent of pine smoke drifts down from the woodshed.

I lean my forehead briefly against the cold iron gate pillar, feeling the thin pink scar under my left cuff pulse in the subzero air, and watch the first cruiser headlights sweep through the spruce trees toward us, cutting bright white cones through the drifting snow.

# Chapter 24

Gray Friday dawn breaks over Blackthorne Ridge in a flat wash of freezing sleet and wet pine smoke.

By six-thirty, two county pumper trucks have knocked down the worst of the carriage barn fire, leaving the rear kitchen wing a blackened skeleton of steaming rafters against the snow. Red and blue cruiser strobes pulse steadily across the turnaround, turning the churned ice into bruised purple slush. The sharp smell of wet charcoal and cold diesel clings to every wool coat in the clearing.

I sit on the open tailgate of an Essex County ambulance beside my sabotaged Honda Civic—which a state tow rig hauled up to the turnout twenty minutes ago, its clipped distributor wire dangling loose beneath the popped hood—wrapped in an olive wool blanket while a paramedic cleans the split across my right index finger. My bruised left shoulder aches under the wool, yet every time I glance toward the adjacent stretcher, **Maya** is sitting upright in a clean navy fleece, sipping hot black coffee from a paper cup with her ribs freshly taped. She hasn't let the paper cup out of her left hand since the medics wrapped her chest, as if holding onto something warm proves she's no longer hiding in a freezing attic wall.

Down by the wrought-iron gates, two Vermont State Police troopers unfasten Hollis Greer from the iron spear, clap steel handcuffs over his uninjured right wrist, and load him onto a rolling backboard. His soot-smeared jaw stays locked shut, though his eyes never leave Maya until the ambulance doors swing closed.

Across the plowed apron, **Celeste Vane** sits in the back seat of a state cruiser with her pale wrists cuffed in front of her cashmere turtleneck. A state fire marshal carries the black leather *VaneCor* medical case off the passenger seat of Sloane's SUV in a clear evidence bag, right behind a pair of forensic techs rolling **Arthur Vane's** salt-packed body bag out of the collapsed stone icehouse on a plastic sled.

Time of death pending toxicology, yet the state medical examiner's preliminary freeze-chart puts Arthur dead at least forty-eight hours—hours ahead of Friday's five-o'clock survivorship deadline.

A second cruiser pulls up behind the tow rig, and **Deputy Enoch Pike** steps out of the rear door in county custody, picked up down the logging grade without a coat. His heavy boots drag through the slush as a trooper reads him his rights beside the gate.

"Ms. Vance," a tall woman in a charcoal wool overcoat says, stepping up to the ambulance tailgate with an evidence clipboard. **Assistant U.S. Attorney Nora Halvorsen**, dispatched out of Burlington after Sloane's compliance call triggered a multi-agency response. "The troopers tell me your sister has the original financial ledger from the house. We'll clear your ribs at the hospital first, then take formal chain of custody once my evidence courier arrives."

Maya steps down from the stretcher beside me, keeping the oilskin-wrapped **Caretaker's Ledger** buttoned tight beneath her navy fleece. She looks at me once—silent, steady—waiting for my nod before climbing into the warm ambulance cab.

***

Two hours later, **St. Johnsbury Hospital** smells of antiseptic bleach, damp wool coats, and scorched vending-machine coffee.

At **8:30 AM**, the ER triage bay is quiet behind thin blue privacy curtains. Three neat black nylon sutures close the split across my right index finger, and a clean gauze square covers the scrape along my temple. The thin paper sheet beneath my legs crinkles every time I shift, and my wool socks are drying stiffly beside the radiator grille. Maya sits on the edge of the rolling chair beside my gurney, holding a thick, warm **photocopy packet** of the **Caretaker's Ledger** that Halvorsen's paralegal just ran off at the hospital records desk while we wait for the federal courier to seal the original calfskin book buttoned inside her fleece.

The privacy curtain rings rattle sharply on their aluminum track.

**Sloane Mercer-Hall** steps into the narrow bay. Someone loaned her a dry tan trench coat over her soot-stained trousers, and a small ice pack rests briefly against the plum bruise along her left cheekbone before she drops it into her coat pocket. Even in a borrowed coat with ash smudged into her collar, she carries the quiet authority of a woman who bills by the quarter-hour. Outside in the bright linoleum corridor, a uniformed state trooper stands with his shoulder braced against the doorframe, waiting to escort her to a material-witness interview.

"You look remarkably intact for someone who climbed out of a burning coal chute," Sloane says, keeping her voice pitched low enough that it won't carry past the curtain.

"I had a good guide," I say, pulling the olive wool blanket tighter around my shoulders. "And your SUV still needs a new ignition relay."

Sloane's mouth curves fractionally, without humor. She glances once at Maya's taped ribs, then pulls a folded cream-bond **wire authorization slip** from her trench pocket and lays it on the steel tray table beside my elbow. The heavy bond paper smells faintly of dry ink and Boston espresso.

"Your **forty-five-thousand-dollar** field contract cleared compliance at seven-thirty," Sloane says smoothly. "You produced Arthur Vane's corpse prior to five o'clock, which preserves the thirty-eight-million-dollar bondholder clawback. My firm honors its paper, Clara. Every cent is already in transit to Portland."

"I didn't do it for State Street," I say.

"I know why you did it," Sloane replies. She steps half a pace closer to the gurney, lowering her voice until it barely rises above the hum of the triage heart monitor. "And that brings us to the second signature on page forty-four. Halvorsen hasn't unsealed the federal grand jury packet yet. If you and your sister sign a joint supplemental **consulting addendum** with Mercer-Hall Fiduciary right now—routing the original Caretaker's Ledger through our Boston compliance counsel for internal audit before the U.S. Attorney takes formal custody—I'll wire a **one-hundred-thousand-dollar** retainer bonus to your Portland account before noon."

Maya's ink-stained fingers tighten on the edge of the photocopy packet in her lap. She doesn't look up at Sloane.

I look at the crisp hundred-thousand-dollar figure in my head. Three years ago, after Harbor Mutual buried me in civil court, that kind of money would have wiped out every debt I carried and bought me a clean office on Commercial Street. I wouldn't have spent two winters living out of a silver Honda Civic.

Yet Maya is sitting two feet away with taped ribs and ink-stained knuckles because she walked away from her life at twenty-one rather than sign off on dead children.

"You co-signed Escrow Account `#884-19B` in October 2020," I say flatly. "You paid Hollis Greer a hundred and forty-five thousand dollars to bury three dead children under fake hypothermia autopsies. If we hand that original ledger to your compliance counsel first, page forty-four will vanish into a shredder before lunch."

Sloane doesn't blink. "If that ledger hits a public docket unredacted, Vane Biotech stock craters by morning, federal regulators freeze Mercer-Hall's institutional bond pool, and forty pension funds lose their shirts. Take the hundred thousand, Clara. Your sister gets a clean name, and nobody has to spend three years in federal depositions."

***

I reach past the wire slip on the steel tray table and push the blue privacy curtain wide open.

**AUSA Nora Halvorsen** stands just outside the bay in the fluorescent corridor beside a federal evidence courier holding a brushed-steel transit case and a clipboard stacked with triplicate chain-of-custody receipts.

"Ms. Halvorsen," I call out, my voice carrying clean down the hallway.

Sloane's jaw locks white beneath the plum bruise on her cheekbone, though she doesn't turn around or step back. She keeps her hands folded calmly inside the pockets of her borrowed trench coat while the state trooper beside the doorframe shifts his weight onto his heels.

Maya stands up from the rolling chair, ignoring the sharp wince across her taped ribs, and walks straight past Sloane into the corridor. She unbuttons her navy fleece, draws the oilskin-wrapped **Caretaker's Ledger** clear, and places the original calfskin book directly into Halvorsen's gloved hands. Beside it, I drop two sealed hospital biohazard bags onto the courier's clipboard—the wrapped **crystal brandy stopper** and the **half-charred nitroglycerin rescue-spray canister** from my coat pocket.

"Original paper, unbroken chain of custody," I tell Halvorsen from the gurney. "Page forty-four has the October 2020 escrow authorization. My sister's whistleblower immunity agreement goes on the record before she gives her formal statement."

"Agreed," Halvorsen says. She unwraps a corner of the oilskin just enough to verify the embossed calfskin spine, then slides the ledger into the foam-lined steel transit case. Both twin brass clasps snap shut with a flat metallic click, and her black ballpoint scratches briskly across the yellow receipt copy. "Your sister is under federal protection as of eight o'clock this morning, Ms. Vance. And we'll keep your forty-five-thousand-dollar Mercer-Hall contract invoice on file as a verified investigative expense—nobody's clawing back your earned fee."

She tears off the yellow carbon receipt, stamps the federal courier's evidence log, and lays my copy beside the wire slip on the steel tray table.

Sloane adjusts the collar of her borrowed trench coat, smooths her silver-streaked hair back from her forehead, and steps calmly into the corridor beside the waiting state trooper. She doesn't look angry. She looks like an attorney who has already calculated her next three bail motions and knows which Boston judge sits on the weekend rotation.

Before climbing into the back of the state cruiser idling outside the glass ER doors, Sloane pauses briefly beside the edge of my pulled-back curtain. A cold gust of wet sleet and ambulance diesel slips in from the covered bay. Her gray eyes drop straight to the thick **photocopy packet** of the Caretaker's Ledger resting across Maya's lap.

"Enjoy your forty-five thousand, Clara," Sloane says quietly, her breath misting faintly in the draft off the automatic doors. "You think Arthur Vane was the top of that ledger? Look at the notarized seal on the back cover. Your father's maritime firm audited the first shipment."

She turns cleanly on her heel and walks out through the sliding glass doors toward the idling cruiser, leaving the sharp smell of expensive espresso and dry paper hanging in the bleach-heavy air.

Beside my gurney, Maya slowly flips the warm photocopy packet over to the final sheet, exposing the faint circular impression of an embossed notary seal pressed deep into the calfskin backing.

# Chapter 25

Ten days after the fire on Blackthorne Ridge, December rain hits Portland Harbor sideways off the Casco Bay chop.

I walk the granite seawall along Commercial Street with a paper bag of groceries tucked under my left arm and a cardboard carrier of black coffee balanced in my right hand. Diesel exhaust drifts across the wet cobblestones from the morning island ferry idling at the pier. Overhead, two herring gulls wheel into the salt wind, screaming over the clatter of lobster traps stacked beside the bait shed.

Three days ago, the **forty-five-thousand-dollar** wire from Mercer-Hall Fiduciary cleared into my Portland checking account without a single compliance hold. Yesterday, an urgent-care nurse snipped the three black nylon sutures out of my right index finger, leaving a tight pink seam across the knuckle that still pulls whenever I curl my hand around a steering wheel. My silver Honda Civic sits parked two blocks up the hill with a new distributor harness, fresh winter tires, and zero snow chains in the trunk.

Two nights ago, **AUSA Nora Halvorsen** faxed the signed federal whistleblower immunity order to my lawyer's office on Middle Street, formally vacating the six-year-old fugitive warrant that kept **Maya Vance** off every bank ledger and lease agreement in New England.

I turn up the steep brick slope toward the Old Eastern Promenade, climbing past three-story clapboard triple-deckers with peeling white trim and salt-frosted storm windows. My bruised left shoulder aches under the damp wool of my coat, yet the heavy lead weight that lived behind my ribs for six years—the constant low math of wondering whether my sister was alive under a highway overpass or buried in unmarked pine dirt—is finally gone.

Inside my third-floor apartment, the cast-iron radiator by the front window hisses and clanks against the cold draft.

The narrow hallway smells of damp cedar floorboards, toasted rye bread, and faint eucalyptus liniment from the fresh roll of athletic tape sitting on the bathroom sink. Maya's laced leather boots—scrubbed clean of Vermont mud and soot—stand neatly aligned beside my sneakers on the rubber boot tray. On the brass key hook beside the door hangs a spare set of house keys she hasn't held since college.

I set the paper grocery bag on the butcher-block counter, peel off my wet coat, and check the half-ajar door of the spare bedroom.

For six years, that room held nothing except cardboard bankers' boxes from my father's estate, a folding aluminum cot, and a radiator that rattled through every January gale. Now a real pine bed sits under the east window with crisp flannel sheets, and Maya sleeps curled beneath a thick gray wool quilt with her dark hair spread across a clean cotton pillowcase. Her breathing comes slow and even, without the sharp catch that followed every rib fracture in the Blackthorne attic.

I pull the bedroom door closer to keep the kitchen light off her face, nudge my damp scarf onto the radiator valve to dry, and carry two steaming paper cups of black coffee over to the scarred pine table by the bay window.

***

On the pine tabletop lies the thick **photocopy packet** of the **Caretaker's Ledger**, weighted down by my brass desk lamp and a jeweler's ten-power folding loupe.

Since Halvorsen's evidence courier sealed the original calfskin book inside that brushed-steel transit case in St. Johnsbury, I haven't read past page forty-four again. The Vermont grand jury already has Arthur Vane's salt-packed autopsy, Hollis Greer's forged hypothermia certificates, Celeste Vane's *nitroglycerin* tampering, and Sloane Mercer-Hall's co-signed October 2020 escrow authorization. Federal marshals denied bail for Hollis and Celeste at Monday's arraignment in Burlington. Sloane posted a half-million-dollar surety bond through a Boston law partner and vanished behind three appellate motions before noon.

Yet Sloane's parting line at the St. Johnsbury ER doors has sat in the back of my skull like a pebble inside a boot.

*You think Arthur Vane was the top of that ledger? Look at the notarized seal on the back cover. Your father's maritime firm audited the first shipment.*

I sit down on the wooden chair, click the brass desk lamp on, and angle the shade low so the warm halogen beam rakes almost flat across the final sheet of the photocopy packet.

Halvorsen's paralegal ran the copier on high contrast at the hospital records desk, which washed out half the background grain—yet the heavy circular impression pressed into the original calfskin back cover still catches the raking light in faint gray arcs.

I unfold the steel loupe, brace my elbows on the pine grain, and lean close over the page.

At first glance, the arc looks like a standard Massachusetts corporate notary ring—serrated outer border, sans-serif block capitals, a two-digit year code notched above the center line. Under ten-power glass, the outer ring resolves letter by letter where the original steel embosser bit deep into the leather:

`QUARANTINE STATION NO. 4 — BARROW ISLAND, R.I.`

My grip tightens around the cold brass rim of the loupe.

Barrow Island isn't in Vermont. It sits three miles off the Rhode Island coast in lower Narragansett Bay—a windswept granite reef holding a defunct turn-of-the-century maritime quarantine hospital that the state decommissioned in the late eighties and sold off to a private shell trust.

I reach across the table to the bottom shelf of my bookcase, pull out a battered blue **Harbor Mutual** civil discovery binder from three years ago, and flip past the burnt warehouse photographs from Portland's Commercial Street wharf. Halfway through the cargo manifests, tabbed in faded yellow plastic, sits the original **salvage bill of lading** that got me blackballed by every insurance underwriter in Maine.

Three years ago, when the Commercial Street warehouse burned to the waterline, Harbor Mutual's forensic adjuster swore the manifest held nothing except surplus textile looms and damp wool batting. When I refused to sign the payout clearance because the pallet weights didn't match the harbor master's crane logs—crated refrigeration compressors billed as dry cotton yarn, offloaded at low tide without a customs seal—the firm buried me under a fraudulent-interference suit. They stripped my license, froze my escrow bond, and wiped out every dime I owned before the charred pilings cooled.

I lay the yellowed Harbor Mutual manifest flat beside the ledger photocopy and shift the loupe from one sheet to the other. The paper still carries the faint, sour tang of wet wharf ash.

There, stamped in faint violet ink along the lower-left margin of the Portland warehouse manifest, is the exact same octagonal port-clearance code: `B.I.Q.S.-04`.

Barrow Island Quarantine Station.

I swing the desk lamp closer, squinting through the glass at the faded inner ring of the notary seal on the Caretaker's Ledger cover. Beneath the Rhode Island station name, crookedly over-stamped in tiny serif capitals, sit three letters and a registration number that stop the breath in my chest:

`V.M.C. — AUDIT 1998.`

**Vance Maritime & Cargo.**

My father's old marine surveying firm—the same three-man office on Custom House Wharf that went under when he died of a coronary in my second year of college. I still keep his brass parallel ruler and cracked leather logbook in the hall closet. He spent twenty years auditing cold-storage cargo manifests up and down the Atlantic coast, and his signature sat on the opening shipment before Arthur Vane ever bought a single pine acre on Blackthorne Ridge.

***

Wooden floorboards creak behind me in the narrow hallway.

I set the loupe down beside the Harbor Mutual binder without looking up right away, waiting for the cold knot between my shoulder blades to loosen.

**Maya** steps into the kitchen wearing one of my oversized flannel shirts over gray sweatpants, her left arm tucked lightly across the white athletic tape wrapping her ribs. Dark circles still shadow the skin beneath her eyes, yet the hunted set around her jaw has softened since St. Johnsbury. She carries a small, dented **steel lockbox** under her good arm—its corners scraped raw down to bright metal, its hinged lid held shut by a **rusted brass padlock** thick with white sea-salt corrosion.

"The coffee's still hot," I say, nudging the second paper cup across the pine table toward her.

Maya doesn't sit down right away. She looks from the opened Harbor Mutual binder to the raking desk lamp angled across the ledger photocopy, then straight at my face.

"You found the Barrow Island stamp," she says quietly.

"My father audited the first shipment in ninety-eight," I say, tapping the faint `V.M.C.` ring with the tip of my scarred index finger. "Same station code that sat on the Harbor Mutual warehouse manifest before the wharf burned in Portland. Sloane wasn't bluffing at the hospital."

Maya sets the dented steel box onto the pine table between our coffee cups. The rusted brass padlock hits the wood with a dry metallic thud, leaving a faint scatter of gray salt grit across the grain. Smells of wet iron, mildew, and old basement concrete rise off the dented lid.

"When Hollis torched my Burlington apartment six years ago," Maya says, pulling out the opposite wooden chair and easing herself down carefully to favor her ribs, "I didn't grab just the *VaneCor* trial binders. I had a rented storage locker out on Shelburne Road that Arthur didn't know about. Troopers cleared the seal on it yesterday morning."

She reaches into her flannel shirt pocket, pulls out a tiny **tarnished brass key** tied to a frayed length of red baker's twine, and lays it on top of the lockbox lid.

"I told you in the St. Johnsbury ER that I ran because of the pediatric fatalities," Maya says, sliding the heavy steel box toward my hands until its front edge bumps the Harbor Mutual binder. "That was half of it. Look at the notary ring again, Clara. I didn't disappear six years ago just because of Vane Biotech."

She keeps two fingers pressed against the rusted brass padlock, holding my eyes steady across the steam rising off the black coffee.

"I disappeared because the man who framed you in Portland was looking for what's inside this box."

Outside the bay window, the morning ferry horn blows a long, low blast across the gray harbor chop, shaking the frosted glass in its wooden sash. I wrap my scarred right hand around the cold brass padlock, feel the rough salt crust bite into my palm, and reach for the tarnished key beside my thumb.
