# The Caretaker's Ledger
*A Tessa Calder Thriller (Book 2) — By David Seelmann*

---

## Chapter 1

Tire chains bite hard into packed mountain ice, rattling up through the floorboards of my 2014 silver Honda Civic with every switchback.

Outside my salted windshield, the Northeast Kingdom of Vermont looks nothing like the Maine coast. There is no open water here, no foghorns groaning across a harbor channel, and no tide clock counting down the hours. Instead, towering black spruce crowd both edges of the unplowed logging road, their boughs bent low under fresh snow that swallows headlights ten paces ahead.

On the passenger seat beside me sits my faded navy canvas duffel, zipped tight over forty-five thousand dollars' worth of new nondisclosure paperwork from Kiefer-Hall Fiduciary. Tucked into the cup holder, resting atop the folded copy of my restored Maine private investigator bond, is the heavy brass skeleton key Margot Kiefer-Hall slid across the table at Becky's Diner this morning.

Even inside the heated cab, the key smells faintly of cold iron and pine resin.

My left wrist still itches beneath my wool cuff where the Salt Point stitches came out, a thin pink ridge against pale skin. Seven hours ago, I had a ten o'clock interview lined up on Congress Street for a daylight desk job auditing flooded boat hulls. Safe work. Clean chain-of-custody. Zero locked rooms.

Then Margot flipped a glossy photograph over on the diner Formica and showed me three words written in my younger sister's cramped, back-slanted cursive in the corner of a mountain guest ledger:

*Don't trust him.*

Six years. Seventy-two months since Nina walked out of a winter internship in Burlington and vanished off every bank grid, tax roll, and credit bureau in New England. I ruined my savings and half my twenties chasing cold tips across three states before the Harbor Mutual lawsuit buried me for good. I told myself I had accepted the silence.

One ink stroke proved I was lying.

Ahead, through a break in the timber, wrought-iron gates loom out of the drifting snow. They match the photograph in my coat pocket—tall forged spears flanked by rough fieldstone pillars, guarding the private drive to Cragmont Lodge. According to Margot's briefing packet, pharmaceutical heir Arthur Cutler, his second wife, and their live-in winter caretaker walked out of the lodge during Tuesday night's blizzard and left three running snowmobiles idling by the woodshed. Nobody has filed a missing-persons report yet. The family trustees in Boston want the house inventoried and cleared of private papers before the county sheriff asks why the snowmobiles ran out of gas.

I ease my foot onto the brake pedal and bring the Civic to a halt in front of the iron gates.

My headlights sweep across the drift piled against the lower crossbars.

I don't reach for the gate latch. I sit still behind the wheel, heater fan clicking softly in the dash, staring down at the snow between the stone pillars.

Margot told me the private road had been drifted shut since Tuesday night's storm. She said the state plow stops at the county line three miles back, and nobody from the Boston office had been able to reach the mountain.

Yet two deep, wide tire ruts cut straight through the powder beneath the iron gates—fresh enough that the waffle tread of heavy mud-terrain tires hasn't filled in with blowing snow.

Someone drove a heavy truck up this mountain within the last hour. And judging by the single set of tracks pointing uphill, they haven't driven back down.

***

I kill the Civic's headlights, leave the car tucked in the dark spruce turnout outside the pillars, and slip through the narrow pedestrian gap beside the stone post with my duffel slung over one shoulder.

Cold air bites into my lungs like crushed glass, carrying fresh birch smoke, sharp and resinous, down from the ridge above.

My boots crunch softly in the truck ruts as I climb the final bend of the drive.

Cragmont Lodge rises against the gray twilight sky like a stone fortress wedged into the granite mountainside. Steep timber gables sag under thick white drifts. Every window across the three-story front elevation stands dark, heavy interior timber shutters latched tight behind frosted panes. The turnaround in front of the wide stone porch is empty—no pickup truck in sight.

I follow the fresh tire ruts with my eyes. They don't stop at the front steps. They curve past the main house toward a timber carriage barn fifty paces back in the trees, where a pair of double barn doors stands padlocked from the outside, fresh snow kicked loose around the threshold.

Whoever drove up here parked their truck inside the barn and locked it behind them.

I step onto the covered porch, keeping my weight near the stone wall where the hemlock planks don't creak. In my right pocket, my fingers wrap around my steel multi-tool. In my left hand, I hold the heavy brass skeleton key from Margot's briefcase.

I slide the resin-stained bit into the oversized iron mortise lock on the front oak door.

The wards turn without a scrape. Oiled recently.

I push the heavy door open a crack and slip inside, easing the latch shut behind my heel until the bolt clicks home.

The great hall is cavernous and dim, lit only by pale snow-glare filtering through high clerestory glass near the rafters. Mounted stag heads stare down from dark timber walls above a massive fieldstone fireplace. Across the room, a wide oak staircase climbs toward a shadowed balcony.

The air inside isn't freezing. It's warm.

I walk slowly toward the hearth. No flames burn on the iron grate, but a thick bed of gray birch ash glows dull orange at the center, radiating steady heat across the flagstone floor. Beneath the birch smoke hangs a second smell—greasy, chemical, and unmistakable to anyone who ever investigated an arson claim.

Lamp kerosene. And burnt bond paper.

Someone used the hearth to ash a stack of files less than twenty minutes ago.

To my right, beside the archway leading to the study, stands a slanted oak reception lectern—the old-fashioned kind built for nineteenth-century hunting clubs. Resting open across its brass lip is a thick, maroon-leather guest ledger.

I cross the flagstones, keeping my boots quiet on the slate.

I recognize the cream, red-ruled paper from the cropped fax printout taped to the back of Margot's photograph. Same faded header print. Same column lines for *Date*, *Guest*, and *Room*.

Except when I look down at the open ledger under the gray clerestory light, my breath catches in my throat.

The top sheet hasn't been turned. It has been sliced clean out along the leather spine with a straight-edged blade, leaving a jagged fringe of cream paper still glued into the binding.

Only the bottom right corner of the missing page tore loose before the blade finished its cut—a tiny triangular scrap pinned beneath a heavy brass desk weight. On that scrap sits the tail of a single cursive letter *m* in dark blue ink, back-slanted. Nina's ink.

And resting directly on top of the sliced binding, where the wet glue is still tacky to the touch, sits a freshly stamped brass room tag on a steel ring.

**ROOM 4 — ATTIC EAST.**

Above my head, directly across the third-floor rafters where the attic corridor runs, a heavy floorboard groans under the slow, deliberate shift of a boot.

Then an iron slide bolt grates home on the upper stairwell door—and behind me on the front porch, a heavy brass padlock clanks shut across the outer storm door.

## Chapter 2

The key turns twice in the upper stairwell lock, iron scraping dry brass, and then the third-floor rafters fall silent.

I don't move. My boots stay planted on the cold flagstones beside the oak lectern while my lungs pull in thin, kerosene-tainted air. Overhead, the heavy floorboard doesn't groan again. Whoever stands on the attic landing isn't pacing. They're listening.

If they locked the stairwell door from above, they didn't lock me *into* the Great Hall—they locked themselves *out* of it, or sealed the third floor against me. Either way, the front oak door behind my back is still my way out. I pivot slowly toward the porch threshold, keeping my heels off the stone so my snow-damp treads don't squeak.

My left hand still grips Margot's brass skeleton key. My right fingers close around the cold steel frame of my multi-tool inside my coat pocket. Before I cross the hall, I glance back at the lectern. If I leave the torn corner scrap and the brass room tag sitting on the wet glue, anyone who comes downstairs will know I stood right here and read their work. I peel the freshly stamped **ROOM 4 — ATTIC EAST** tag off the tacky binding—a thin strand of hide glue stretches and snaps—and slip both the brass tag and the tiny triangular scrap of Nina's ink into my inside breast pocket.

I cross the flagstones toward the front oak door and test the heavy iron thumb-turn. It slides smoothly. I pull the inward-swinging oak slab open half a hand's breadth—and freeze.

Beyond the oak threshold sits the heavy timber-and-iron storm door I passed on the porch, designed to swing outward against mountain drifts. Right now, its iron latch bar is jammed tight from the exterior porch side. Through the narrow crack between the two doors, freezing wind whistles across my knuckles, carrying fresh birch smoke and the unmistakable metallic click of a steel padlock hasp swinging against wood.

Someone didn't just lock the third-floor stairs. While I stood staring at the lectern, a second person—or the man who parked that truck in the carriage barn—slipped onto the porch and padlocked the outer storm door behind me. I shove my shoulder against the inner oak door just to check if the storm latch has play, though the iron frame holds solid without giving a hair.

I ease the inner oak door shut and throw the thumb-turn back into place before the wind rattles the glass and announces where I'm standing.

Trapped inside. Just like Salt Point, except there is no ocean glass to smash here—only thick granite walls and heavy interior timber shutters bolted tight over every ground-floor window. Even the high clerestory panes near the rafters sit too far above the stone floor to reach without a rigging ladder.

I shift the strap of my navy duffel higher on my shoulder, feeling the stiff leather spine of Margot's nondisclosure portfolio press against my ribs. Four walls. Two staircases—one grand oak flight rising straight across the Great Hall to the second-floor gallery, and whatever service stairs hide behind the kitchen archway.

If the person upstairs wanted me dead in the next thirty seconds, they'd be coming down those stairs with a rifle. Instead, they locked the top door. Why lock yourself upstairs unless you're guarding something up there—or buying time to clear out through another exit?

I cross the Great Hall along the shadowed wall beneath the mounted stag heads, avoiding the dull orange glow of the birch embers on the hearth. The flagstones give way to dark hardwood planks beside the study archway, and to my left, a narrow paneled door stands ajar beneath the curve of the main staircase.

The hall mudroom.

I push the door wide with my elbow, holding my breath against the stale tang of cedar mothballs.

***

No overhead bulb burns in the mudroom, so I pull the small penlight from my coat pocket and cup my fingers over the lens to keep the beam narrow and low.

The space is cramped. Iron hooks line the cedar wall. Three heavy waxed-canvas parkas hang undisturbed along the pegs, their brass snaps dull with tarnish. Beneath the bench, pairs of rubber chore boots sit neatly aligned on a galvanized drip tray. Nobody has tracked snow in here for days.

My light sweeps the corner umbrella stand—and stops.

A heavy walnut walking cane fitted with a custom, oversized rubber ferrule leans upright against the cedar wainscoting. The dark wood handle is worn pale along the *left* side of the curve where a palm leaned hard against it, and a brass collar halfway down the shaft bears an engraved monogram: *A.C.* Beside it sits a small brass tin of orthopedic heel wax, its lid crusted with dried yellow balm.

Beside the cane sits a pair of tall, shearling-lined leather snow boots. I crouch and run one bare knuckle across the leather toes and rubber treads. Bone-dry. Not a speck of melted snow, salt crust, or pine needle sits trapped in the lug pattern. I straighten slowly, clicking the penlight off in my palm.

Margot's briefing packet claimed pharmaceutical heir Arthur Cutler, his second wife, and their winter caretaker walked out into Tuesday night's blizzard and rode three snowmobiles off the ridge. Maybe Arthur didn't ride a snowmobile. Maybe he only walked to the woodshed—except even on flat floorboards, a man who wears down the walnut grip of a custom orthopedic cane doesn't hike into a whiteout wearing thin house slippers while leaving his shearling snow boots dry in the mudroom.

I step back out into the dim Great Hall and test the study archway next. The tall mahogany doors stand closed, their brass lever handles cold under my thumb. Locked. I crouch and peer through the keyhole—pitch black, no key resting on the inside, and no draft moving beneath the threshold.

Across the hall, the wide oak staircase climbs into shadow toward the second-floor balcony. Above that, invisible from the flagstones, sits the third-floor landing where the key turned.

If I wait down here in the dark, whoever is upstairs controls the high ground, the phone lines, and every exterior shutter. My chest feels tight, the familiar cold knot sitting behind my sternum—the same trap I swore off in Portland this morning before Margot slid Nina's cursive across the Formica. I can't walk out. The outer storm door is padlocked from the porch, and Nina's ink is still fresh on that torn corner in my pocket.

I sling my duffel off my shoulder and tuck it deep behind the heavy velvet drapes flanking the study archway where it won't slow me down on the stairs. My boots hug the wall stringer where oak treads carry the most support as I start up the main staircase.

Every step tastes like dust and cold varnish. Halfway up, the muffled hum of a diesel generator vibrates faintly through the floorboards beneath my soles—a steady, low thrum coming from somewhere deep below the kitchen wing. That explains why the hall felt warm despite the blizzard outside. Somebody is burning fuel to keep the pipes from freezing, or to keep one specific wing livable.

I reach the second-floor gallery landing. A wide corridor stretches left and right, lined with dark oil portraits and closed bedroom doors. No light leaks under any of them. At the far east end of the gallery, a narrower enclosed staircase angles sharply upward behind a wrought-iron handrail.

No carpet covers those upper treads. Just bare pine boards leading straight up to a heavy oak-and-iron fire door at the top.

I climb slowly, placing the ball of each boot near the riser so the old pine doesn't pop under my weight. When I reach the top landing, the smell hits me before my hand touches the brass hardware—lamp kerosene and heavy gun oil.

***

I shield my penlight between two fingers and flick the beam onto the fire door's mortise deadbolt plate.

The brass escutcheon gleams clean against the dark oak, wiped recently enough that no dust coats the bevel. Fresh oil glistens in the keyhole slot—the same slickness I felt when Margot's skeleton key slid into the front porch lock without a scrape.

Then I tilt the narrow beam sideways across the polished brass plate just above the cylinder.

A dull, greasy smudge breaks the shine. Not a smeared palm print, and not the clean vertical wipe of a rag. Whoever braced their hand against this plate while turning the key left a distinct, three-pad impression in pine resin and gun oil: index finger, middle finger, and pinky.

Where the ring finger pad should press against the brass, there is only a smooth, uninterrupted sheen of clean metal.

I hold the light steady for two seconds, tracing the spacing of the three oily ridges. Left-handed writers smudge ink from the heel of the palm; right-handed mechanics brace the cylinder with their right thumb on the knob and their right fingertips spread flat against the upper plate. The missing pad belongs to a right ring finger—either bandaged stiff, or missing its tip entirely.

I click the penlight off and slip it back into my coat. Then I pull Margot's brass skeleton key from my left pocket. The front door took it without a fight, oiled by the same hand. If Cragmont Lodge runs on old nineteenth-century mortise wards, one master bit might throw this upper deadbolt too.

I slide the brass bit gently into the oiled keyhole. It stops halfway in—blocked inside the cylinder by the snapped-off steel tip of a broken key wedged tight against the tumbler.

Whoever threw this deadbolt from the stairwell side broke their key off in the lock—then slipped down the back stairs to padlock the front porch door.

I lean close, pressing my ear directly against the cold oak panel, holding my breath until the blood thumps loud in my eardrums.

On the far side of the door, pine floorboards creak—a quick, light shift of weight moving away from the threshold toward the east gable. Not heavy winter treads. Lighter leather boots, moving fast in the dark.

Then the deep, steady vibration of the basement generator beneath my boots stutters twice—and dies completely.

The hall plunges into dead, suffocating silence. No furnace hum. No distant relay click. Even the faint draft along the floorboards stops as the blower fans coast to a halt.

Before I can pull back from the oak panel, a sharp metallic hiss erupts inside the plaster wall right beside my left shoulder—a taut steel cable racing downward through a hidden wooden shaft at full drop.

## Chapter 3

The dumbwaiter cable slams against the wooden guide rollers somewhere below the second floor, then stops with a dull thud of hemp rope slackening inside the wall.

I pull back from the third-floor fire door and descend the bare pine stairs without turning on my penlight. Darkness wraps the gallery corridor thick enough to press against my eyelids. No furnace blower hums beneath the floorboards now. Without the generator, the lodge settles into the cold creak of timber rafters contracting in the subzero drop outside.

At the east end of the gallery, a narrow service door stands half-latched beside a linen press. I push it open with my knuckles. Steep, pie-shaped back stairs spiral downward behind the main chimney stack, thick with the smell of cold bacon grease.

I take the treads sideways, one hand trailing the rough plaster wall to keep my balance. If the person behind the third-floor fire door didn't just drop that dumbwaiter car by accident—if somebody else is riding the shafts or sending cargo down from the attic—they're moving faster than I am.

At the bottom of the service flight, a swinging baize door opens into a cavernous commercial kitchen. Pale snow-glare leaks weakly through a small frosted pane set high in the rear porch door, enough to outline a long butcher-block island, copper stockpots hanging from an iron rack, and a double-door stainless refrigerator standing dead and silent.

To my right sits the butler's pantry.

The narrow wooden door to the dumbwaiter shaft hangs unlatched, a thin draft of warm air rising out of the dark vertical box. I pull my penlight, cup three fingers around the bezel, and shine a sliver of light down into the maple car resting at waist height.

Empty—almost.

In the back corner of the wooden tray sits a small, uncapped amber glass bottle with a stiff hog-bristle brush resting across its rim. I lean close without touching the glass. Sharp, animal-protein stink rises off the amber liquid—hot hide glue, still faintly warm from being carried against someone's coat pocket.

Beside the bottle lies a fresh crescent smudge of dark-blue fountain ink on the maple lip, no wider than a fingernail. I reach higher into the dark shaft and run two fingers along the hemp hoist rope. Warmth lingers on the braided fibers, and a faint smear of pine turpentine clings to the wood guide rail where someone braced a hand on the descent.

The man who oiled the upper deadbolt threw that lock from the stairwell side, snapped a key tip into the cylinder, and went down to padlock the front porch door. Meanwhile, whoever was penned behind that attic door dropped straight down this service shaft two minutes ago—leaving behind a still-warm bottle of hide glue from earlier when they pinned the `ROOM 4 — ATTIC EAST` tag to the guest ledger.

Two people are moving inside Cragmont Lodge. One of them is wearing heavy winter boots and burning paper in the hearth. The other is using the walls—and left me a key tag written in Nina's ink.

I click the penlight off and ease the pantry door shut until the wooden latch catches.

***

I cross the dark kitchen back toward the Great Hall archway, keeping the butcher block between my ribs and the frosted rear porch window.

Back in the main hall, the birch embers in the fieldstone hearth have dimmed to a faint cherry crust beneath gray ash. No boots sound overhead.

I pull Margot's heavy brass skeleton key from my left pocket and kneel beside the locked mahogany doors of Arthur's study. If the same nineteenth-century mortise wards run through the ground floor, the master bit should throw this bolt too.

The brass wards slide in cleanly. No key blocks the far side here. I turn my wrist slowly, bracing the mahogany handle with my forearm so the bolt doesn't snap loud across the flagstones, and ease the door inward.

Stale cigarillo smoke hits my face. Clove tobacco, bitter and dry, hangs trapped inside a room sealed since Tuesday's storm. I step inside, close the door until the latch whispers shut, and flick my penlight low across the carpet.

Arthur's walnut desk dominates the center of the room. Its green leather blotter sits swept clean except for a heavy square brass paperweight—an exact twin to the one pinning Nina's torn ledger corner out in the Great Hall. Tall built-in bookshelves line the wall behind the desk, packed tight with bound medical journals and thick county land-survey binders. Against the east plaster hangs a framed, hand-inked topographical survey of the Cragmont ridge.

I cross to the map, shielding the penlight beam against the glass. The heavy oak frame sits slightly crooked on its brass wall cleats, leaving a pale dust-free shadow along the plaster where someone swung it aside recently—probably to reach a recessed wall safe hidden behind the backing board. I don't pry the frame open now. No time to crack a dial safe with someone breathing upstairs.

Instead, I trace the survey's crisp surveyor's ink: the main lodge, the carriage barn fifty paces northwest in the spruce, the woodshed bay, and a half-buried granite rectangle tucked into the hillside directly behind the kitchen wing, labeled **ICEHOUSE — 1904**. A dotted red line runs straight from the back wall of that icehouse into the lodge's sub-basement boiler room.

*Coal & Ash Chute.*

I memorize the layout, then drop to one knee beside the small cast-iron hearth set into the study's north wall. Cold soot, untouched for weeks. Whoever burned bond paper tonight didn't use the study grate—they used the massive fieldstone fireplace out in the Great Hall.

I step back to the walnut desk and try the center drawer. Locked. I pull my steel multi-tool, pop the narrow flathead blade, and slide it into the brass gap above the lip just far enough to feel the bolt resistance. No give. Heavy mortise lock, not a cheap desk catch.

On top of the mahogany apothecary cabinet against the far wall sits a small brass tray holding three loose items: a silver cigarillo cutter, a crumpled Boston pharmacy receipt dated Monday, and a half-empty prescription vial of *Colchicine*—gout medication prescribed to Arthur Cutler. The cabinet doors below stand bolted with a miniature brass padlock, the wood around the hasp scarred by fresh pry marks.

Acute gout in his left foot. Dry shearling boots in the mudroom. A custom walnut cane left behind in the stand.

Arthur Cutler never walked out into Tuesday night's blizzard.

I slip out of the study, relock the mahogany door with the skeleton key so nobody spots a broken latch, and move silently toward the Great Hall hearth.

***

The fieldstone fireplace still radiates dry heat against my shins.

I kneel on the slate apron, ignoring the grit biting through my wool trousers, and wrap my gloved hand around the iron handle of the small brass ash shovel leaning against the stone jamb. The glowing birch coals sit mounded in the center of the grate, ringed by pale gray ash and curled black ribbons of burnt paper.

Most of the stack reduced to weightless carbon. Even a breath stirs the flakes into gray dust.

I slip the flat blade of the shovel carefully beneath the cooler ash along the *left* edge of the grate, where the air draft from the chimney flue pushes unburned corners against the firebrick. I lift a shallow scoop onto the cold hearth slate and spread the warm gray powder apart with the tip of my multi-tool.

Two blackened edges crumble at a touch. Beneath them lies a folded corner of heavy bond paper, singed brown along the crease where tight folding starved the flame of oxygen.

I pinch the unburned corner between my thumb and forefinger and tilt it toward the faint orange glow of the coals, clicking my penlight on for one second.

Stamped purple ink crosses the top margin: **CUTLER BIOTECH — CLINICAL AUDIT & DISBURSEMENT VOUCHER.** Below the header sits a partial ledger column—*Batch CC-09 / Pediatric Mortality Transfer*—and a handwritten dollar figure cut off halfway through the digits: **$145,000.00**.

The rest of the sheet ends in brittle charcoal.

I click the penlight off and fold the scorched voucher corner inside my breast pocket beside Nina's torn ledger scrap and the **ROOM 4** brass tag.

Someone drove a heavy mud-terrain pickup up this mountain within the last hour, parked inside the carriage barn, padlocked the outer front storm door behind me, and burned *Cutler Biotech* clinical payout vouchers in this hearth using lamp kerosene. Meanwhile, a second person—carrying warm hide glue in the pantry dumbwaiter—sliced Nina's page out of the guest ledger, left me a directional tag for **ROOM 4 — ATTIC EAST**, and dropped back down the wall before the generator died.

If the person in heavy boots locked the third-floor fire door from *above* while I stood at the lectern, how did they get downstairs to padlock the front storm door? Unless the man who parked in the barn and padlocked the storm door *wasn't* the person on the third floor—or the person upstairs climbed down an exterior fire escape or service stair after sealing the attic.

No. Stop guessing and check the kitchen exit.

I slip quietly back through the dark archway toward the commercial kitchen. If the front storm door is padlocked from the porch, the rear kitchen porch door might still open from the inside—and if I can reach the carriage barn or my Civic in the spruce turnout, I can grab my spare tire iron and check what's sitting behind those padlocked barn doors.

Halfway across the kitchen flagstones, my boot heel catches a dry grain of spilled kosher salt near the butcher block.

I freeze.

Outside on the rear porch, crisp snow crunches under heavy, deliberate boots. Not climbing stairs. Stepping straight onto the back wooden landing.

A gloved fist slams hard against the frosted glass pane of the kitchen door, rattling the wood in its frame.

I drop low behind the butcher block—too late. A blinding halogen flashlight beam stabs straight through the frosted pane, pinning my silhouette against the copper pots behind the island.

Through the glass, a broad-shouldered man in a snow-crusted waxed parka raises a double-barreled shotgun until the twin steel muzzles press flat against the frosty pane.

"Put the brass key on the butcher block, lady," he barks through the wood, his voice thick and gravelly, "or I blow the latch off."

## Chapter 4

I don't reach for my multi-tool. Twin twelve-gauge muzzles sit an inch from frosted glass, and men holding shotguns in the dark don't wait for explanations.

I rise slowly from behind the butcher block with both empty palms raised beside my ears. The halogen beam blinds me for two heartbeats before dipping toward the maple island. I reach into my left coat pocket with two fingers, lift Margot's heavy brass skeleton key by the bow, and lay it flat on the butcher block.

"Boston fiduciary office," I say, keeping my voice flat and dry, the same clerk cadence I used to bore insurance adjusters in Portland. "Margot Kiefer-Hall sent me up to catalog the estate before the county probate clerk arrives. Check the leather portfolio in my duffel if you want."

The man outside doesn't lower the barrel. Behind the glass, his silhouette shifts sideways as a heavy key ring jingles against his hip. A bolt snaps back inside the rear kitchen door. Cold air rushes across my ankles as he kicks the timber slab inward and steps over the threshold, filling the doorway in a snow-crusted waxed parka and size-eleven Vibram lug boots.

Up close, he reeks of wintergreen smokeless tobacco and lamp kerosene. Deep weathered lines bracket his mouth beneath a salt-and-pepper beard trimmed short against his face. He's pushing fifty, broad through the shoulders, and his left thumb rests steady across the shotgun's top tang safety.

"I'm Hollis Greer," he says, his eyes flicking from my wet snow boots to the brass key on the island. "Winter caretaker. Who gave you a master key to the front mortise?"

"Margot Kiefer-Hall. State Street." I nod toward the Great Hall archway without dropping my hands. "My navy duffel is behind the study curtains. I hid it when the stairs locked overhead."

Hollis spits a thin dark stream of tobacco juice into the stainless kitchen sink beside the door, then steps past me—keeping the shotgun muzzle angled toward my knees—and snatches the brass skeleton key off the butcher block with his gloved right hand.

"You shouldn't be in here," he mutters, shoving the key into his parka pocket. "Generator threw a fuel line an hour ago, and some local trespasser broke into the east gable earlier today. I locked the third-floor fire door from the landing to pen him up and snapped a broken key tip into the ward until I could fetch my twelve-gauge from the barn, then went down the back stairs and padlocked the front storm door so nobody else wandered in."

A smooth lie. Too smooth. If he locked the front storm door to keep people out, he did it *after* I was already standing at the lectern—and he didn't explain why his heavy boots were still creaking on the rafters before I even crossed the Great Hall.

"I'm not here to argue with your security," I say, letting a trace of tired irritation leak into my voice. "I'm a records clerk on a flat ten-day fee. If there's a trespasser in the attic, call the sheriff from your truck. Otherwise, point me to the guest wing and show me the three snowmobiles so I can log the property status and sleep."

Hollis studies my face in the pale snow-glare from the door. For a second, I think he's going to march me straight out to the porch at gunpoint. Instead, he clicks the shotgun safety on with a sharp metallic snick and rests the barrels across his crooked left forearm.

"Show me that Boston paperwork first," he says. "Then I'll show you the woodshed."

***

Ten minutes later, Hollis stands beside the Great Hall hearth while a single battery lantern he pulled from his coat illuminates the leather *Kiefer-Hall Fiduciary* portfolio in my lap. He doesn't touch the hearth ash. He doesn't glance at the sliced guest ledger on the lectern either, though the faint smell of lamp kerosene still hangs thick around the fireplace stones.

He reads Margot's signed estate-archiving authorization twice, his gloved thumb smudging the embossed State Street seal, then hands the folder back without a word.

"Ten days," he says, his voice dropping low. "You stay out of the third floor until I clear that attic lock in daylight, and you keep clear of the carriage barn. Snow's too deep by the woodshed anyway."

"My briefing says Arthur Cutler, his wife, and the caretaker left three snowmobiles idling by the woodshed Tuesday night," I say, zipping the portfolio back inside my navy duffel. I sling the bag over my shoulder—no chance I'm leaving Margot's contract out of my sight again—and follow him back toward the kitchen. "If you're the caretaker, why did the Boston trustees think you walked out with them?"

Hollis pauses at the kitchen threshold, his broad back blocking the porch light. "Because I took my pickup down to the county line Tuesday afternoon to buy shearling blankets before the whiteout hit. Bridge washed out below the ridge, so I couldn't get back up until an hour ago. Somebody else took those sleds—or tried to."

Another clean cover story. He even accounted for the fresh mud-terrain truck ruts I saw under the iron gates when I arrived at dusk.

"Then I still need serial numbers off the sleds for the fiduciary inventory," I tell him, stepping onto the back porch ahead of his lantern. "Five minutes in the cold, and I'm out of your hair."

Freezing air hits my face like dry needles, sharp and still beneath a heavy pewter sky. The snow has stopped falling, leaving the ridge buried under deep white drifts that swallow every sound past the porch eaves.

Hollis leads me twenty paces behind the kitchen wing toward a low timber woodshed crouched beneath snow-laden black spruce. Beside the woodshed's double doors, three sleek black-and-silver Arctic Cat snowmobiles sit lined up side by side in the drift, their windshields crusted with Tuesday's frozen sleet.

Hollis holds the battery lantern high, throwing stark shadows across the cowlings. "There they are. Keys still in the ignitions. Tanks bone-dry when I checked them at five."

On the pine tabletop lies the thick **photocopy packet** of the **Caretaker's Ledger**, weighted down by my brass desk lamp and a jeweler's ten-power folding loupe.

Since Halvorsen's evidence courier sealed the original calfskin book inside that brushed-steel transit case in St. Johnsbury, I haven't read past page forty-four again. The Vermont grand jury already has Arthur Cutler's salt-packed autopsy, Hollis Greer's forged hypothermia certificates, Simone Cutler's *nitroglycerin* tampering, and Margot Kiefer-Hall's co-signed October 2020 escrow authorization. Federal marshals denied bail for Hollis and Simone at Monday's arraignment in Burlington. Margot posted a half-million-dollar surety bond through a Boston law partner and vanished behind three appellate motions before noon.

Yet Margot's parting line at the St. Johnsbury ER doors has sat in the back of my skull like a pebble inside a boot.

*You think Arthur Cutler was the top of that ledger? Look at the notarized seal on the back cover. Your father's maritime firm audited the first shipment.*

I sit down on the wooden chair, click the brass desk lamp on, and angle the shade low so the warm halogen beam rakes almost flat across the final sheet of the photocopy packet.

Halvorsen's paralegal ran the copier on high contrast at the hospital records desk, which washed out half the background grain—yet the heavy circular impression pressed into the original calfskin back cover still catches the raking light in faint gray arcs.

I unfold the steel loupe, brace my elbows on the pine grain, and lean close over the page.

At first glance, the arc looks like a standard Massachusetts corporate notary ring—serrated outer border, sans-serif block capitals, a two-digit year code notched above the center line. Under ten-power glass, the outer ring resolves letter by letter where the original steel embosser bit deep into the leather:

`QUARANTINE STATION NO. 4 — BARROW ISLAND, R.I.`

My grip tightens around the cold brass rim of the loupe.

Barrow Island isn't in Vermont. It sits three miles off the Rhode Island coast in lower Narragansett Bay—a windswept granite reef holding a defunct turn-of-the-century maritime quarantine hospital that the state decommissioned in the late eighties and sold off to a private shell trust.

I reach across the table to the bottom shelf of my bookcase, pull out a battered blue **Harbor Mutual** civil discovery binder from three years ago, and flip past the burnt warehouse photographs from Portland's Commercial Street wharf. Halfway through the cargo manifests, tabbed in faded yellow plastic, sits the original **salvage bill of lading** that got me blackballed by every insurance underwriter in Maine.

Three years ago, when the Commercial Street warehouse burned to the waterline, Harbor Mutual's forensic adjuster swore the manifest held nothing except surplus textile looms and damp wool batting. When I refused to sign the payout clearance because the pallet weights didn't match the harbor master's crane logs—crated refrigeration compressors billed as dry cotton yarn, offloaded at low tide without a customs seal—the firm buried me under a fraudulent-interference suit. They stripped my license, froze my escrow bond, and wiped out every dime I owned before the charred pilings cooled.

I lay the yellowed Harbor Mutual manifest flat beside the ledger photocopy and shift the loupe from one sheet to the other. The paper still carries the faint, sour tang of wet wharf ash.

There, stamped in faint violet ink along the lower-left margin of the Portland warehouse manifest, is the exact same octagonal port-clearance code: `B.I.Q.S.-04`.

Barrow Island Quarantine Station.

I swing the desk lamp closer, squinting through the glass at the faded inner ring of the notary seal on the Caretaker's Ledger cover. Beneath the Rhode Island station name, crookedly over-stamped in tiny serif capitals, sit three letters and a registration number that stop the breath in my chest:

`C.M.C. — AUDIT 1998.`

**Calder Maritime & Cargo.**

My father's old marine surveying firm—the same three-man office on Custom House Wharf that went under when he died of a coronary in my second year of college. I still keep his brass parallel ruler and cracked leather logbook in the hall closet. He spent twenty years auditing cold-storage cargo manifests up and down the Atlantic coast, and his signature sat on the opening shipment before Arthur Cutler ever bought a single pine acre on Cragmont Ridge.

***

Wooden floorboards creak behind me in the narrow hallway.

I set the loupe down beside the Harbor Mutual binder without looking up right away, waiting for the cold knot between my shoulder blades to loosen.

**Nina** steps into the kitchen wearing one of my oversized flannel shirts over gray sweatpants, her left arm tucked lightly across the white athletic tape wrapping her ribs. Dark circles still shadow the skin beneath her eyes, yet the hunted set around her jaw has softened since St. Johnsbury. She carries a small, dented **steel lockbox** under her good arm—its corners scraped raw down to bright metal, its hinged lid held shut by a **rusted brass padlock** thick with white sea-salt corrosion.

"The coffee's still hot," I say, nudging the second paper cup across the pine table toward her.

Nina doesn't sit down right away. She looks from the opened Harbor Mutual binder to the raking desk lamp angled across the ledger photocopy, then straight at my face.

"You found the Barrow Island stamp," she says quietly.

"My father audited the first shipment in ninety-eight," I say, tapping the faint `C.M.C.` ring with the tip of my scarred index finger. "Same station code that sat on the Harbor Mutual warehouse manifest before the wharf burned in Portland. Margot wasn't bluffing at the hospital."

Nina sets the dented steel box onto the pine table between our coffee cups. The rusted brass padlock hits the wood with a dry metallic thud, leaving a faint scatter of gray salt grit across the grain. Smells of wet iron, mildew, and old basement concrete rise off the dented lid.

"When Hollis torched my Burlington apartment six years ago," Nina says, pulling out the opposite wooden chair and easing herself down carefully to favor her ribs, "I didn't grab just the *CutlerCor* trial binders. I had a rented storage locker out on Shelburne Road that Arthur didn't know about. Troopers cleared the seal on it yesterday morning."

She reaches into her flannel shirt pocket, pulls out a tiny **tarnished brass key** tied to a frayed length of red baker's twine, and lays it on top of the lockbox lid.

"I told you in the St. Johnsbury ER that I ran because of the pediatric fatalities," Nina says, sliding the heavy steel box toward my hands until its front edge bumps the Harbor Mutual binder. "That was half of it. Look at the notary ring again, Tessa. I didn't disappear six years ago just because of Cutler Biotech."

She keeps two fingers pressed against the rusted brass padlock, holding my eyes steady across the steam rising off the black coffee.

"I disappeared because the man who framed you in Portland was looking for what's inside this box."

Outside the bay window, the morning ferry horn blows a long, low blast across the gray harbor chop, shaking the frosted glass in its wooden sash. I wrap my scarred right hand around the cold brass padlock, feel the rough salt crust bite into my palm, and reach for the tarnished key beside my thumb.
