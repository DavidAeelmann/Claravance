### HIGH-01: Hardware Contradiction on the Front Entry Door Deadbolt (`Smart-Lock` vs. `Interior Thumb-Turn` vs. `Double-Keyed Deadbolt`)
- **Classification:** `[HIGH]` (Confirmed internal hardware contradiction)
- **Chapters & Exact Passages:**
  - **Chapter 1 (lines 23, 95):** `"Before I can knock, the smart-lock deadbolt snaps open on its own... He digs into his pocket and drops a heavy brass house key onto the tabletop, its teeth worn smooth. 'The porch keypad rotates codes every two days. Use the brass key for the front door.'"`
  - **Chapter 19 (line 39):** `"Heavy boots hit the front slate overhead, followed by the rattle of the front deadbolt throwing shut from inside. Cole is back from the causeway."` *(Note: Cole does not have Graham's key ring here; 12 lines later in line 51, Lydia tells Cole: `"Grab Graham's key ring off the study desk"`).*
  - **Chapter 22 (lines 19–21):** `"I dig the brass front-door key out of my jeans with my free hand and toss it across the vestibule to Graham. 'Unlock the deadbolt. Now.' Graham catches it against his chest, stares at me for half a beat, then turns and works the double-keyed lock beside the sidelight."`
- **Explanation of the Issue:**
  - A front door deadbolt cannot simultaneously be (1) a motorized smart-lock deadbolt (Ch. 1), (2) thrown shut from the inside by Cole without a key upon stepping through the door (Ch. 19, line 39), and (3) a double-cylinder (`"double-keyed"`) deadbolt that requires Tessa to toss Graham her brass key so he can unlock it from the *inside* after power is cut (Ch. 22, line 21).
- **Recommended Action:**
  - Either (a) in **Chapter 22, lines 19–21**, have Graham simply twist the manual interior deadbolt thumb-turn when Tessa orders `"Unlock the deadbolt. Now"` (or if Tessa tosses him the key because his hands are shaking / she thinks it's locked, clarify it's a standard deadbolt), OR (b) if preserving the `"double-keyed lock"` in Ch. 22, specify in **Chapter 19, line 39** that Cole locked the interior cylinder with a spare house key on his sheriff's key ring.

### HIGH-02: Antagonist Motivation Paradox for Planting the `OCT 17` Weekender Bag in Graham's Coat Closet
- **Classification:** `[HIGH]` (Antagonist plot/motivation logic issue)
- **Chapters & Exact Passages:**
  - **Chapter 1 (lines 119–143):** `"Shoved into the back corner on the floor, half-hidden under the hem of Graham's longest charcoal trench coat, sits an olive-canvas weekender trimmed in brown leather. Lydia Kiefer drowned three days earlier."`
  - **Chapter 14 (lines 105–107):** `"Graham turns sharp, yanks the closet door open, and shoves two heavy overcoats aside. He stares down at the green canvas bag on the floor, his hand dropping off the wool sleeve. 'That wasn't there Sunday night. Halstead came by Monday morning to drop off a supplemental marine report while I was down in the cellar—stood right here in this vestibule for five minutes.'"`
  - **Chapter 17 (lines 53–55):** `"'Right. That's why Cole had to hide your weekender behind Graham's coats.' 'I told him to drop it there Monday morning,' she shoots back. 'Same way I had to babysit him November first when we staged the sunroom floor—the idiot dropped his pilfered B-4 key behind the baseboard mopping up leaked bleach and hammered the trim right back over it.'"`
- **Explanation of the Issue:**
  - Lydia and Cole's entire scheme is to frame Graham for **murdering Lydia in the sunroom on October 14** and dragging her body to the boathouse at 02:14 AM on October 15.
  - Why would Lydia intentionally order Cole on Monday morning (Nov 2) to plant her packed weekender bag—bearing a **Bar Harbor–Yarmouth ferry tag stamped `OCT 17` (three days *after* her alleged murder)**—in Graham's coat closet? An `OCT 17` ferry tag indicates Lydia was *alive and taking a ferry to Nova Scotia three days after October 14*, which undermines the October 14 murder frame against Graham and immediately tips Tessa off on Night 1!
- **Recommended Action:**
  - Modify **one sentence in Chapter 17, line 54**: instead of Lydia boasting that she *wanted* the `OCT 17` tagged bag planted in the closet as part of the frame, have Lydia snap that she told Cole to stow her travel bag out of sight when she got back from Nova Scotia (or plant her bag without the tag), and **Cole stupidly shoved it behind Graham's coats without stripping the `OCT 17` Yarmouth ferry tag off the handle**—which pairs naturally with her very next sentence complaining about Cole's incompetence (`"Same way I had to babysit him November first when we staged the sunroom floor—the idiot dropped his pilfered B-4 key behind the baseboard"`).

## 6. Potential AI-Writing Patterns Across the Manuscript

Overall, the manuscript is remarkably clean of heavy "AI purple prose" (it avoids florid metaphors, abstract philosophizing, and melodramatic summary endings, adhering instead to concrete physical nouns and procedural verbs). However, a forensic cross-chapter scan identified five subtle recurring structural patterns characteristic of AI drafting that future line editors should watch for:

1. **Utensil / Hand-Stopping Dialogue Beats:** Characters repeatedly pause mid-action with a kitchen utensil or towel when asked a pointed question (most densely in Ch. 6: `"His ladle stops against the rim of the pot"`, line 23; `"Graham's spoon stops halfway to his mouth"`, line 53; `"His hands stop inside the dish towel"`, line 83; also Ch. 10, line 25 `"His fingers go still on the glass"` and Ch. 17, line 45 `"Her thumbs stop on the glass"`).
2. **Periodic Internal Recaps of the "$50,000 Breach Clause / $18,412 Judgment" Stakes:** At scene transitions (Ch. 1, 2, 4, 7, 8, 9, 10, 11, 15), Tessa periodically reminds herself of the $50,000 NDA penalty and the $18,412 judgment. While realistic for someone facing a Monday bank levy, trimming 2–3 redundant mid-book occurrences (such as Ch. 2, line 39 and Ch. 8, line 61) will make the remaining references hit harder.
3. **Sensory Pairing Echoes Across Chapters:** A few specific two-part sensory phrases recur across chapters—e.g., `"smells faintly of cedar shavings and cold rain"` (Ch. 3, line 25) vs. `"smells of cedar and cold rain"` (Ch. 7, line 27); `"ticks in the quiet"` / `"cracking in the quiet"` (Ch. 1, line 19; Ch. 4, line 9); and radiator ticking beats opening or closing scenes (Ch. 2, line 35; Ch. 10, line 99; Ch. 11, line 3; Ch. 20, line 75; Ch. 23, line 3).
4. **Anatomical Stress Micro-Tells:** Recurring use of `"His jaw tightens"` / `"jaw tight"` (Ch. 3, line 69; Ch. 8, lines 41, 97), `"His mouth pulls tight"` / `"mouth goes thin"` (Ch. 2, line 11; Ch. 10, line 35; Ch. 15, line 59), and `"stomach knots"` / `"stomach drops"` (Ch. 1, line 51; Ch. 5, line 19).
5. **Three-Beat Action/Observation Sentences at Scene Openings:** Many chapters open with an atmospheric weather/time sentence followed immediately by a three-part physical positioning sentence (`"I stand/crouch/lean at [X], keeping [Y], while [Z]"`). The openings are strong and grounded, so no structural rewrite is needed—only occasional sentence-rhythm variation during copyediting.
