# Sneak Peek: *The Caretaker's Ledger*
### A Tessa Calder Thriller — Book 2 by David Seelmann

## Chapter 1

Tire chains bite into packed mountain ice and rattle up through the floorboards of my car on every switchback. The dash heater blasts dry air into my eyes while my toes stay numb.

Outside my salted windshield, the Northeast Kingdom of Vermont looks nothing like the Maine coast. Towering black spruce crowd both sides of the unplowed logging road, their boughs bent low under fresh snow that swallows headlights ten paces ahead.

My duffel takes up the passenger seat, stuffed with forty-five thousand dollars' worth of new nondisclosure paperwork from Kiefer-Hall Fiduciary. In the cup holder, the skeleton key Margot Kiefer-Hall slid across the table at Becky's Diner this morning rests on top of a folded copy of my restored Maine private investigator bond.

My left wrist still itches beneath my wool cuff where the Salt Point stitches came out, a thin pink ridge against pale skin. Seven hours ago, I had a ten o'clock interview lined up on Congress Street for a daylight desk job auditing flooded boat hulls. Steady hours, clean paperwork, and daylight.

Then Margot flipped a glossy photograph over on the diner Formica and showed me three words written in my younger sister's cramped, back-slanted cursive in the corner of a mountain guest ledger:

*Don't trust him.*

Six years. Seventy-two months since Nina walked out of a winter internship in Burlington and vanished off every bank grid, tax roll, and credit bureau in New England. I ruined my savings and half my twenties chasing cold tips across three states before the Harbor Mutual lawsuit buried me for good. I told myself I had accepted the silence.

One ink stroke proved I was lying.

Ahead, through a break in the timber, wrought-iron gates loom out of the drifting snow. They match the photograph in my coat pocket: tall forged spears flanked by rough fieldstone pillars, guarding the private drive to Cragmont Lodge. According to Margot's briefing packet, pharmaceutical heir Arthur Cutler, his second wife, and their live-in winter caretaker walked out of the lodge during Tuesday night's blizzard and left three snowmobiles idling by the woodshed. Nobody has filed a missing-persons report yet. The family trustees in Boston want the house inventoried and cleared of private papers before the county sheriff asks why the snowmobiles ran out of gas.

I ease my foot onto the brake and bring the Civic to a halt in front of the iron gates.

My headlights sweep across the drift piled against the lower crossbars.

I don't reach for the gate latch. I sit behind the wheel with the heater fan clicking in the dash and stare down at the snow between the stone pillars.

Margot told me the private road had been drifted shut since Tuesday night's storm. She said the state plow stops at the county line three miles back, and nobody from the Boston office had been able to reach the mountain.

Yet two deep, wide tire ruts cut straight through the powder beneath the iron gates, fresh enough that the waffle tread of mud-terrain tires hasn't filled in with blowing snow.

Someone drove a truck up this mountain within the last hour. And judging by the single set of tracks pointing uphill, they haven't driven back down.

***

I kill the Civic's headlights, leave the car tucked in the dark spruce turnout outside the pillars, and slip through the narrow pedestrian gap beside the stone post with my duffel slung over one shoulder.

Cold air hits my lungs like crushed glass, dry and thin. High above the treeline, a faint ribbon of gray smoke drifts across the ridge.

My boots crunch in the truck ruts as I climb the final bend of the drive.

Cragmont Lodge rises against the gray twilight sky like a stone fortress wedged into the granite mountainside. Steep timber gables sag under thick white drifts. Every window across the three-story front elevation stands dark, interior timber shutters latched tight behind frosted panes. The turnaround in front of the wide stone porch is empty.

I trace the fresh tire ruts past the front steps. They curve around the main house toward a timber carriage barn fifty paces back in the trees, where double barn doors stand padlocked from the outside, fresh snow kicked loose around the threshold.

Whoever drove up here parked their truck inside the barn and locked it behind them.

I step onto the covered porch, keeping my weight near the stone wall where the porch planks don't creak. I pull the skeleton key from my pocket.

I slide the bit into the oversized iron lock on the front oak door.

The wards turn without a scrape. Oiled recently.

I push the door open a crack and slip inside, easing the latch shut behind my heel until the bolt clicks home.

The great hall is cavernous and dim, lit only by pale snow-glare filtering through high windows near the rafters. Mounted stag heads stare down from dark timber walls above a massive fieldstone fireplace. Across the room, a wide oak staircase climbs toward a shadowed balcony.

Warm air hits my face.

I walk slowly toward the hearth. A thick bed of gray birch ash glows dull orange on the iron grate, throwing steady heat across the flagstone floor. A sharp, greasy chemical tang hangs above the grate—unmistakable to anyone who ever investigated an arson claim.

Lamp kerosene. And burnt bond paper.

Someone used the hearth to ash a stack of files less than twenty minutes ago.

A slanted oak reception lectern stands beside the archway leading to the study, the old-fashioned kind built for nineteenth-century hunting clubs. A thick, maroon-leather guest ledger lies open across its brass lip.

My pulse beats hard against my collarbones as I cross the flagstones.

I recognize the cream, red-ruled paper from the cropped fax printout taped to the back of Margot's photograph. Same faded header print. Same column lines for *Date*, *Guest*, and *Room*.

Except when I look down at the open ledger under the gray overhead light, my stomach drops.

The top sheet hasn't been turned. It has been sliced clean out along the leather spine with a straight-edged blade, leaving a jagged fringe of cream paper still glued into the binding.

Only the bottom right corner of the missing page tore loose before the blade finished its cut—a tiny triangular scrap pinned beneath a brass paperweight. The tail of a single cursive letter *m* curls across that scrap in dark blue ink, back-slanted. Nina's ink.

A freshly stamped brass room tag on a steel ring rests directly on top of the sliced binding, where the wet glue is still tacky to the touch.

**ROOM 4 — ATTIC EAST.**

Above my head, directly across the third-floor rafters where the attic corridor runs, a floorboard groans under the slow, deliberate shift of a boot.

Then an iron slide bolt grates home on the upper stairwell door—and behind me on the front porch, a padlock clanks shut across the outer storm door.
