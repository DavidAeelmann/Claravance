# The Wife's Inventory

### By David Seelmann

---

# Chapter 1

The Salt Point causeway runs barely above the tide line, a wet strip of asphalt hemmed in by black granite and dark water. My Civic's muffler shield rattles up through the floorboards. The wipers smear salt spray across the windshield faster than they clear it, and a three-note mattress-outlet jingle from a Brunswick radio station has been looping in my head for forty miles.

Rusted tide markers bolted to a concrete culvert slide past the mirror. Low water tonight, exposing flats of marsh grass along the shoulder, though the mainland behind me is already gone in the fog. Off to the right, a shingle shack squats on pilings over a mudflat stacked with wire oyster traps. One yellow bulb bleeds into the mist beside its door.

My phone dropped to zero past the lobster pound and now clings to a single bar. The blue GPS line dead-ends at a private peninsula lot owned by a Delaware LLC. I pulled the corporate filing and the town tax card at six this morning outside Portland over a stale cruller. Getting sued into the ground cures you of answering burner texts without checking who holds the deed.

The Cumberland County clerk's letter rides shotgun on the passenger seat. *Eighteen thousand, four hundred and twelve dollars.* If I miss another installment, they levy my bank account Monday. Fifteen grand cash for two weeks of boxing up a dead woman's closets won't erase the judgment, but it stalls the sheriff and keeps my landlord off my back. Nobody drives out into a Maine fog bank in November because they're brave. They do it because their checking account has four hundred dollars and change.

Two cedar posts flank a timber gate at the neck of the drive, a tarnished keypad mounted on rusted pipe beside the driver's window. I crank the glass down. The rubber sticks, then gives with a dry groan, and freezing salt air hits me in the face.

I punch the code from the broker's message. Nothing. The buttons are crusted with dried salt, so I scrub them with my cuff and press harder. A sharp beep cuts the fog. Under wet gravel, the gate motor grinds and drags the barrier partway back so the Civic can squeeze through.

It shuts behind me with a heavy hydraulic clunk. I ease back into drive and creep up the lane.

Past a stand of windbreak pines, the Kiefer house comes out of the mist low against the cliff. The tax card listed a seventies cottage on this lot, Lydia's family's old place, and whoever rebuilt it bolted modern glass and dark steel right onto the original stone foundation and chimney. All the windows facing the cove are dark except for one amber strip tucked under the eaves. The floodlight pole over the turnaround is dead.

I pull in beside a dark Audi sedan and kill the engine. It ticks in the quiet. Out past the breakwater, a buoy bell clangs twice, dull and far off.

My duffel's worn zipper catches my thumb as I drag it off the passenger seat. I cross the wet flagstone walk to the landing. Something sharper cuts through the damp wood: fresh linseed oil, maybe deck sealer, except the planks under my boots are bone-dry.

Before I can knock, a key turns inside the heavy brass deadbolt and the bolt throws back.

The black door pulls back. Graham Kiefer stands in the doorway with one hand on the steel handle and a brass key still pinched between two fingers. He's thinner than he looked in his spring magazine profile, gaunt under pale gray eyes, the cuffs of his dark olive sweater fraying at the wrists. His eyes flick from my wet hair to the duffel, then out past my shoulder at the Civic.

"Ms. Calder," he says. His voice has that worn-down steadiness of someone who has given the same timeline to Coast Guard officers and insurance adjusters twenty times running. "You beat the tide."

"Barely. Your keypad's caked in salt."

"Everything out here rots if you turn your back on it." He steps aside and lets me carry my own bag. "Come in before the damp gets into the hall."

***

The front entry is a low, chilly mudroom tacked onto the main two-story wing, wrapping past a utility sink toward a side door by the garage bay. A coat bench and tall closet line the inner wall. Above the bench, rain taps on a skylight hatch set low into the pitched roof, eight feet up at most.

Through the archway, the main floor opens up. A modern kitchen with a long stone island takes up the right side of the room; to the left, an open oak staircase climbs to the second floor, with a ground-floor hallway tucking behind it. Straight ahead, past the dining room, a massive wall of ocean glass frames black water and churning foam below the cliff. It throws my own tired face back at me over the drop.

Graham walks me past the island and stops at a narrow table near the stairs. A thick manila folder lies centered on the tabletop beside a cheap black rollerball, its edges squared to the grain.

"Tea?" He nods toward an electric kettle with its cord coiled by the sink. "Chamomile, or black? I forget to bump the heat until the fog moves in."

"Water's fine later." I drop my bag beside a chair. "Your broker said five thousand up front. Ten on day fourteen."

"All the way from Portland, and we're talking cash before you take your coat off." Dry, seeing if I'll blink. He pulls an unsealed white envelope out of his sweater pocket and lays it next to the folder, flap tucked in. "Count it after you sign."

"I'll count it first." I toss my jacket over the chair back and open the folder.

Six pages of dense type. Non-disclosure, non-disparagement, a blanket ban on photographing hardware or records on the property, and a fifty-thousand-dollar breach penalty clause bolded near the bottom.

*Fifty thousand.* Whoever drafted this pulled my Harbor Mutual judgment and knew a second suit would bury me for good. For half a second I think about grabbing my bag and seeing if I can beat the tide back across the causeway.

Then I picture the clerk's envelope sitting on my front seat. I stay in the chair.

"Tell me something." Graham leans against the counter. "Your broker said you worked commercial fraud claims before moving to estates. Why pack closets?"

He already knows Harbor Mutual blacklisted me over that Commercial Street fire manifest. He just wants to watch me say it.

I keep my eyes on page four. "Less paperwork. Nobody drags you into superior court over boxed wool sweaters. Though fifty grand in breach penalties is steep for old clothes."

"That clause isn't for her clothes." He crosses his arms. "My company's buyout filings and proprietary records are in this house. If a contractor leaks a page, or runs to local papers or county deputies with half-baked stories before probate clears, my Arlington buyer walks. Whatever you see here stays inside these walls until Friday."

"It pays cash." I tap the pen against the margin. "Clause four bars removing personal correspondence or digital media. If a thumb drive falls out of a coat pocket, where does it go?"

He watches my hand instead of my face. "Into the green archival totes in the study. Unopened. My wife's attorneys are coming up from Boston Friday to file the probate petition."

I look up. "This Friday? Your broker told me fourteen days."

"Fourteen days is the full pack-out and charity haul." He nudges one corner of the white cash envelope until the paper sits dead square with the wood grain. "Clothes, kitchenware, guest linens, studio canvases. Probate counsel just wants an itemized preliminary ledger by Friday at five so the court can seal the inventory before the weekend. Can you hit Friday?"

Four days to catalog a two-story glass house, cooped up with a man who can't stand a crooked envelope on his table. "Depends how much she kept."

"Less than you'd think." He glances toward the dark ocean glass where both of us hang pale over empty water. "Lydia cared about keeping things in order."

I flip back to page two and tap clause three with the pen cap. "Fix this before I sign. Your agreement bans all photography on the premises, yet probate counsel expects condition records on high-value items. I'm not stepping under a fifty-thousand-dollar breach clause without proof of what I logged, and what was already damaged before I walked in. Write in an exception for timestamped estate-verification photos."

He watches me, weighing whether I'm worth the hassle. Then he holds out a hand for the pen. He doesn't argue; he writes a tight line in the margin allowing timestamped inventory photographs, adds *G.K.* and today's date, and slides the folder back.

I sign *Tessa Calder* on all three lines, pull the white envelope over, and flip through the bills.

Fifty fresh hundreds strapped in a single five-thousand-dollar Portsmouth bank band. Real ink, blue security strips catching the kitchen downlights. Enough to kill Monday's levy. I tuck the envelope into my duffel's inside pocket and snag a loose thread from the lining across the zipper teeth. An old habit, in case anyone gets curious overnight.

"House rules," Graham says. He straightens up now that the ink's dry. "One: everything from the master closet, the upstairs studio, and the living areas goes onto the shared spreadsheet. Photograph anything worth over five hundred dollars before you wrap it in acid-free tissue."

"Fine."

"Two: the boathouse at the bottom of the cove stairs is locked, and it stays locked. The Coast Guard cleared it after the storm, and salt damp ruins winter storage anyway. Stay off the cliff steps after dark. There aren't any rails."

"Got it."

"Three." He nods toward the underside of the open oak stairs, where a flush wood door blends into the paneling. "Those concrete steps go down to my acoustic darkroom and prototype servers, behind a steel fire door with a heavy deadbolt. Half those units are under Defense Department NDAs. If you trip the perimeter latch, my security vendor in Portland logs an automatic breach alert. Your contract voids on the spot. Clear?"

"I'm here to pack sweaters, Mr. Kiefer, not steal defense patents."

"Graham." He digs into his pocket and drops a heavy brass house key onto the tabletop, its teeth worn smooth. "The porch keypad rotates codes every two days. Use the key for the front door. Your guest suite is down the ground-floor north hall, first left. Towels are on the rail."

I start to ask where the green archival totes are, but his phone buzzes on the counter.

He glances down. The crease between his brows tightens, then smooths out. "Estate counsel. Take ten minutes to drop your bag. My study's across the north hall from you, though I'm taking this upstairs in the master suite for a little privacy. I'll bring the Wi-Fi password down in a minute."

He doesn't wait for an answer and heads up the stairs, his wool socks silent on the oak. Halfway up, he pauses and looks past my shoulder toward the front entry. Then he rounds the landing, and a heavy door latches upstairs.

I stand by the table and listen to the house settle. Under the floor, a dehumidifier rumbles like an idling engine. Wind leans into the seaward glass, and the fridge compressor clicks off behind the island.

The whole drive hits me at once, and I didn't even get that glass of water before he vanished upstairs. Four days in a cold house where every door comes with a threat—maybe I should have turned around at the gate, though my checking account doesn't care about my pride.

I grab my duffel and jacket and head back to the front entry before the coat drips on the floors.

My boot clips the edge of the rubber boot tray beside the coat bench. A pair of men's duck boots stands on the left side, crusted with dried salt and squared up against the baseboard. The right half has been wiped bare—a clean oval where a smaller pair used to sit.

Of course he put his dead wife's boots away. Anyone would.

I pull open the tall coat closet beside the bench. The hinges don't make a sound. Four heavy men's wool overcoats hang spaced apart on wooden hangers, still stiff from the dry cleaner. An olive-canvas weekender trimmed in brown leather is shoved into the back corner on the floor, half-hidden under the hem of Graham's longest trench coat. A gold-stamped luggage tag dangles from the zipper ring: *L.K.*

I stop, the jacket still wadded in my hand.

Nineteen days since Lydia Kiefer took a skiff past the breakwater during an October squall and didn't come back. The Coast Guard called off the search days later. Her attorneys file for probate Friday.

Shoved all the way to the back, out of sight. My hand is already on the leather grip.

A tiny tarnished padlock hangs through the zipper eye, unlocked, its metal loop pushed through loose and left unlatched. An elastic cord loops a stiff paper luggage tag onto the leather handle, blue ink printed along its perforated edge, the kind ferries use for deck baggage.

I angle the cardstock toward the dim entry light. The port ink across the top is smudged: *BAR HARBOR–YARMOUTH — SLIP 4.*

Below it, the date stamp is dry and black.

**OCT 17.**

Right over the main hall, a floorboard pops. I shove the tag back under the leather handle as footsteps start toward the stairs.

# Chapter 2

I bump the closet door shut with my hip just short of the latch click and hang my wet jacket on the hook beside the bench. By the time Graham's socks hit the bottom stair, I'm already reaching down for my duffel.

He stops in the archway with his phone pressed against his thigh, looking from my dripping coat to the closed closet door before meeting my eyes. Neither of us moves. If he sees the hairline gap along the jamb, or notices the wool coat shifted inside, he keeps it to himself.

"Forgot the Wi-Fi password," he says, holding out a torn square of cardstock with ink scrawled across it. "And you left your front-door key on the table."

"Thanks." I step over to take both, the key cool against my palm. "Which room is Lydia's studio? If you want a preliminary ledger by Friday, I'd rather see how much is in there tonight than waste morning light."

"End of the upper hall, opposite the master suite. Don't start boxing anything tonight, Ms. Calder. Get some sleep. The wind off the cove gets loud after midnight."

*Ms. Calder*, said the way a man says *plumber* when he thinks he's overpaying by the hour. "I sleep fine with noise."

He doesn't argue. He just leans against the archway trim and watches me haul my bag down the ground-floor north hall until I turn into the guest suite.

First left, like he said. Small and noticeably colder than the hall. A narrow bed sits pushed under a high window facing the dark trees instead of the water, which feels like a minor mercy until I shut the door and twist the lock. The bolt turns with zero resistance, the kind of flimsy privacy latch anyone with a butter knife or a credit card could pop in three seconds.

On the small oak dresser, a slate tray holds a nine-dollar glass bottle of Italian sparkling water, two tiny folded squares of Belgian linen, and a lavender pillow mist nobody asked for. A mean little spike of resentment hits me out of nowhere. Rich people will drop forty bucks on perfumed water for a room nobody sleeps in, then talk to the hired help like they're overcharging for drywall, when my checking account is sitting in the red waiting on a Portland bank levy. I shove the pillow mist behind the lamp so I don't have to look at it.

I drop my duffel on the luggage rack and check the bit of lining thread I hooked across the zipper teeth downstairs. Still intact. Nobody opened it while I was in the mudroom, though the fact that I'm checking my own zipper tells me I'm already too wired to sleep. I pull my laptop out and sit on the edge of the mattress, staring at the closed door. Six hours in the car, a dead woman's husband watching me from his hallway, and a smoke detector blinking red overhead.

The smart play is to brush my teeth, crawl under the quilt, and treat this like any other estate job. Take the check, keep my head down, and stop wondering why Graham shoved a packed weekender behind his coats.

The quiet in this room brings back my sister Nina's last voicemail from six years ago—her bright, hurried laugh cutting off mid-sentence before a county deputy in Vermont decided three weeks was long enough to stop looking.

I open the laptop anyway. Next to Graham's sterile inventory spreadsheet, I start an offline encrypted note on my hard drive and type a single line about the closet, just enough to anchor the timestamp before my memory blurs it. I stare at the blinking cursor, waiting for the adrenaline to drop. It doesn't.

A sharp metal tick from the radiator under the window makes me flinch. Downstairs, Graham hadn't looked like a man improvising. He'd looked like a man who lined up envelopes with the wood grain on his table. Leaving a sloppy loose end in his own front closet didn't fit him—unless he hadn't been the one to carry that bag into the house, and he'd only buried it behind his coats because he didn't know who might be watching the drive.

Across the north hall, the study door shuts softly. Footsteps cross the wood inside, then a desk chair scrapes back. He'd glanced toward the front entry from the stairs and told me to stay out of the studio until morning. If I wait for daylight, he'll be hovering over my shoulder with a clipboard, or whatever he doesn't want seen up there will already be in a trash bag.

I tug off my wet boots, dig dry wool socks out of my bag, and slip the house key into my jeans pocket. Slightly stupid and wide awake, I ease the privacy bolt back. A gray wool runner covers the north hall and swallows my footsteps. Light leaks from under Graham's study door directly across from mine, along with the clack of a mechanical keyboard. Every few seconds the typing stops for the dry rustle of paper, then picks back up.

I hug the drywall where the floorboards stay quiet, slip past his door to the main staircase, and take the steps slowly toward the upper landing. Halfway up, a stair tread groans under my heel. I freeze mid-step and wait until the keyboard starts again below, then finish the climb on the outside edge of the treads.

At the far end of the upstairs hall, the left leaf of the studio's double doors is pinned shut and the active door stands cracked open.

The air inside Lydia's studio is cold, sharp with the bite of paint thinner. Pale moonlight angles through a tall seaward sash window across the room, catching an easel beside stacks of canvases turned face-inward against the wall. Three wide flat-files line the left wall with their drawers pushed shut and an unlit desk lamp resting on the nearest cabinet. A heavy worktable takes up the center of the floor, with a writing desk set back to the right.

I ease the door nearly shut behind me, leaving a narrow gap so I'll hear if Graham's keyboard quits downstairs. The wall beside the jamb is bare—no overhead switch. I pull out my phone, drop the screen brightness to the bottom, and use the dim blue glow to get my bearings.

A dozen sable brushes stand bristles-up in a mason jar on the worktable, metal ferrules shining, not a speck of pigment on the hair. Beside them, the ceramic palette is scoured down to cloudy glaze.

I crouch by the flat-files along the north wall and ease the top drawer out an inch, wincing at the dry drag of wood on wood. Watercolor sheets lie stacked inside, each labeled in neat ink: *Rosa rugosa — Salt Point, Aug 2026.* *Laminaria — Cove Breakwater, Sept 2026.* Every date stops in September, every label slanting up to the right. Under a sheet of waxed tissue paper, I find a folded marine chart of Penobscot Bay with penciled tick marks tracing low-water depths and boulder shoals along the Salt Point breakwater. Normal enough for someone who ran a skiff, except the folds are razor-sharp and barely handled, the paper stiff and dry straight off a laser printer.

I push the drawer shut and remember a throwaway line from the broker's intake sheet: Lydia hated central air and kept her studio window cracked even in October storms so the turpentine wouldn't set off migraines. I step around the easel and push up on the painted wooden sash overlooking the cove.

It doesn't budge.

I tilt my phone light down into the track. Fresh white semi-gloss paint bridges the seam between sash and frame, cured solid without a single pry mark. I wipe my damp palms on my jeans and turn back to the desk. A green blotter covers the middle of the desktop, where a tarnished iron paperweight holds down a closed sketchbook. An uncapped silver Montblanc fountain pen rests beside the book, dried black ink crusted on the nib, next to a cream stationery pad with a handwritten departure checklist on top.

I keep my fingers off the barrel. Old Harbor Mutual reflex—touch a scene before you shoot it, and defense counsel eats you alive over smeared prints. I kill the flash, brace my elbows against my ribs, and snap two tight shots of the desk layout: the sketchbook, the paperweight, the angle of the pen, the ink lines on the pad. If Graham walks in tomorrow and claims something moved, I want proof of how it sat tonight.

Then I lift the paperweight by a fraction and flip the sketchbook open by its bottom corner. Fine pencil studies of kelp fronds and barnacles fill the front half, Latin names penciled lightly underneath. Every stroke slopes forward, right-handed, clean through page forty. Toward the back, three blank leaves show faint pressure lines where someone sliced pages cleanly out along the binding with a sharp blade.

I ease the cover shut and set the paperweight back on the corner a hair off its original angle without thinking. Maybe Graham wouldn't notice a millimeter shift, or maybe he'd spot it the second he stepped through the door; either way, I'm too tired to line it back up against the photo on my phone. I lean over the stationery pad instead, careful not to brush the pen beside it.

Four quick items in dark dry ink: *skiff mooring line, sunroom latches, cellar latch, harbor tide*. Down the left margin, smeared ink drags through every first letter. I angle my phone light across the ink once more, memorizing the backward hook on each stroke.

Downstairs, the keyboard stops.

For two seconds the house goes dead silent, save for the wind rattling the sash outside. Then a chair scrapes back across wood.

I shove my phone into my pocket and back away toward the flat-files. Panic hits me. A stair tread creaks up from the landing—the same board I stepped on five minutes ago. Slow weight shifts across the upstairs runner, then stops right outside the cracked studio door.

Out in the hall, the doorknob turns. I step fast toward the flat-files and reach for the desk lamp in the dark.

# Chapter 3

My hand is already on the neck of the desk lamp, fingers curled loose like I just bumped into it hunting for the switch in the dark.

The studio door swings wide on silent hinges and throws a pale wedge of hallway light across the floorboards. Graham stops on the threshold with an empty mug hooked on his finger, still in the olive sweater. He checks the desk first—the blotter, the closed sketchbook—then drops his gaze to my wool socks.

"Thought I heard a latch," he says in a low voice that barely carries over the wind against the glass. "You said you weren't starting till morning."

"Couldn't sleep." I take my hand off the lamp. "And you want a preliminary ledger by Friday. Figured I'd count the flat-file drawers and see how many boxes we're short before daylight."

He steps into the room anyway, slow and methodical, as if checking whether a drawer hangs crooked on its runners. He stops by the desk and looks down at the uncapped Montblanc and the smudged scratch pad. I stay still by the cabinets, waiting for him to catch the shifted paperweight, pull the NDA, and march me back to my Civic before I've earned a cent of the balance.

Instead, the stiffness goes out of his shoulders. He doesn't touch the pen or the pad. He just looks at the blotter for a second, then turns toward the window.

"Lydia hated overhead lights," he says. He stares out at the black water through the painted-shut sash. "She'd work under that lamp until two in the morning when she had a commission due. I left the room the way she had it for the lawyers."

"Even the brushes?" It comes out before I can stop it.

He turns his head back toward me, gray eyes unreadable in the dim hall light. "Meaning?"

"Nothing. Just tidy for an active studio." Too much. I give a tired shrug and nod toward the hall. "I'll log the canvases first thing after breakfast. Goodnight, Graham."

He stays planted in the doorway until I'm right in front of him, so I have to turn sideways past his sleeve to reach the runner. He smells faintly of cedar shavings and cold rain, odd for a man who hasn't been outside since I arrived. He doesn't follow me down the stairs toward the ground-floor guest room. Halfway down, I hear the studio door pull shut behind him, followed by the click of a key turning in a lock that wasn't engaged ten minutes ago.

Back in my room, I wedge the desk chair under the doorknob before I drop onto the bed. Friday feels a long way off.

***

By seven-thirty, the fog has pressed right up against the dining-room glass, swallowing the outer breakwater in flat gray. Low tide has stripped the cove down to black ledges and knotted rockweed under a low pewter sky. Graham stands at the island in a clean gray oxford shirt with the sleeves rolled to his elbows and pours coffee from a glass carafe into two mugs. In daylight, the hollows under his eyes look bruised.

"Black?" he asks. He keeps his eyes on the carafe as he slides a mug across the stone.

"Please." I pull out a barstool and take the mug. It tastes like it sat on the burner since Tuesday. I thought about hitting record on my phone in my pocket. Maine is a one-party consent state, sure, but getting caught running a hot mic in a defense contractor's kitchen is an easy way to buy a lawsuit.

I set the mug down on the bare stone. Graham's eyes drop straight to the faint damp ring around the base, and he reaches across for a cork coaster stacked beside the fruit bowl, nudging it next to my elbow without a word.

"Lydia couldn't stand rings on the honed soapstone," he says, still looking at the counter. "Even plain water. And she alphabetized the spice jars in the pantry by Latin name, which drove me crazy every time I wanted cumin."

I lift the mug onto the cork. "Good to know. I'll stay out of the cumin."

He gives a stiff, joyless half-nod, the small talk dying flat between us, and glances back toward the glass.

Graham leans back against the counter, sips, and watches the exposed mudflats. "I ordered twenty wardrobe cartons and acid-free tissue from a supply house in Rockland. They're dropping them at the gate by noon."

"Good. I'll need the study totes too, if I'm logging paperwork."

"After breakfast." He sets his mug beside a folded yellow legal pad. "How did your old outfit operate, Tessa? Harbor Mutual. When a claim felt off—say, a marine loss in bad weather—what did they actually pull?"

*Tessa.* Last night it was *Ms. Calder*. Now we're having morning coffee over marine claims.

I take a swallow before answering. "Depends on the payout. Small skiff losses under fifty grand usually clear on a harbor deputy's report and a Coast Guard incident log. Nobody pays a diver to go down in a rocky cove in November unless the hull washes up with drill holes in it."

"And if there's an estate fight?"

"Then the lawyers subpoena phone pings, bank pulls, toll transponders." His thumb presses hard against the ceramic mug handle. "Why? Is Lydia's family contesting probate on Friday?"

He looks down into his cup. "Her brother lives in Zurich. Hasn't spoken to her since their father died, and he's already leaning on the Boston trustees at Bramwell & Shaw to freeze our joint accounts until a formal death certificate clears."

"Does he have standing?"

"Enough to stall it."

"Stall what?"

"My access to Lydia's trust. I'm a joint signatory, provided everything stays in good standing and probate doesn't lock the ledger."

"How much?"

"Five-point-four."

"Million?"

"Before you ask, no, it isn't money I can just move around. But Kiefer Soundworks has a patent buyout sitting in escrow with a defense prime in Arlington. If Bramwell & Shaw freezes the accounts before that closes, I'm screwed."

I keep my face neutral, nod like a sympathetic clerk, and watch his hands. "And a probate freeze ties up company equity?"

"Ties up all of it." The smoothness drops out of his voice. "My R&D payroll hits next Wednesday. If they freeze the trust before Friday's preliminary filing, the Arlington buyer walks, my cash dries up, and creditors call the lab lease by Thanksgiving. If the filing clears on time, the buyout closes, and I have the money to clear every company obligation before an audit ever touches it."

"Still doesn't explain why you hired me through a blind broker instead of calling an estate crew in Camden," I say. "Local outfits pack faster and don't charge cash premiums."

He sets his mug down, the polite host routine gone. "Local outfits talk at the general store before five o'clock. Every clerk in this county knows Deputy Halstead, and Halstead already has three reporters poking around the causeway. I needed someone with zero local ties who knows how to document property thoroughly enough that Boston counsel can't claim I hid marital assets—or missed something inside this house."

I hold his gaze across the counter. For the first time since I drove through the gate, the panic behind his eyes looks real. I know what eviction panic looks like. Even so, five-point-four million in trust money buys a much bigger motive than winter sweaters.

"What happened the night of the storm?" I ask quietly, while he's still talking. "The broker said October fourteenth. Squall came in fast?"

His expression shutters. "She took the fiberglass skiff out around ten-thirty to check the mooring line before the wind shifted. I was down in the basement darkroom running an acoustic sweep with noise-canceling headphones on. When I came up at midnight, the dock light was off and her rain slicker was gone."

"And the Coast Guard found the boat at dawn?"

"Drifting half a mile past the breakwater, oars still shipped." He picks up his mug and turns toward the back glass sliders. The gray morning light catches a small tremor in his wrist before he sets the cup down again. "Deputy Halstead logged it before noon. If you're done, I'd like you to start in the sunroom before the supply truck gets here."

***

The back glass sliders open onto a weathered deck wrapping the seaward side of the house, the planks slick from the overnight fog. Directly below the rail, steep granite steps with no handrail zig-zag down the bluff to the dark timber boathouse perched over the pilings. Green algae coats the lower risers where high water reaches in winter storms; I wouldn't try those stairs in dry weather, let alone after fog.

I tell Graham I want to check the light on the sunroom windows before pulling the inventory sheets, then step out onto the deck. Behind the glass, he's already wiping the spotless counter where my cup sat.

From up here, the boathouse looks weather-sound and well-kept, its cedar shingles weathered darker than the main house from salt spray. A brass padlock secures the double doors facing the cliff. A faded strip of county paper seal bridges the seam, torn cleanly in two with one loose end fluttering in the breeze. The side window facing the rock wall is shuttered tight from inside. If the Coast Guard cleared it three weeks ago, nobody bothered cracking a shutter to air the place out.

Out past the rocky ledge, the fog parts over the shallow mud channel between Kiefer's cove and the next spit—the same shingle shack on pilings I passed coming over the causeway last night. The yellow porch bulb is off now, and only wet gray siding shows against the tide line.

Something moves down near the water.

Well below the locked boathouse, an older woman in a stiff yellow oilskin bib is hauling wire traps onto a weathered work skiff. A thick silver braid hangs out from under her dark wool watch cap, and even from the top of the cliff she moves with the easy speed of someone who has worked cold water her whole life. She stacks two dripping traps on the bow, wipes her forehead with the back of a rubber-gloved wrist, and looks directly up at me standing alone on the deck.

I lift a hand in a half-automatic wave, expecting her to turn back to her boat. Instead, she presses an open palm against her chest, glances left toward the dining glass where Graham's silhouette moves behind the counter, and holds two fingers stiffly out beside her hip. She holds them there until she's sure I've seen, then jerks her chin toward the locked boathouse right beneath me.

Two what? Two people? Two days? Before I can even mouth a question, she bends back to her traps and shoves the skiff off the mudflat with a wooden oar until the fog closes behind her stern.

Behind me, the glass door slides open on its track. Graham steps out onto the deck with a legal pad and clipboard.

"Sunroom rugs are first on the ledger," he says with a glance down at the empty mudflat where ripples are still fading across the gray water. "Maren Lindqvist keeps to herself down there. Don't waste your afternoon talking if she wanders up the beach. She's been fighting us over the cove boundary line for six years."

"Doesn't look like she's wandering up here." I step back inside before he can read my face.

Graham holds the glass door open, his hand lingering on the frame until I pass him. Neither of us mentions the boathouse or the torn county seal down at the bottom of the stairs. I take the clipboard he hands me, nod once, and walk toward the sunroom.

# Chapter 4

The taillights of Graham's Audi disappear down the drive, and I open the shared spreadsheet on my laptop to type the first line: *Sunroom, hand-knotted wool rug, charcoal gray, fair condition*.

He's down by the timber gate hauling twenty flat-packed wardrobe cartons up from the Rockland supply truck. If he checks edit timestamps from his phone, he'll see me working. Dull clerical work makes good cover. As long as I look like a slow clerk logging lamps and throw pillows, he won't hover.

Just off the dining room, the sunroom juts out over the cliff on the southeast corner of the house: all tall glass, a wide slider out to the deck, and cold gray glare across the floorboards. Two armchairs face the cove across a low stone table and a broad woven rug. Below the bluff, the fog has thinned into damp shreds curling around the pilings of Maren Lindqvist's shack, though her skiff is gone. Just the empty wooden dock and a gull hunched on the corner post.

*Two fingers.* I kneel to shoot the maker's tag under the first armchair. *Two people? Two days till Friday?*

Whatever she meant, she hadn't risked climbing the bluff to say it out loud. That meant she was scared of Graham, or scared of whoever else carried a key to that boathouse, or maybe she just hated rich people from Boston and wanted to rattle the hired help. Half the time at Harbor Mutual, I talked myself into seeing arson where a guy had simply left a space heater too close to a stack of dry rags, and look where that kind of certainty got me—broke, blacklisted, and pricing throw pillows on a wet rock in November. No, stop it. Nobody presses a flat palm to their chest and jerks their chin at a sealed boathouse because of a property-line dispute over mudflats. Even if Maren saw something, I can't walk into Graham's kitchen accusing a grieving tech founder of staging his own house without something solid in my hand. Not with Friday's probate petition already sitting on a judge's desk and my own rent due on the first.

I log both armchairs, checking the undersides for loose webbing and water stains, then move on to a floor lamp with a heavy base. Its braided cord disappears under the rug toward a floor outlet. Next is a side table holding three unread books on Maine coastal plants. Their spines crack stiffly when I fan the pages for tucked receipts or margin notes. Nothing inside. The price stickers are still on the backs, untouched. I set them back in the same crooked stack and rub my hands together. The gray light makes the whole room feel like a waiting room.

Next is the rug. To log the weave and check for a maker's label on the backing, I have to turn up a corner. I grab the thick bound edge nearest the wall and haul back. It's heavier than it looks, dense and stiff-backed; the coarse weave drags across the planks and kicks dust up into the gray light.

A sharp chemical smell rises off the bare wood—bleach, trapped under the heavy wool.

I lean in over the floorboards. Most of the cedar has weathered to honey-amber under coastal sun, except for a jagged patch along the baseboard where the grain is stripped pale and rough. Someone scrubbed it hard, then rubbed boiled linseed oil over the chalky scar to dull the contrast. I glance up at the ceiling corners—just standard glass-break sensors wired along the upper window frames, no cameras—and take three close shots of the bleached fibers and oily sheen.

When I brush my fingertips across the pale patch, it leaves a faint, sticky drag on my skin. The oil hasn't finished curing. Right above the scar, a hairline gap runs between the baseboard and the drywall.

Heavy wool traps fumes for weeks, so the bleach alone wouldn't prove when it happened. Still, the oil is tacky, and the molding above it has been pried out and shoved back. Whatever happened here doesn't look nineteen days old.

By the time the front latch clicks and Graham's boots thump onto the mudroom tray, I'm kneeling beside the second armchair with my clipboard tucked under my arm, writing *Armchair #2: minor sun fade on west arm* as if I've been at it for twenty minutes.

"Cartons are in the foyer," he calls from the kitchen arch, slightly winded from hauling cardboard in from the rain. Water beads on the shoulders of his fleece, and he shakes his hair back once before wiping his palms on his jeans. "Half of them are blocking the coat closet, so watch your shins. Take a break whenever you want lunch. There's sourdough and turkey in the fridge."

"I'm good for another hour," I call back, and try to keep my voice flat and bored. "Just finishing the sunroom before I start the master closet. Lots of sun fade on the upholstery out here."

"Makes sense. South-facing glass eats everything eventually. Coffee's still warm if you want it."

His footsteps pause near the table, then move toward the kitchen sink. Water runs, a glass clinks, and a moment later the stairs creak under his weight as he heads up to the second floor.

I wait until an upstairs door clicks shut overhead, then step into the main hall to check that the ground floor is empty. Through the mudroom doorway, the wiped-clean empty half of the boot tray sits bare beside Graham's duck boots.

Back in the sunroom, I push the table leg aside and fold the rug back farther so the whole scarred patch is open. The paint along the top of the baseboard is intact, though two finishing nails near the corner stick out far enough to catch a fingernail. Someone pried the molding out to wipe liquid that had run underneath, then kicked it back into place with the heel of a shoe.

I hook my thumbnail behind the lip and pull. Dry plaster dust sifts onto the pale boards. Down inside the dark gap between wood and drywall, something dull catches the light.

I ease the slotted driver of my multi-tool behind the molding, careful not to bite the paint. One gouge in the woodwork and Graham would spot it the second he walked back in. I lever the trim out a fraction, hook the edge of whatever is wedged down there, and drag it out onto the floor.

It hits the planks with a heavy *tink*.

A rusted brass door key, green tarnish crusted into the teeth, tied to a faded red plastic tag by frayed cotton string. Someone punched **B-4** into the wide head.

I stare at it lying in the middle of the bleached patch. The grease around the collar is smeared, as if wet or gloved fingers fumbled it before it dropped behind the trim.

*B-4.* Basement. Maybe the acoustic darkroom Graham swore ran an automatic electronic breach alert. Why a rusted key to a defense vault was sitting behind a freshly bleached baseboard, I didn't want to guess standing in front of thirty feet of plate glass. I don't touch it with bare skin. I pull a square of acid-free tissue from the sample pack Graham left me this morning and fold it around the key. Graham wouldn't leave his own basement key behind a sloppy pry job under a rug he knew I had to inspect. Someone else had fumbled it while scrubbing this floor—unless he planted it there just to see what I'd do.

I smooth the rug down, nudge the table leg back into its dents, and head into the hall.

The glass walls suddenly feel like a specimen jar. Every pane throws my own reflection back at me. The house stays dead quiet overhead. I can't just wait around down here for Graham to wander back in and hope the room explains itself. I need twenty minutes off the peninsula: a real cell signal to run a background check on Kiefer Soundworks, a couple rolls of packing tape, and a look at Salt Point harbor in daylight where nobody is watching me through the windows.

In the mudroom, I pull on my jacket and slide the tissue-wrapped key into the inside breast pocket. The hard metal presses against my ribs through the lining. I grab my keys off the shelf and turn toward the porch.

Boots cross the floorboards overhead.

"Tessa?" Graham calls down from the upper hall. "You heading out?"

I step back into the archway where he can see me and let my arms hang loose so I'm not shielding my chest. "Out of heavy packing tape," I call up. "And there were only two rolls of label stickers on the counter. Figured I'd hit the general store past the causeway before the fog closes in."

Steps move along the upstairs runner until Graham leans over the railing to look down into the hall. He glances at my jacket pocket, hesitates, and meets my eyes.

"Thought you were doing the master closet after lunch," he says.

"Can't box sweaters without tape." My mouth feels dry. I force myself not to shift my weight or touch the zipper.

He studies me for a second before answering and taps his fingers against the rail. "Fair enough. Skip the coastal road past the lobster pound, though," he says. "County crew's patching a culvert washout. Take the main harbor route, and keep your receipt. I'll reimburse supplies Friday."

"Will do."

I head out through the mudroom onto the porch. Salt wind hits my face, and the breakwater buoy clangs somewhere out in the gray. My Civic sits beside Graham's Audi, small and rusted against the wet pines, with a fresh smear of needles across the hood. I get in, shut the door, and sit for a second with the cold vinyl under my legs. Mist gathers along the inside of the windshield. The key turns, the engine catches with its usual rattle, and the defroster blows cold air against the glass.

Before I put it in reverse, I glance up at the second-floor bedroom window. Graham is standing right behind the glass, watching the car. I throw the Civic into reverse anyway and crank the radio up to drown out the rattling muffler shield.

# Chapter 5

Every frost heave along the narrow neck rattles the loose muffler shield under the floorboards and knocks the **B-4** key against my ribs. I keep checking the rearview mirror all the way to the mainland like Graham's palm is still pressed to that second-floor pane.

Behind me, the timber gate grinds shut. The pines swallow the glass house while my Civic's heater takes half the causeway just to push lukewarm air against the fogged windshield. At the mainland fork, I wait with my blinker ticking over wet asphalt. Graham told me to take the main harbor route because a county crew was patching a culvert washout past the lobster pound. I take the harbor road anyway. Past the Salt Point firehouse, the tree line thins over the salt marsh and my phone finally jumps from one bar to four.

Notifications dump onto the screen before I clear the curve: two missed calls from my landlord, one automated reminder from the Cumberland County clerk. At a four-way stop, I brace my wrist against the wheel and type *Kiefer Soundworks Arlington* with my thumb. Three hits load before the pickup behind me taps its horn: a spring defense-tech press release about a pending patent buyout, and a short regional item noting Kiefer Soundworks missed its third-quarter lab lease extension in Cambridge.

Then red and blue strobes fill my rearview mirror. I haven't even touched forty. I signal right, pull onto the gravel shoulder beside a tangle of bare sumac, and kill the engine. A Knox County sheriff's cruiser rolls in close behind my bumper, its light bar pulsing across the mist. By the time heavy boots grind up the gravel, my license and registration are out of the glovebox and both hands are resting on the wheel.

The deputy who steps up to my window is broad-shouldered under a navy rain slicker and clean-shaven. Before he even bends down to the glass, he takes a slow inventory of my rusted front fender and the Cumberland County sticker on my Portland plates. His badge catches the dash light, and above it is the exact same flat stare that made me fold Nina's frayed rust sweater into a cardboard box six years ago because nobody wearing a star cared enough to look past a neat report. When this one talks, a slow coastal drawl softens his edges, and a slightly crooked upper incisor shows in the dim cab light.

"Afternoon. License. Registration."

I pass the cards through the gap. Cold drizzle spits across the back of my hand. "Was I speeding, Deputy? Thought it was forty through here."

"Brake lamp's out on the right." He turns my license over twice, slow, like he's got all day to stand in the rain. "Muffler shield's scraping low, too. Tessa Calder. Long haul up from Portland in a rusted Civic."

"Short contract. Packing an estate out on Salt Point."

Deputy Cole Halstead doesn't blink. He hands my license back, hooks a forearm along the door frame, and leans in close enough that I catch the wintergreen dip tucked behind his lip.

"Kiefer's glass box." He shifts the dip in his cheek and looks past me at the empty passenger seat. "Wrote the marine report out there three weeks back. Lydia grew up climbing those ledges before Boston money ever bolted steel over 'em. Folks on this point don't love mainland help poking through a dead woman's closets just to pad an invoice."

Twenty-four hours on the peninsula. The local sheriff's office already knows where I'm sleeping. "I'm boxing winter coats and logging furniture," I say. "Not poking into anything."

"Good. Keep it to sweaters." He slides a yellow warning pad and tin clipboard out from under his slicker. "Every fall we get looky-loos creeping down private docks like a drowning's free theater. Wet granite snaps an ankle quick, and that boathouse is under county seal. Don't go testing the padlock."

*County seal.* Against my ribs, the B-4 key suddenly feels five pounds heavier. If he asks me to step out into the drizzle and pop the trunk, this ends right here on the shoulder of Route 1. He starts writing my plate number and the date across the slip, and I watch his hands. He braces the clipboard against his right hip, curls his left wrist high over the top of the paper, and drags the pen backward against the grain in a hard, hooked push.

"Swap that bulb before Friday," he says, ripping the sheet free and dropping it across my wrist. "Shame to tow a Portland plate off the causeway over a six-dollar lamp."

I take the slip. "Thanks, Deputy. I'll pick one up at the general store."

"Yeah. You do that." He taps my roof twice, walks back to his cruiser, and swings a U-turn across the double yellow toward the Salt Point causeway and the peninsula I just left. My hands shake so hard I grind first gear twice before pulling back onto the asphalt. Nobody pulls a car over for a brake lamp in a coastal fog bank unless they were already parked in the bend waiting for it. Now he's rolling back up Kiefer's private lane while everything I brought sits behind a cheap turn-bolt lock in the guest room. I should have shoved the duffel under the bed. Or brought it with me.

I drive into Salt Point harbor checking my mirror every ten seconds. The town is barely a crossroads: a brick post office and a weathered general store beside a diesel dock where three lobster boats rock against black pilings.

I park nose-out by the steps. With four bars holding steady, I pull Lydia Kiefer's vessel ID and passport number from the broker's preliminary estate tax sheet and dial Samira Patel in Portland before I lose my nerve. She still has read-only access to Atlantic Marine Underwriters' cross-border port-clearance watch. The line rings four times before a trunk lid slams on her end.

"Tell me you're calling to pay back the twenty bucks from July," Samira says, wind popping over her speaker. "Because I'm in a Target lot with melting ice cream and a busted bag strap."

"I need a quiet border-watch check on a passport and a skiff ID," I say. "Atlantic Marine's cross-border feed. Just flag me if it pings."

"Are you out of your mind? After how Harbor Mutual ended, you want me risking my login on another off-book favor for you? No. Not touching it."

"She supposedly drowned off Salt Point October fourteenth," I say, lowering my voice against the fogged glass. "Last night I found her packed weekender hidden in her husband's closet with a Bar Harbor–Yarmouth ferry tag stamped October seventeenth."

Silence on the line except for a cart rattling over asphalt. "Text me the numbers on Signal," Samira says, flat and pissed. "And if this blows back on my desk, Tess, I'm blocking you for good."

The call clicks dead. I fire off the passport number and vessel ID over encrypted text, lock the phone, and head inside under a jangling bell.

Fluorescents buzz overhead along pressed-tin panels as I grab two rolls of packing tape, a twin pack of permanent markers, and a blister pack of brake bulbs near the register. Behind the counter, a skinny kid in a Patriots hoodie drags my items across the scanner one-handed while his thumb scrolls a cracked phone screen. When the scanner misses a barcode twice and beeps like a dying smoke detector, I nod at it and say, "Sounds like my car." He doesn't look up once. Beside him, a corkboard is layered thick with laminated tide tables and curling lost-dog flyers.

The register chimes. Behind me, the door bell jangles again.

A draft of harbor fog follows the new customer inside. I turn by a wire rack of work gloves and run straight into Maren Lindqvist. Up close without fifty yards of fog between us, she looks harder: deep weather lines carved around pale blue eyes, silver braid tucked under a wool cap, rubber boots caked in dried mudflat silt. She carries a dented tin of roll-up tobacco in one hand.

"Blow coming Friday," she says, loud enough for the kid at the register as she sidesteps past me to the coffee urns. "Spring tide'll top the culvert if it swings east."

I keep my voice low. "I'll watch the road."

She draws a paper cup at the urn, snaps a lid on, and blocks the convex mirror over the counter with her shoulder. "Heard Kiefer hired down from Portland. Smart move? Cash the check. Hit Route One before dark."

"You held up two fingers on the mudflat this morning," I murmur, pretending to check the seams on a pair of leather work gloves. "Who else was down at that boathouse?"

Maren cuts her eyes toward the clerk, then drops them to her cup. "Not here. Oyster lease comes up in January. Halstead's boys board my brother-in-law's boat soon as anybody runs their mouth. Too deep in winter dockage to tangle with a badge over summer people."

"Then why wave me off from the cove?"

She yanks a fistful of brown paper napkins from the counter dispenser. Her cold, salt-cracked knuckles brush the back of my hand, and she jerks back fast. "Hate watching city boots hit slick rock blind," she mutters. "That's all you're getting. Don't trust Kiefer on when that squall hit. Don't put your back to Cole Halstead neither."

The clerk clears his throat behind me and drops my receipt into a brown paper bag on the counter beside the tape rolls. "Eighteen forty-two, miss."

Maren doesn't wait. She steps past my open bag to drop a crumpled five beside the napkin dispenser, her free hand grazing the top edge of the sack for a split second before she shoulders out the door. I pay, grab my bag, and hurry onto the porch just in time to see her pickup rattle away up the harbor hill. Back in the Civic, I blow a long breath into the cold cab until the windshield clouds over and toss the bag onto the passenger seat, where the cheap pine air freshener dangling from my mirror swings against the glass. A corner of white card stock catches my eye inside, wedged between the tape rolls and the brake bulbs. The kid at the register only threw my receipt on top.

I pull the card out and go still. It's a torn strip of plasticized marine tide-chart card—the same coated stock pinned by the condiment counter where Maren stood. Someone tore off the October column, circled *Oct 15* at the top, and boxed a single line in carpenter's pencil:

*2:14 AM — High Water.*

Underneath the tide mark, spiky block letters press hard into the card:

*Ask him why his dock lights were off.*

I fold the strip twice and slip it into my jeans watch pocket, separate from the B-4 key. Cole Halstead drove back toward Graham's gate fifteen minutes ahead of me, and I still haven't swapped the busted brake bulb sitting on my passenger seat. I turn the key in the ignition anyway, wondering how much money it actually takes to make a smart person drive back into a trap.

# Chapter 6

*Ask him why his dock lights were off.* The sentence keeps turning over in my head while I steer between two slick wheel tracks of gray water, trying to picture how to drop it into small talk over dinner without sounding like I've been talking to the neighbors.

By the time I climb back onto the Salt Point causeway, the wind has swung east just like Maren said it would. Rain drives sideways across the asphalt while surf slams into the granite culvert and throws spray over my hood like gravel. I punch the four-digit pin at the timber gate, my cuff soaking through while the barrier groans back, and let my headlights sweep the gravel turnaround.

Graham's Audi sits under the evergreens. Fresh, deep-lugged tire tracks cut a wide loop through the wet gravel where a heavy vehicle pulled up to the house, swung around, and headed back down the lane before I made it home from the harbor.

I cut the engine and grab the paper bag from the general store. As I head up the wet walk, B-4 rides flat and heavy against my ribs.

The front door isn't locked. It swings in as soon as I turn the handle, letting out warm kitchen air. Through the archway, Graham stands at the range and stirs something pale in a heavy saucepan.

"You're drenched," he calls over his shoulder. "Did you hit the washout?"

"Took the harbor route like you said." In the mudroom, I hang my dripping jacket on a peg with the breast pocket turned to the wall. "Got heavy tape and markers. Kept the receipt."

"Leave it on the counter." He turns the burner down and watches through the arch while I unlace my boots beside the tray. "I made leek soup. Eat before you get back to work. Boston counsel called while you were out, and they moved the preliminary filing window up to Thursday night."

I step into the kitchen and stop by the island. "Thursday?"

Twenty-four hours gone, right in the teeth of a November storm. "This morning you said you had joint signing control over Lydia's trust," I say. "Why does one day matter if you've got a normal probate window?"

His ladle stops against the rim of the pot, though he doesn't turn from the stove. "Because her father wrote a felony-forfeiture clause into the original instrument—standard fiduciary language on old family trusts, nothing exotic—except if her brother in Zurich, or anyone local with an axe to grind, gets a prosecutor to float a criminal charge before our preliminary inventory seals, my joint signing authority suspends automatically."

"No grace period?"

"None. Well—normally there's a thirty-day administrative cure on routine accounting disputes, sure, which is standard, except under the felony trigger that window evaporates on the spot. The Arlington buyer sees a frozen collateral line, they walk, and I'm locked out overnight." He ladles soup into two bowls without asking if I want any. "Which means we work late tonight. Grab a spoon, Tessa. Let's go over the basement exclusions and the mudroom gear."

Before sitting down, I tell him I'm dropping the bag in my room.

Down the north hall, my guest room door stands unlocked the way I left it, the privacy bolt open. My duffel rests on the luggage rack. I crouch beside it in the dim lamp light and look at the zipper.

The loose lining thread I hooked across the teeth last night is gone.

I yank the zipper back and reach inside. The five-thousand-dollar Portsmouth bank band is still there, all fifty hundreds untouched. Next I slide a hand into the side sleeve onto my laptop. The aluminum lid feels faintly warm along the battery edge.

I shut it all the way down before leaving for town. A warm battery could be a fluke. A missing thread isn't. Whoever went through my bag left the cash alone and went straight for the laptop, though my notes are locked behind a twelve-character passphrase. Still, somebody stood right here in my room while I was down at the harbor, and the idea of them going through my things makes my skin crawl.

I zip the duffel shut, leave the thread off, and shove the paper bag out of sight behind the radiator.

Back in the kitchen, gusts buffet the ocean glass and rattle the aluminum frames. Rain sheets down the dark panes, blotting out the cove.

I sit at the island with a bowl of leek soup I can barely taste while Graham stands at the sink rinsing a cutting board like we're just two people waiting out a normal storm. Maybe I did leave the laptop on sleep mode when I rushed out to beat the fog. No—I remember waiting for the little white screen to go black because the battery barely holds an hour unplugged, and even if I'd botched the shutdown, a MacBook doesn't reach out and pluck a loose lining thread off its own duffel zipper. Whoever stood in my bedroom didn't touch the five grand in Portsmouth hundreds, which somehow bothers me more than if they'd stolen it. You take cash when you're desperate; you leave cash alone when you're looking for what someone knows. The soup is actually good, buttery and hot, and my spoon clinks too loud against the ceramic because my wrist still won't quite settle.

"Did Deputy Halstead stop by while I was in town?" I ask.

"Why?"

"Fresh heavy-lug tracks in the turnaround."

"He checked the gate keypad."

"Didn't come inside?"

"Somebody reported a loose dinghy drifting near the outer rocks this afternoon. He didn't even get out of the gravel."

Funny. Halstead hadn't mentioned a stray dinghy an hour ago with his arm hooked over my car door.

I swallow a spoonful of broth and nod toward the mudroom. "Speaking of marine gear, your probate spreadsheet has a line for safety equipment and boats. There's an orange life vest hanging on peg two above the boot tray. Want that logged under Lydia's personal gear or the house emergency kit?"

Graham glances toward the archway, the quiet grief sliding back over his face. "Leave it on the peg for now. The Coast Guard pulled it off the cove rocks the morning after the squall and brought it back once they logged the hull number. I couldn't throw it away, and I didn't feel like boxing it up with her coats either."

"Got it. Mind if I grab the manufacturer tag and serial number off the lining? Boston counsel likes serial numbers on marine items."

"Go ahead." He turns back to the sink and lets the tap run again. "Just don't take it off the property."

I slide off the stool and walk into the mudroom, loud enough for him to hear.

The vest hangs squarely on peg two right over Graham's duck boots. Up close under the entry light, it looks wrong. I lift the chest strap with two fingers. The orange canvas is stiff, the stitching crisp and new, the reflective tape clean right down to the edges, and even the stainless buckle rings are bright and dry.

I open the collar lining and lean in. It smells like tap water. Clean, flat, nothing like the bay. Somebody could have rinsed a vest in a laundry sink or hung up a spare from a boat locker, so my nose alone won't win an argument. I kill the flash on my phone, angle the lens inside the collar, and take two tight shots of the Coast Guard approval tag, serial number, and lot stamp to check against recovery logs later.

Behind me, the kitchen faucet shuts off.

"Get the serial number?" Graham asks from the archway, a damp dish towel balled in one hand.

"Got it." I let the vest drop and turn around. "Graham—can I ask you one thing about the night of the fourteenth? Just so my notes line up with the Coast Guard timeline if Boston asks."

He leans a shoulder against the doorframe. "What about it?"

I watch his eyes. "You said Lydia went down to the cove around ten-thirty to check the mooring line. Last night the turnaround floodlight was dead, and you told me to stay off the cliff stairs after dark. Were the dock lights on that night? Or did the squall knock the power out before she went down?"

He folds the damp towel into a neat rectangle, squares the corners twice, and lays it flush with the edge of the counter before he answers.

"We cut the dock floodlights at the sub-panel past mid-October—well, usually right after Columbus Day, depending on when the first southeast blow hits," he says, talking a hair faster than before. "Salt mist trips the exterior ground-fault loop every time spray gets into the conduit, and the electrician out of Camden never sealed that junction box right back in 'twenty-four, plus Lydia hated the halogen glare bouncing off the glass wall when she was sketching at night. Look, if you need the circuit specs for the property schedule, I'll pull the diagram tomorrow, though honestly I wouldn't waste Boston counsel's time itemizing exterior breakers."

A wet slam of wind shakes the whole house, and the ceiling lights flicker twice and die. The kitchen, the mudroom, and the ocean glass go black all at once. Just past the end of the counter, inside the narrow corridor leading to the pantry, the smart-home wall tablet stays lit on its backup battery and throws a pale blue square across Graham's cheekbones. Both of us stay still in the dark. Rain hammers the roof and drowns out the buoy bell while the low mechanical hum beneath our feet goes dead silent.

"Stay put," Graham says, sharp. "The diesel generator takes a few seconds to kick in."

I stay where I am, eyes locked on his silhouette in the blue wash. If the grid's down across Salt Point, nobody calls out without a hardline.

Outside, a heavy thump vibrates through the foundation as the generator catches. An engine rumble rises through the floorboards, and the ceiling lights flicker back on, dim at first before they hold.

Graham starts past the island toward the pantry corridor to clear the power-loss alert, and I follow him to the edge of the counter. A yellow service tag is wired to the conduit above the panel: `EXT-DECK BYPASS`. Across the bottom of the screen, a green line reads: `40-MIN HOLD STANDBY`.

Graham reaches for the glass, half a second too late. Right before the display resets to **BACKUP POWER / ARMED**, a red-trimmed amber banner flashes across the top third of the screen:

**MOTION DETECTED — BASEMENT ZONE 4 (PERIMETER BYPASS ACTIVE)**

Graham's thumb hits the glass and wipes the banner gone. He keeps his back to the kitchen as he taps twice to dim the display.

"Power surge trips the old basement sensors," he says, though the skin across the back of his neck has gone dark red. "Happens every November storm. Finish your soup, Tessa. We start the master closet at eight sharp."

I pick up my spoon and take one cold bite of soup just to watch the color stay in his neck.

# Chapter 7

Graham pauses inside the front entry, dark suit pressed and leather briefcase already locked in the Audi's trunk, and taps a temporary guest invite onto his phone. A second later, mine chimes with a Kiefer Soundworks home-access link, valid until five, with gate and delivery-door feeds while he's in Portland.

"Skip the kitchenware today," he says, and turns his wool coat collar up against the drizzle spitting off the cove. "Stick to the master closet and Lydia's vanity. I need all of it shot and logged before tomorrow night."

"I'll stay on schedule." I lean a shoulder against the archway. "Boston confirmed Thursday?"

"They're sending a courier tomorrow evening once I upload the preliminary ledger at five." He looks up at me under the entry light, eyes tired and hard. "Text me before you leave the property. Wind's supposed to peak around noon, and I don't need you stuck on the causeway if high water tops the culvert."

"Not going anywhere."

The second his taillights clear the timber gate and the barrier clunks shut, the house settles into its daytime noise: crawlspace dehumidifier rumbling underfoot, wind ticking pine needles against the glass. Half the day with the place to myself.

The relief doesn't even last until the kettle would've boiled. I could walk out right now, except I can't, and standing in the hall staring at the stairs isn't going to move the ledger any faster. I'm already behind before eight in the morning.

My sweater catches on a rusted bracket and rides up my ribs while my sneakers scrape down the masonry. I don't wait for a second swing. I yank the knife free, drive forward under her raised arm, and slash hard across her parka sleeve.

"Tessa!" Lydia screams behind me. Cole fires one blind shot into the foam; the crack is deafening in the concrete box.

Inside Zone 4, Cole's boots slam against the door, his shouts muffled to a dull roar by the foam. With a slashed left wrist, he isn't climbing a vertical iron shaft anytime soon. I don't stay to listen to him kick the steel.

Lydia doesn't turn to fight on the stairs. She scrambles upward ahead of me in the dark, one hand clamped over her bleeding sleeve, the backup drive clutched against her ribs. Her wet boots slip on the middle landing, and she catches herself against the drywall with a smeared palm before lunging on.

I scramble up the steps behind her. My sneakers skid on the top landing before I shoulder the stairwell door open into the hall.

Freezing rain pours into the entry and drenches her head and shoulders in a second as she grabs for the wet roof framing. Wind howls down the opening, whipping her hair across her eyes and scattering cold spray across Graham's face. The gale fills the entry so fast the stairwell door slams back against its stop.

# Chapter 22

I drop the oyster knife, lunge onto the coat bench, and grab Lydia's collar before she can haul herself up onto the wet cedar shingles.

"Let go!" she shrieks, and kicks backward. Rain pours through the open skylight above us and turns the front entry stone slick under strobing red and blue light, her torn jacket sleeve dark with blood as she tries to hook a boot onto the roof frame.

Her boot heel catches my left knee hard and nearly buckles my leg. I don't let go. I drop my weight backward, the bench tips out from under us, and both of us hit the stone together. Lydia's elbow cracks against the floor, and the backup drive shoots out of her grip and skids across the hall toward the stairs.

Graham doesn't lift a finger to help her. He just stands back against the wall in the flashing light and watches the woman who tried to bury him thrash on the floor.

"Stay off that drive," I pant. I pin Lydia's good wrist to the stone with my knee. "Don't move."

Heavy boots hammer up the granite steps outside. A bright flashlight cuts through the glass sidelight beside the door, followed by three hard knocks that rattle the frame.

"Maine State Police! Open the door!"

I dig the brass key out of my jeans and toss it to Graham. "Unlock the deadbolt. Now."

Graham catches it, hesitates, then turns and works the double-keyed lock beside the sidelight. The door swings wide. Wind and pine needles blow in behind two Maine State Troopers in dripping yellow slickers, weapon lights up and pistols at the low-ready. Outside, a third officer jogs up the gravel from a State Police tactical 4x4 parked nose-in behind my Civic.

"Hands up! Both of you, off the woman on the floor!"

I lift both hands and step back off Lydia's wrist. The lead trooper drops down and snaps steel cuffs behind her back.

"That drive by the stairs came off the study tower," I tell him, nodding toward the tiles. "And keep everybody out of the upstairs studio. There's a silver Montblanc on the desk that needs latent prints before anyone touches it."

Lydia glares up from the floor, blood smeared across her cheekbone where her sleeve brushed her face. She keeps her mouth shut.

The second trooper sweeps his light over Graham, then toward the open door under the stairs. "Anybody else in the house?"

"Deputy Cole Halstead." I point down the hall. "Locked in the basement darkroom behind the steel fire door. Armed, slashed left wrist. The exterior deadbolt's thrown from this side."

I back clear of the stairwell so the backup trooper can move past. "Cover the spice-rack wall in the walk-in pantry too. There's an old dumbwaiter shaft behind the shelves that drops right into that room."

He gives one short nod, racks his shotgun, and moves into the dark hall.

***

Twenty minutes later, generator floodlights off the State Police rescue rig wash the gravel turnaround in harsh white glare. I stand under the rear awning of a Knox County ambulance with a scratchy wool blanket over my wet sweater and my duffel, pulled from behind Graham's study bookcase once the troopers cleared the hall, slung heavy against my hip as two troopers walk a cuffed Cole Halstead out the front door toward a waiting cruiser. Fresh white gauze wraps his left forearm. He keeps his eyes dead ahead, ignoring both the SUV where Lydia sits and me.

Maren Lindqvist stands beside the rescue rig's idling fender in her yellow oilskin bib with a thermos pressed against her chest. Her silver braid is plastered wet to her collar.

"Parked my pickup on the high mainland shoulder before the surge, then walked the shoreline path to check my skiff's storm chain," she says as I step out from the awning into the drizzle. "Found your gouged tide card jammed behind my shed latch. Saw that and Cole's dark cruiser wedged sideways across the causeway, so I drove straight to Thomaston. Dispatch couldn't raise him on county radio. Troopers let me ride back across the washout in the storm rig to point out the lane."

"Thank you," I say, my throat raw from salt water and rust dust. "If you hadn't—"

"I took her money once," Maren cuts in. She glances toward the tinted glass of the lead SUV. "Wasn't letting her bury two people so I could keep my dock."

She unscrews the lid of her thermos, pours a small metal cup of tea, and holds it out to me without asking. I wrap both hands around the warm metal.

A plainclothes State Police detective steps away from the open rear hatch of the tactical rig with a clipboard and an evidence box. He's lean, gray at the temples, his badge clipped to the belt of a soaked nylon windbreaker.

"Ms. Calder. Detective Sergeant Ward," he says, flipping open a rain-spotted pad. "Techs secured the Zone Four server. The boathouse recording survived, and state techs pulled the router log showing Lydia Kiefer's iPad killed your internet from inside the cove right before Halstead hit the gate. We've also pulled Halstead's cruiser off the causeway."

"Good," I say. I drop my duffel on the rescue rig's tailgate. "There's more."

Ward pauses, pen poised over an evidence bag. "Show me what you pulled."

I pull the thumb drive wet from my jeans watch pocket, hoping the sealed casing survived the cove swim, then unzip my duffel's outer pouch and set out Cole's Tuesday warning slip beside the knotted glove finger holding the P-trap swab. "The raw overnight audio from the storm: Cole and Lydia in the sunroom and boathouse around two-fourteen. Plus Cole's warning slip as a left-handed sample, and a swab of white paste I pulled from the master bath P-trap."

Ward bags each piece with gloved fingers and waves an evidence tech over. "Have the studio team print that Montblanc before anyone moves it," he tells the tech. "And pull Halstead's October fifteenth report so documents can compare his writing against this slip and that smudged checklist on the blotter. I want his prints, his installer logs, and the two-fourteen audio lined up together."

He glances back toward the lit windows of the house, where state crime-scene techs are already sweeping the downstairs rooms, then drops his eyes back to my open duffel.

Across the wet gravel, Graham stands beside an unmarked sedan with a uniformed trooper at his elbow as another officer takes a preliminary statement. He catches my eye through the rain, waiting to see what I do.

I could leave what's buried under my spare shirts right where it is. Keep my mouth shut, take what Graham offered me over the stove, and drive back to Portland solvent instead of wondering how I'm going to pay rent next week.

I hold Graham's gaze, pull the manila envelope stamped `OCT 14 — MASTER BACKUP / DO NOT OPEN BEFORE PROBATE` out of the main compartment, and lay it on the tailgate.

"And this," I tell Ward. "Graham Kiefer's unredacted trust ledger and three pages sliced out of his wife's sketchbook. Across the drive, Graham shuts his eyes and turns his head away from the lights. He doesn't pull back when the trooper reaches for his wrists.

# Chapter 23

Fourteen thousand four hundred dollars left on the defamation suit that wrecked my career three years ago, and the county levy is barely lifted.

I stand at the kitchen counter with a mug of black coffee, looking down at two sheets of bond paper under the overhead bulb. The first is a stamped levy-release and payment-plan agreement from the Cumberland County clerk; the second is an embossed letter from the Maine Department of Public Safety restoring my private investigator bond, effective today, Monday morning. Four weeks after Salt Point, the electric baseboard heater in my Portland studio still ticks along the wall, and cold harbor air leaks around the single-pane glass.

Graham's frozen accounts stiffed me out of my ten-thousand-dollar completion fee the second the state auditor locked his estates down. All I saw out of Salt Point was the original five-thousand-dollar cash retainer the Knox County DA finally released last Tuesday. Putting four grand straight down barely convinced Harbor Mutual's lawyers to lift the bank freeze and move the remaining fourteen thousand four hundred onto a tight weekly payment plan. What stayed in my checking account covered the state bond fee and left me staring at two weeks of rent.

My left wrist still shows a pink seam under the cuff of my wool sweater where the stitches came out. Two weeks of grand jury testimony in Rockland took care of the rest—once state techs pulled the boathouse audio off the server and matched Cole's prints to the Montblanc, the Knox County DA dropped any talk of trespass over Graham's basement cabinet. Lydia and Cole are sitting in separate cells without bail, and Graham's hearing got pushed back while the state auditor digs through his trust accounts.

I skipped the press. I gave my statement, signed the chain-of-custody logs, and drove back down Route 1 before Boston reporters ever found the causeway. Even Maren Lindqvist came out clean. The state granted her full immunity as the reporting witness, and three days ago a wooden crate of Salt Point oysters turned up on my landing with a harbor receipt tucked under the twine: *Dock's paid. Keep your head down.* I spent half of Thursday shucking them over the sink with a dull butter knife and gave the rest to my downstairs neighbor before they turned.

The studio is already half-empty. My landlord's eviction notice is dead, though I'm still getting out of this drafty shoebox before the lease rolls. Two taped boxes are stacked beside the door. My duffel sits on the bare mattress, no longer packed for a midnight run, and the Civic waits at the curb downstairs with its muffler shield finally bolted down. I still owe Harbor Mutual's lawyers for years, and one missed Friday drops the levy right back on my checking account, though for this morning nobody's padlocking my door.

At ten o'clock, I have an intake interview with a regional marine-insurance adjuster on Congress Street. Standard desk audits: flooded hulls, dock claims, documented chain-of-custody. Daylight work with real witnesses and paper trails that don't end inside a soundproofed cellar. I'll have to sit across a laminate desk and explain why the state pulled my bond three years ago, and why they handed it back this morning, and try not to let him notice the salt crusted along the hem of my only clean slacks.

I'm not leaving my laptop in the car off the waterfront, so I pocket the bond letter, grab my duffel, and lock the apartment door behind me.

***

Ten minutes later, I push through the glass door of Becky's Diner on Commercial Street. Heat from the flat-top hits me first, thick with fried bacon. Two sternmen in salt-stained bibs are arguing over diesel prices at the counter, and out past the fogged front window, lobster boats idle along the working pier. Gulls turn through gray morning mist. I drop my duffel on the vinyl bench of a corner booth and slide in facing the street.

The waitress tops off a chipped mug before I ask, her pencil tucked behind one ear, and I pull out the printed marine-insurance application I brought to look over before ten. Half the questions are standard underwriting boilerplate, the kind of thing I used to fill out with my eyes closed, except my left wrist still stiffens whenever I try to write for more than a minute.

The bell over the door jingles. A woman in a tailored burgundy wool coat ignores the host stand and heads straight for my booth. Her silver-streaked dark hair is pulled into a low knot, a slim oxblood briefcase tucked under her arm, and somehow her boots don't have a speck of road salt on them. She doesn't ask if the seat is taken. She slides onto the red vinyl across from me and sets the briefcase between the salt shaker and the ketchup bottle.

"Ms. Calder," she says, her voice low and clipped. "You're harder to pin down than a probate subpoena. Your number's been going straight to voicemail since November fifth."

"Dropped the phone in a boathouse," I say. I keep both hands on my mug. "Who are you?"

"Margot Kiefer-Hall." She sets a cream business card beside my mug. No title—just a State Street address and a private line. "Managing partner at Kiefer-Hall Fiduciary."

I read the card and look up at her.

"Kiefer-Hall?"

"Distant branch," she says. "My grandfather founded the fiduciary arm in Boston; Graham's side took the acoustics patents and the coastal land. We hadn't spoken in a decade, and I have no intention of posting his bail."

She pauses until the waitress walks past with a fresh carafe and the booth behind us empties out.

"I pulled the Rockland docket," Margot says in a low voice. "Graham's frozen estate stiffed you out of ten thousand dollars. You handed over two-point-four million dollars' worth of forged trust records when you could have taken fifty thousand over a gas burner and walked away—while sitting under a Monday bank levy."

"Most people don't like getting locked in a basement," I say. "If you're here for Graham's lawyers, I already signed everything the DA wanted."

"I'm not here for Graham, Tessa." She unsnaps the latches on her briefcase. "My firm represents families whose problems can't survive a public probate docket. Quiet archiving. Discreet asset verification. Your investigator bond reinstated this morning, and Harbor Mutual still has fourteen thousand four hundred hanging over your checking account."

I keep my face still. She knows the exact morning my bond cleared and the exact balance left on my Cumberland County payment plan. That kind of reach doesn't stop at a public docket pull.

"I have an interview on Congress Street at ten," I say. "I'm done working off-book inside other people's houses."

Margot doesn't push. She just folds her gloved hands over the oxblood leather and holds my gaze.

"Ten days in northern Vermont, starting tonight if you can clear the notch before dark," she says, cold and flat. "A private mountain lodge sealed for the winter after a sudden family disappearance—except the caretaker walked off the job Tuesday without returning the keys. Inventory the estate, flag anything off before probate counsel arrives, and stay clear of the local sheriff. Thirty-five thousand for the Vermont engagement. And since my family name is on the mess in Rockland, Kiefer-Hall will pay the unpaid ten-thousand-dollar balance Graham owes you straight out of our retainer pool—forty-five thousand total. Only if you take the job."

Forty-five thousand. Enough to wipe Harbor Mutual off my back in one afternoon, replace the Civic before winter eats the floorboards, and keep a real roof over my head without begging a marine-insurance adjuster for desk scraps. I take a swallow of burnt coffee and set the mug down. It's a leash dressed up as a lifeline, and both of us know it. Inside my jacket pocket, the folded bond letter presses against my ribs, and I remember how fast three days in an isolated house turned into a locked cellar when I stopped asking who was really paying the bill.

"Pass," I say, even though the word tastes like copper. "Find somebody else."

***

Out past the fogged window, a tugboat horn groans across Portland Harbor, and the vibration hums up through the tabletop.

Margot studies me for a second. Then she reaches into her briefcase and sets two things on the Formica. First, a heavy brass skeleton key, its bow worn smooth from decades of hands, with dark pine resin clinging inside the cut of the teeth and a blank manila tag wired to the ring. Next to it, she slides a glossy color photograph across the table until its white edge stops against my mug.

I look down despite myself. A sprawling timber-and-stone lodge is buried halfway to its cedar eaves in fresh Vermont snow, dark evergreens crowding a steep mountain drive. Thin gray smoke curls out of a massive stone chimney against a gray-white sky.

"I said no, Ms. Kiefer-Hall." I reach for my coat on the hook. "Take your key back to Boston."

"The lodge has been legally empty since October," Margot says. She taps one gloved fingertip against the white border of the print. "It's clear you like a little mystery, that you don't walk past a loose thread once you see it. Turn it over."

The diner door bangs behind a pair of dockworkers, letting in a sharp draft of harbor wind. Everything I dragged myself out of at Salt Point tells me to grab my duffel, walk out into the cold, and keep walking. I didn't crawl out of an icy cove just to step into another rich family's locked house in the snow.

Still, Margot hasn't moved her hand.

Slowly, I lift the corner of the photograph and flip it over.

A black-and-white fax printout of a lined paper guest ledger is taped square to the matte backing. It's just one corner of the page, cropped close around a date stamp and a signature line.

The date reads two days ago.

In the margin beside a room number, three words are written in the cramped, back-slanted cursive I haven't seen on paper since the night my younger sister vanished six years ago. Nina's handwriting:

*Don't trust him.*

My thumb goes still against the glossy border. Across the Formica, Margot watches me with her gloved hands folded—and I don't know which is worse: that she has no idea whose handwriting she just put in front of me, or that she knew before she ever walked through the door.
