# The Wife's Inventory

### By David Seelmann

---

# Chapter 1

The Salt Point causeway runs barely above the tide line, a wet strip of asphalt hemmed in by black granite and dark water. My Civic's muffler shield rattles up through the floorboards. The wipers smear salt spray across the windshield faster than they clear it, and my hands are stiff from fighting the wheel since Brunswick.

Rusted tide markers bolted to a concrete culvert slide past the mirror. Low water tonight, exposing flats of marsh grass along the shoulder, though the mainland behind me is already gone in the fog. Off to the left, a shingle shack squats on pilings over a mudflat stacked with wire oyster traps. One yellow bulb bleeds into the mist beside its door.

My phone dropped to zero past the lobster pound and now clings to a single bar. The blue GPS line dead-ends at a private peninsula lot owned by a Delaware LLC. I pulled the corporate filing and the town tax card at six this morning outside Portland over cold coffee. Getting sued into the ground cures you of answering burner texts without checking who holds the deed.

The Cumberland County clerk's letter rides shotgun on the passenger seat. *Eighteen thousand, four hundred and twelve dollars.* Miss another installment and they levy my bank account Monday. Fifteen grand cash for two weeks of boxing up a dead woman's closets won't erase the judgment, but it stalls the sheriff and keeps my landlord off my back. Nobody drives out into a Maine fog bank in November because they're brave. They do it because their checking account has four hundred dollars and change.

Two cedar posts flank a timber gate at the neck of the drive, a brass keypad mounted on rusted pipe beside the driver's window. I crank the glass down. The rubber sticks, then gives with a dry groan, and freezing salt air hits me in the face.

I punch the code from the broker's message. Nothing. The buttons are crusted with dried salt, so I scrub them with my cuff and press harder. A sharp beep cuts the fog. Under wet gravel, the gate motor grinds and drags the barrier partway back so the Civic can squeeze through.

It shuts behind me with a heavy hydraulic clunk. I ease back into drive and creep up the lane.

Past a stand of spruce, the Kiefer house comes out of the mist low against the cliff. The tax card listed a seventies cottage on this lot, Lydia's family's old place, and whoever rebuilt it bolted modern glass and dark steel right onto the original stone foundation and chimney. All the windows facing the cove are dark except for one amber strip tucked under the eaves. The floodlight pole over the turnaround is dead.

I pull in beside a charcoal Audi sedan and kill the engine. It ticks in the quiet. Out past the breakwater, a buoy bell clangs twice, dull and far off.

My duffel's worn zipper catches my thumb as I drag it off the passenger seat. I push the door locks down by hand and cross the wet slate walk to the landing. Something sharper cuts through the damp wood: fresh linseed oil, maybe deck sealer, except the planks under my boots are bone-dry.

Before I can knock, the smart-lock deadbolt snaps open on its own.

The black door pulls back. Graham Kiefer stands in the doorway with one hand on the steel handle. He's thinner than he looked in his spring magazine profile, gaunt under pale gray eyes, the cuffs of his charcoal sweater fraying at the wrists. He takes in my damp hair, my scuffed duffel, then looks past me at the Civic.

"Ms. Calder," he says. His voice has that worn-down steadiness of someone who has given the same timeline to Coast Guard officers and insurance adjusters twenty times running. "You beat the tide."

"Barely. Your keypad's caked in salt."

"Everything out here rots if you turn your back on it." He steps aside and lets me carry my own bag. "Come in before the damp gets into the hall."

***

The front entry is a low, chilly mudroom vestibule tacked onto the main two-story wing, stale with unscented soap and wrapping past a utility sink toward a side door by the garage bay. A coat bench and tall closet line the inner wall. Above the bench, rain taps on a skylight hatch set low into the pitched roof—eight feet up, tops—the only bare glass left uncovered above the metal shutter boxes mounted over the lower windows.

Through the archway, the main floor opens up. A modern kitchen with a pale limestone island takes up the right side of the room; to the left, an open oak staircase climbs to the second floor, with a ground-floor hallway tucking behind it. Straight ahead, past the dining room, a massive wall of ocean glass frames black water and churning foam below the cliff. It throws my own tired face back at me over the drop.

Graham walks me past the island and stops at a narrow maple table near the stairs. A thick manila folder lies centered on the tabletop beside a cheap black rollerball, its edges squared to the grain.

"Tea?" He nods toward an electric kettle with its cord coiled by the sink. "Chamomile, or black? I forget to bump the heat until the fog moves in."

"Water's fine later." My damp jeans stick cold to my knees as I drop my duffel beside a chair. "Your broker said five thousand up front. Ten on day fourteen."

"All the way from Portland, and we're talking cash before you take your coat off." Dry, seeing if I'll blink. He pulls an unsealed white envelope out of his sweater pocket and lays it next to the folder, flap tucked in. "Count it after you sign."

"I'll count it first." I shrug off my damp waxed jacket, hook it over the chair back, and open the folder.

Six pages of dense type. Non-disclosure, non-disparagement, a blanket ban on photographing hardware or records on the property, and a fifty-thousand-dollar liquidated damages clause bolded near the bottom.

*Fifty thousand.* Whoever drafted this pulled my Harbor Mutual judgment and knew a second suit would bury me for good. For half a second I think about grabbing my bag and seeing if I can beat the tide back across the causeway.

Then I picture the clerk's envelope sitting on my front seat. I stay in the chair.

"Tell me something." Graham leans against the counter. "Your broker said you worked commercial fraud claims before moving to estates. Why pack closets?"

He already knows Harbor Mutual blacklisted me for refusing to sign off on a crooked Commercial Street fire manifest without chain-of-custody. He just wants to watch me say it.

I keep my eyes on page four. "Less paperwork. Nobody drags you into superior court over boxed wool sweaters. Though fifty grand in liquidated damages is steep for old clothes."

"That clause isn't for her clothes." He crosses his arms. "My company's buyout filings and proprietary records are in this house. If a contractor leaks a page, or runs to local papers or county deputies with half-baked stories before probate clears, my Arlington buyer walks. Whatever you see here stays inside these walls until Friday."

"It pays cash." I tap the pen against the margin. "Clause four bars removing personal correspondence or digital media. If a thumb drive falls out of a coat pocket, where does it go?"

He watches my hand instead of my face. "Into the green archival totes in the study. Unopened. My wife's attorneys are coming up from Boston Friday to file the probate petition."

I look up. "This Friday? Your broker told me fourteen days."

"Fourteen days is the full pack-out and charity haul." He nudges one corner of the white cash envelope until the paper sits dead square with the wood grain. "Clothes, kitchenware, guest linens, studio canvases. Probate counsel just wants an itemized preliminary ledger by Friday at five so the court can seal the inventory before the weekend. Can you hit Friday?"

Four days to catalog a two-story glass house, cooped up with a man who can't stand a crooked envelope on his table. "Depends how much she kept."

"Less than you'd think." He glances toward the dark ocean glass where both of us hang pale over empty water. "Lydia wasn't sentimental about things. Just meticulous."

I flip back to page two and tap clause three with the pen cap. "Fix this before I sign. Your agreement bans all photography on the premises, yet probate counsel expects condition records on high-value items. I'm not stepping under a fifty-thousand-dollar breach clause without proof of what I logged, and what was already damaged before I walked in. Write in an exception for timestamped estate-verification photos."

He studies me for a long beat, weighing whether I'm worth the hassle. Then he holds out a hand for the pen. He doesn't argue; he writes a cramped line in the margin allowing timestamped inventory photographs, adds *G.K.* and today's date, and slides the folder back.

I sign *Tessa Calder* on all three lines, pull the white envelope over, and flip through the bills.

Fifty fresh hundreds strapped in a single five-thousand-dollar Portsmouth bank band. Real ink, blue security strips catching the kitchen downlights. Enough to kill Monday's levy. I tuck the envelope into my duffel's inside pocket and snag a loose thread from the lining across the zipper teeth. An old habit, in case anyone gets curious overnight.

"House rules," Graham says. He straightens up now that the ink's dry. "One: everything from the master closet, the upstairs studio, and the living areas goes onto the shared spreadsheet. Photograph anything worth over five hundred dollars before you wrap it in acid-free tissue."

"Fine."

"Two: the boathouse at the bottom of the cove stairs is locked, and it stays locked. The Coast Guard cleared it after the storm, and salt damp ruins winter storage anyway. Stay off the cliff steps after dark. There aren't any rails."

"Got it."

"Three." He nods toward the underside of the open oak stairs, where a flush wood door blends into the paneling. "Those concrete steps go down to my acoustic darkroom and prototype servers, behind a steel fire door with a brass deadbolt. Half those units are under Defense Department NDAs. Trip the perimeter latch and my security vendor in Portland logs an automatic breach alert. Your contract voids on the spot. Clear?"

"I'm here to pack sweaters, Mr. Kiefer, not steal defense patents."

"Graham." He digs into his pocket and drops a heavy brass house key onto the tabletop, its teeth worn smooth. "The porch keypad rotates codes every two days. Use the brass key for the front door. Your guest suite is down the ground-floor north hall, first left. Towels are on the rail."

Before I can ask where the green archival totes are, his phone buzzes on the counter.

He glances down. The crease between his brows tightens, then smooths out. "Estate counsel. Take ten minutes to drop your bag. My study's across the north hall from you, though I'm taking this upstairs in the master suite for a little privacy. I'll bring the Wi-Fi password down in a minute."

He doesn't wait for an answer and heads up the stairs, his wool socks silent on the oak. Halfway up, he pauses and looks back. Not at me. Following his line of sight, he's looking past my shoulder toward the front entry. Then he rounds the landing, and a heavy door latches upstairs.

***

I stand by the table while the house settles. Under the floor, a dehumidifier rumbles like an idling engine. Wind leans into the seaward glass, and the fridge compressor clicks off behind the island.

The whole drive hits me at once. Four days to Friday. Four days in a cold house where every door comes with a threat, and my stomach still remembers how fast Harbor Mutual ground me into dust the last time I wouldn't look away.

I sling the duffel over my shoulder, scoop my damp waxed jacket off the chair, and head back into the front entry to hang it up before it drips on the floors.

My boot clips the edge of the rubber boot tray beside the coat bench. A pair of men's duck boots stands on the left side, crusted with dried salt and squared up against the baseboard. The right half has been wiped bare—a clean oval where a smaller pair used to sit.

Of course he put his dead wife's boots away. Anyone would.

I pull open the tall coat closet beside the bench. The hinges don't make a sound. Four heavy men's wool overcoats hang spaced apart on wooden hangers, stiff with dry-cleaning chemical and cedar blocks. An olive-canvas weekender trimmed in brown leather is shoved into the back corner on the floor, half-hidden under the hem of Graham's longest charcoal trench coat. A gold-stamped luggage tag dangles from the brass zipper ring: *L.K.*

I stop, wet jacket still wadded in my hand.

Nineteen days since Lydia Kiefer took a skiff past the breakwater during an October squall and didn't come back. The Coast Guard called off the search days later. Her attorneys file for probate Friday.

Her travel bag isn't upstairs waiting for tissue paper. It's jammed behind Graham's overcoats three feet from the front door, canvas bulging tight. Maybe he couldn't stand looking at it in the bedroom, or maybe he shoved it out of sight and forgot.

Except a man who squares envelopes to table grain doesn't cram a packed weekender behind his coats where a hired cleaner will trip over it night one. Not unless he was in a hurry—or wanted me to find it.

Too late either way. My hand is already on the brass grip.

A tiny tarnished padlock hangs through the zipper eye, unlocked, its metal loop pushed through loose and left unlatched. An elastic cord loops a stiff paper luggage tag onto the leather handle, blue ink printed along its perforated edge, the kind ferries use for deck baggage.

I angle the cardstock toward the dim entry light. The port ink across the top is smudged: *BAR HARBOR–YARMOUTH — SLIP 4.*

Below it, the date stamp is dry and black.

**OCT 17.**

Lydia Kiefer drowned three days earlier.

Right over the main hall, a floorboard pops.

Footsteps start toward the stairs.

# Chapter 2

I bump the closet door shut with my hip just short of the latch click and hang my wet jacket on the brass hook beside the bench. By the time Graham's socks hit the bottom stair, I'm already reaching down for my duffel.

He stops in the archway with his phone against his leg and takes in my dripping coat on the hook, the closed closet door, and my face. Neither of us moves. I keep my hand easy on the strap. If he sees the hairline gap in the door, or notices the trench coat shifted inside, he keeps it to himself.

"Forgot the Wi-Fi password," he says, and holds out a torn square of cardstock with ink scrawled across it. "And you left your front-door key on the table."

"Thanks." I step over to take both, the heavy brass key cool in my hand. "Which room is Lydia's studio? If you want a preliminary ledger by Friday, I'd rather see how much is in there tonight than waste morning light."

His mouth pulls tight. "End of the upper hall, opposite the master suite. Don't start boxing anything tonight, Ms. Calder. Get some sleep. The wind off the cove gets loud after midnight."

"I sleep fine with noise."

He doesn't argue. He just leans against the archway trim and watches me haul my duffel down the ground-floor north hall until I turn into the guest suite.

First left, like he said. Small, spare, and cold. A narrow iron bed is pushed under a high window facing the spruces instead of the water, which feels like a minor mercy until I shut the door and twist the lock. Zero resistance in the bolt: a cheap privacy turn-latch anyone with a butter knife or a credit card could pop in three seconds.

I drop my bag onto the luggage rack, check the lining thread I hooked across the zipper teeth downstairs to make sure it is still intact, and pull my laptop from the side sleeve. Stale radiator heat and old cedar-chest mothballs cling to the bedspread, the dry air of a room nobody has slept in for years.

I sit on the edge of the bed, stare at the closed door for a long minute, and try to talk myself into going to sleep. That's what a smart contractor does: takes the five grand, keeps her head down, and doesn't ask why a dead woman's packed bag is stashed downstairs with a ferry tag stamped three days after she drowned. Except the dead quiet in this room pulls me back to a Vermont sheriff's office six years ago, where a deputy closed my sister Nina's binder in three weeks because nobody wanted to push past the neat version of events.

A fifty-thousand-dollar gag clause hangs over my head if Graham catches me poking around. I open the laptop anyway. Next to Graham's sterile fifty-row spreadsheet, I start an offline encrypted note on my hard drive and type four lines:

*1. Porch deck: fresh linseed/sealer smell, planks dry.*

*2. Mudroom tray: right half wiped clean, women's boots gone.*

*3. Foyer closet: L.K. olive weekender, packed taut, unlocked padlock.*

*4. Bar Harbor–Yarmouth Slip 4 ferry luggage tag: OCT 17 (Drowning reported Oct 14, 11:30 PM).*

A sharp metal tick from the radiator under the window makes me jump. Leaving an October seventeenth ferry tag tied to the grip is sloppy work for a man who lines envelopes up with table grain—unless Graham wasn't the one who brought that bag back to the house, and he only shoved it behind his coats because he didn't know who was watching the drive.

Across the north hall, the study door shuts softly. Footsteps cross the wood inside, then a desk chair scrapes back. He glanced back toward the front entry from the stairs and told me to stay out of the studio until morning. If he catches me upstairs tonight, I lose the remaining ten grand and eat a lawsuit. If I wait for daylight, he'll be hovering over my shoulder, or whatever else he doesn't want seen in that room will already be gone.

I pull off my wet boots, dig thick wool socks out of my bag, and slip my phone and the brass house key into my jeans pocket. My hands are still cold. Slightly stupid and wide awake, I ease the cheap privacy bolt back.

***

A gray wool runner covers the north hall and muffles my footsteps. Light leaks out from under Graham's study door directly across from mine, along with the steady clack of a mechanical keyboard. Every few seconds the typing stops for the rustle of paper, then picks back up.

I hug the drywall where the floorboards stay quiet, slip past his door to the main staircase, and take the steps slowly to the upper landing. At the far end of the upstairs hall, the left leaf of the studio's double doors is pinned shut and the active door stands cracked open.

The air inside Lydia's studio is cold, the bite of rectified turpentine cutting through the lemon oil drifting up from downstairs. Pale moonlight angles through a tall seaward sash window across the room and lights an easel beside stacks of canvases turned face-inward against the wall. Three wide oak flat-files line the left wall with their drawers pushed shut and an unlit brass desk lamp resting on the nearest cabinet. A heavy worktable takes up the center of the floor, with a walnut desk set back to the right.

I step inside and ease the heavy door nearly shut, leaving a crack so I'll hear if Graham's keyboard quits downstairs. No overhead switch. I pull my phone out, drop the screen brightness to the bottom, and use the dim blue glow to get my bearings.

On the worktable, a dozen sable brushes stand bristles-up in a glass jar, their metal bands catching my dim screen. Not a crust of dried paint on any of them. Next to the jar, the white ceramic palette has been scoured so hard the glaze looks cloudy. Nobody stops to bleach their tools before walking out into an October squall.

I crouch by the flat-files along the north wall and ease the top drawer out an inch, holding my breath against the drag of wood. Watercolor sheets lie stacked inside, each labeled in neat ink: *Rosa rugosa — Salt Point, Aug 2026.* *Laminaria — Cove Breakwater, Sept 2026.* Every date stops in September, every label slanting up to the right. Under a sheet of glassine interleaving, I find a folded marine chart of Penobscot Bay with penciled tick marks tracing low-water depths and boulder shoals along the Salt Point breakwater. Normal enough for someone who ran a skiff, except the folds are razor-sharp and barely handled, with the dry chemical tang of printer toner instead of salt air.

I push the drawer shut and remember a throwaway line from the broker's intake sheet: Lydia hated central air and kept her studio window cracked even in October storms so the turpentine wouldn't set off migraines. I step around the easel and push up on the painted wooden sash overlooking the cove.

It doesn't budge.

I tilt my phone light down into the track. Fresh white semi-gloss paint bridges the seam between sash and frame, cured solid without a single pry mark. Maybe maintenance painted it shut months ago. Or maybe somebody sealed it after Lydia vanished, either to kill a draft or to bury tool marks on the sill. I wipe my damp palms on my jeans and turn back to the walnut desk.

***

A thick green leather blotter covers the middle of the desktop, where a tarnished brass paperweight holds down a closed linen-bound sketchbook. An uncapped silver Montblanc fountain pen rests beside the book, its heavy black barrel catching the screen light next to a cream stationery pad with a handwritten departure checklist on top. Leave a fountain pen uncapped on a blotter and the ink dries into a gummy crust within days. Someone laid it out like a prop, or dropped it running.

I keep my fingers off the barrel. Old Harbor Mutual reflex—touch a scene before you shoot it, and defense counsel eats you alive over smeared prints. I kill the flash and snap two tight shots of the desk layout: the sketchbook, the paperweight, the angle of the pen, the ink lines on the pad. If Graham walks in tomorrow and claims something moved, I want proof of what was already here.

Then I lift the brass paperweight by a fraction and flip the sketchbook open by its bottom corner. Fine pencil studies of kelp fronds, barnacles, and beach peas fill the front half, Latin names penciled lightly underneath. Every stroke slopes forward, right-handed, steady through page forty. Toward the back, three blank leaves show faint pressure lines where someone sliced pages cleanly out along the binding with a sharp blade. Deliberate, practiced cuts meant to pass a quick glance.

I ease the cover shut and set the paperweight back on the corner a hair off its original angle without thinking, then lean over the cream stationery pad, careful not to brush the pen beside it.

Four quick items in dark dry ink: *skiff mooring line, studio shutters, cellar latch, harbor tide*. Down the left margin, a curved smear cuts straight through the first letters of every line, the kind of drag you get when a left-handed writer hooks a wrist across wet ink.

I lift the sketchbook cover one more time to check. Every ink label on Lydia's botanical sheets leans forward on a clean right-hand slant, not a smudge anywhere. Lydia wrote right-handed. Whoever jotted that checklist beside her uncapped Montblanc likely hooked a left hand across the page.

Downstairs, the steady clack of Graham's keyboard stops.

I shove my phone into my pocket and back away toward the flat-files. A stair tread creaks up from the landing. Slow weight shifts across the upstairs runner, then stops right outside the cracked studio door.

My fingers close around the brass house key in my pocket.

Out in the dark hall, the doorknob turns.

---

# Chapter 3

The studio door swings open on silent hinges and throws a pale wedge of hallway light across the floorboards. I'm already by the flat-files, my phone tucked out of sight in my pocket and one hand resting on the neck of the unlit brass desk lamp like I bumped into it looking for the switch.

Graham stops in the doorway with an empty mug hooked on his finger, still wearing the frayed charcoal sweater from downstairs. He looks past me to the walnut desk to check the blotter and the closed sketchbook, then glances down at my wool socks.

"Thought I heard a latch," he says in a low voice that barely carries over the wind against the glass. "You said you weren't starting till morning."

"Couldn't sleep." I ease my hand off the lamp so the base doesn't scrape. "And you want a preliminary ledger by Friday. Figured I'd count the flat-file drawers and see how many boxes we're short before daylight."

He steps into the room anyway at an unhurried pace, as if checking whether a drawer hangs crooked on its runners. He stops by the desk and looks down at the uncapped Montblanc and the smudged scratch pad. I stay still by the oak cabinets, waiting for him to catch the shifted paperweight, pull the fifty-thousand-dollar NDA, and march me back to my Civic without a dime of the remaining ten grand.

Instead, the stiffness goes out of his shoulders. He doesn't touch the pen or the pad. He just looks at the blotter for a second, then turns toward the window.

"Lydia hated overhead lights," he says. He stares out at the black water through the painted-shut sash. "She'd work under that brass lamp until two in the morning when she had a commission due. I left the room the way she had it for the lawyers."

"Even the brushes?" It comes out before I can stop it.

He turns his head back toward me, gray eyes unreadable in the dim hall light. "Meaning?"

"Nothing. Just tidy for an active studio." Too much. I give a tired shrug and nod toward the hall. "I'll log the canvases first thing after breakfast. Goodnight, Graham."

He stays planted in the doorway until I'm right in front of him, so I have to turn sideways past his sleeve to reach the runner. He smells faintly of cedar shavings and cold rain, odd for a man who hasn't been outside since I arrived. He doesn't follow me down the stairs toward the ground-floor guest room. Halfway down, I hear the studio door pull shut behind him, followed by the click of a key turning in a lock that wasn't engaged ten minutes ago.

Back in my room, I wedge the wooden desk chair under the cheap privacy knob before I sit on the iron bed. Five grand in my duffel won't feel real until it crosses a teller counter in Portland, and Friday feels a long way off.

***

By seven-thirty, the smell of burnt French roast and wet kelp has drifted all the way down the north hall. Low tide has stripped the cove down to black ledges and knotted rockweed under a low slate sky. Thick fog blankets the outer breakwater, swallowing the horizon until the glass wall past the dining table looks out onto white nothing. Graham stands at the island in a clean gray oxford shirt with the sleeves rolled to his elbows and pours coffee from a glass carafe into two mismatched mugs. In daylight, the hollows under his eyes look bruised.

"Black?" he asks. He keeps his eyes on the carafe as he slides a mug across the stone.

"Please." I pull out a barstool and curl my cold fingers around the warm ceramic. My boots are already laced; my phone rests in my jacket pocket with the voice-memo shortcut ready on the lock screen, though I haven't touched record. Maine is a one-party consent state, sure, but getting caught running a hot mic in a defense contractor's kitchen is an easy way to buy a lawsuit.

Graham leans back against the counter and sips his coffee while he watches the exposed mudflats. "I ordered twenty wardrobe cartons and acid-free tissue from a supply house in Rockland. They're dropping them at the gate by noon."

"Good. I'll need the study totes too, if I'm logging paperwork."

"After breakfast." He sets his mug beside a folded yellow legal pad. "How did your old outfit operate, Tessa? Harbor Mutual. When a claim felt off—say, a marine loss in bad weather—what did they actually pull?"

*Tessa.* Last night it was *Ms. Calder*. Now we're having morning coffee over marine claims.

I take a swallow of the bitter coffee before answering. "Depends on the payout. Small skiff losses under fifty grand usually clear on a harbor deputy's report and a Coast Guard incident log. Nobody pays a diver to go down in a rocky cove in November unless the hull washes up with drill holes in it."

"And if there's an estate fight?"

"Then the lawyers subpoena phone pings, bank pulls, toll transponders." His thumb presses hard against the ceramic mug handle. "Why? Is Lydia's family contesting probate on Friday?"

He looks down into his cup. "Her brother lives in Zurich. Hasn't spoken to her since their father died, and he's already leaning on the Boston trustees at Bramwell & Shaw to freeze our joint accounts until a formal death certificate clears."

"Does he have standing?"

"Enough to stall it."

"Stall what?"

"My access to Lydia's trust. I'm a joint signatory, provided everything stays in good standing and probate doesn't lock the ledger."

"How much?"

He hesitates. "Five-point-four."

"Million?"

A muscle works in his jaw. "Before you ask, no, it isn't money I can just move around. But Kiefer Soundworks has a patent buyout sitting in escrow with a defense prime in Arlington. If Bramwell & Shaw freezes the accounts before that closes, I'm screwed."

I keep my face neutral, nod like a sympathetic clerk, and watch his hands. "And a probate freeze ties up company equity?"

"Ties up all of it." The smoothness drops out of his voice. "My R&D payroll hits next Wednesday. If they freeze the trust before Friday's preliminary filing, the Arlington buyer walks, my cash dries up, and creditors call the lab lease by Thanksgiving. If the filing clears on time, the buyout closes, and I have the money to clear every company obligation before an audit ever touches it."

"Still doesn't explain why you hired me through a blind broker instead of calling an estate crew in Camden," I say. "Local outfits pack faster and don't charge cash premiums."

He looks up from his coffee, the polite host routine gone. "Local outfits talk at the general store before five o'clock. Every clerk in this county knows Deputy Halstead, and Halstead already has three reporters poking around the causeway. I needed someone with zero local ties who knows how to document property thoroughly enough that Boston counsel can't claim I hid marital assets—or missed something inside this house."

I hold his gaze across the counter. For the first time since I drove through the gate, the panic behind his eyes looks real. I know what eviction panic looks like. Even so, five-point-four million in trust money buys a much bigger motive than winter sweaters.

"What happened the night of the storm?" I ask quietly, while he's still talking. "The broker said October fourteenth. Squall came in fast?"

His expression shutters. "She took the fiberglass skiff out around ten-thirty to check the mooring line before the wind shifted. I was down in the basement darkroom running an acoustic sweep with noise-canceling headphones on. When I came up at midnight, the dock light was off and her rain slicker was gone."

"And the Coast Guard found the boat at dawn?"

"Drifting half a mile past the breakwater, oars still shipped." He picks up his mug and turns toward the back glass sliders. The gray morning light catches a small tremor in his wrist before he sets the cup down again. "Deputy Halstead logged it before noon. If you're done with your coffee, I'd like you to start in the sunroom before the supply truck gets here."

***

The back glass sliders open onto a weathered deck wrapping the seaward side of the house, the planks slick from the overnight fog. Directly below the rail, steep granite steps with no handrail zig-zag down the bluff to the dark timber boathouse squatting over the pilings. Green algae coats the lower risers where high water reaches in winter storms; one wrong step in leather soles and you're going straight to the rocks.

I tell Graham I want to check the light on the sunroom windows before pulling the inventory sheets, then step out onto the deck with my empty mug. Behind the glass, he's already wiping the spotless counter where my cup sat.

From up here, the boathouse looks weather-sound and well-kept, its cedar shingles weathered darker than the main house from salt spray. A single heavy brass padlock secures the double doors facing the cliff. A faded strip of county paper seal bridges the seam, torn cleanly in two with one loose end fluttering in the damp breeze. The side window facing the rock wall is shuttered tight from inside. If the Coast Guard cleared it three weeks ago, nobody bothered cracking a shutter to air the place out.

Low tide leaves the cove smelling sharply of exposed mussel beds. Out past the rocky ledge, the fog parts over the shallow mud channel between Kiefer's cove and the next spit—the same shingle shack on pilings I passed coming over the causeway last night. The yellow porch bulb is off now, and only wet gray siding shows against the tide line.

Something moves down near the water.

Well below the locked boathouse, an older woman in a stiff yellow oilskin bib is hauling wire traps onto a weathered work skiff. A thick silver braid hangs out from under her dark wool watch cap, and even from the top of the cliff she moves with the easy speed of someone who has worked cold water her whole life. She stacks two dripping traps on the bow, wipes her forehead with the back of a rubber-gloved wrist, and looks directly up at me standing alone on the deck.

I lift a hand in a half-automatic wave, expecting her to turn back to her boat. Instead, she presses an open palm against her chest, glances left toward the dining glass where Graham's silhouette moves behind the counter, and holds two fingers stiffly out beside her hip. She holds them there until she's sure I've seen, then jerks her chin toward the locked boathouse right beneath me.

Two what? Two people? Two days? Before I can even mouth a question, she bends back to her traps and shoves the skiff off the mudflat with a wooden oar until the fog closes behind her stern.

Behind me, the glass door slides open on its track. Graham steps out onto the deck with a fresh legal pad and a metal clipboard.

"Sunroom rugs are first on the ledger," he says with a glance down at the empty mudflat where ripples are still fading across the gray water. "Maren Lindqvist keeps to herself down there. Don't waste your afternoon talking if she wanders up the beach. She's been fighting us over the cove boundary line for six years."

"Doesn't look like she's wandering up here." I step back inside before he can read my face.

Graham holds the glass door open, his hand lingering on the frame. Down by the water, the locked boathouse roof shines wet in the mist, that torn strip of county paper twitching against the dark wood.

# Chapter 4

Around noon, the supply truck from Rockland drops twenty flat-packed wardrobe cartons inside the timber gate, so Graham has to take the Audi down the lane to haul them back up.

From the sunroom window I watch his taillights disappear into the spruce. Then I open the shared spreadsheet on my laptop and type the first line: *Sunroom, hand-knotted wool rug, charcoal gray, fair condition*. If he checks edit timestamps from his phone, he'll see me working. Dull clerical work makes good cover. As long as I look like a slow clerk logging lamps and throw pillows, he won't hover.

Just off the dining room, the sunroom juts out over the cliff on the southeast corner of the house: all tall glass, a wide slider out to the deck, and cold gray glare across pale cedar floorboards. Two low linen armchairs face the cove across a slate coffee table and a broad woven wool rug. Below the bluff, the fog has thinned into damp shreds curling around the pilings of Maren Lindqvist's shack, though her skiff is gone. Just the empty wooden dock and wet stacks of wire traps.

*Two fingers.* I kneel to shoot the maker's tag under the first armchair, my knees cracking in the quiet. *Two people? Two days till Friday?*

Whatever she meant, she hadn't risked climbing the bluff to say it out loud, which told me she was scared of Graham, or scared of whoever else carried a key to that boathouse. A wrong guess here would bury me. Accuse a grieving tech founder of staging his own house without ironclad proof, and my name lands on another court docket before Friday's probate petition hits a desk.

I log both armchairs, a blackened-steel floor lamp, and a low walnut side table holding three unread monographs on Maine intertidal flora. Their spines crack stiffly when I fan the pages for tucked receipts or margin notes. Nothing inside.

Next is the gray wool rug. To log the weave and check for a maker's label on the backing, I have to turn up a corner. After last night, I'm not taking any surface in this house at face value. I grab the thick bound edge nearest the wall and haul back. It's heavier than it looks, dense and stiff-backed; the coarse weave drags across the planks and kicks dust up into the gray light. Bleach hits my nose first. It cuts through the salt air right underneath the oily sweetness of fresh boiled linseed oil.

I lean in over the bare boards. Most of the floor has weathered to honey-amber under coastal sun, except for a jagged patch along the baseboard where the grain is stripped pale and rough. Someone scrubbed it hard with bleach, then rubbed it over with linseed oil to dull the chalky scar. I glance up at the ceiling corners—just standard glass-break sensors wired along the upper window frames, no cameras—and take three close shots of the bleached fibers and oily sheen.

When I brush my bare fingertips across the pale patch, the wood still has a slight tack to it. It doesn't smear on my skin, just leaves a sticky drag that shows the oil hasn't finished curing. Right above the scar, a hairline gap runs between the baseboard and the drywall.

The smell alone doesn't date anything, since heavy wool traps fumes. But the oil still has tack under my fingertips, and the molding above it has been disturbed. Whatever happened here doesn't look nineteen days old.

Tires crunch on wet gravel outside. I let the rug corner drop back into place just as the Audi noses into the turnaround.

By the time the front latch clicks and Graham's boots thump onto the mudroom tray, I'm kneeling beside the second armchair with my clipboard tucked under my arm, writing *Armchair #2: minor sun fade on west arm* as if I've been at it for twenty minutes.

"Cartons are in the foyer," he calls from the kitchen arch, slightly winded from hauling cardboard in from the rain. "Take a break whenever you want lunch. There's sourdough and turkey in the fridge."

"I'm good for another hour," I call back. "Just finishing the sunroom before I start the master closet."

His footsteps pause near the maple table, then move toward the kitchen sink. Water runs, a glass clinks, and a moment later the stairs creak under his weight as he heads up to the second floor.

I wait until an upstairs door clicks shut overhead, then step into the main hall to check that the ground floor is empty. Through the mudroom doorway, that wiped-clean empty half of the boot tray is still bare beside Graham's duck boots.

Back in the sunroom, I drag the coffee table leg aside and fold the wool rug back farther so the whole scarred patch is open. The paint along the top of the baseboard isn't cracked, just two finishing nails near the corner sticking out far enough to catch a fingernail, as if someone pried the molding out to wipe liquid that had run underneath and kicked it back into place with the heel of a shoe.

I hook my thumbnail behind the lip and pull. Dry plaster dust sifts onto the pale boards. Down inside the dark gap between wood and drywall, something dull catches the light.

I pull my pocket multi-tool out, flip open the slotted driver, and ease the tip behind the molding without biting the paint. Leave a gouge in Graham's woodwork and my cover dies before five o'clock; I'll be back in Portland by dinner with a fresh lawsuit stapled to my eviction notice. I lever the trim out a fraction, hook the edge of whatever is wedged down there, and drag it out onto the floor.

It hits the planks with a heavy *tink*.

A rusted brass door key, green tarnish crusted into the teeth, tied to a faded red plastic tag by frayed cotton string. Someone punched **B-4** into the wide head.

I stare at it lying in the middle of the bleached patch. The grease around the collar is smeared, as if wet or gloved fingers fumbled it before it dropped behind the trim.

*B-4.* Basement—maybe the acoustic darkroom Graham swore ran an automatic electronic breach alert. Why is a rusted key to a defense vault hiding behind a freshly bleached baseboard?

***

I don't touch it with bare skin. I pull a square of acid-free tissue from the sample pack Graham left me this morning, fold it around the brass head, and keep it cupped in my hand while I study the corner where it fell. Graham wouldn't leave his own basement key behind a five-minute pry job under a rug he knew I had to inspect. Someone else had fumbled it while scrubbing this floor—unless he planted it there just to see what I'd do.

I smooth the rug back down, set the coffee table leg right back into its dents in the wool, and walk out to the main hall.

The glass walls suddenly feel like a specimen jar. Every pane throws my own reflection back at me while the house stays dead quiet overhead. I can't just wait around in here for Graham to wander downstairs and hope the room explains itself. I need twenty minutes off the peninsula: a real cell signal to pull a background check on Kiefer Soundworks, a couple rolls of packing tape, and a look at Salt Point harbor in daylight where nobody is watching me through plate glass.

In the mudroom I grab my purse and car keys off the shelf, pull my waxed jacket down from the hook, and slide the tissue-wrapped key into its inside breast pocket before shrugging the coat on. The hard brass presses against my ribs through the lining.

I'm turning back toward the front door when boots cross the floorboards overhead.

"Tessa?" Graham calls down from the upper hall. "You heading out?"

I step back a pace into the archway where he can see me and let my arms hang loose so I'm not shielding my chest. "Out of heavy packing tape," I call up. "And there were only two rolls of label stickers on the counter. Figured I'd hit the general store past the causeway before the fog closes in."

Steps move along the upstairs runner until Graham leans over the oak railing to look down into the main hall. He glances at my jacket pocket, lingers there a beat too long, and meets my eyes.

"Thought you were doing the master closet after lunch," he says.

"Can't box sweaters without tape."

He studies me for a second before answering and taps his fingers against the rail. "Skip the coastal road past the lobster pound," he says. "County crew's patching a culvert washout. Take the main harbor route, and keep your receipt. I'll reimburse supplies Friday."

"Will do."

I turn into the mudroom, pull the heavy black door open, and step out onto the porch. Salt wind cuts across the landing, carrying the dull clang of the breakwater buoy. My Civic sits beside Graham's dark Audi, small and rusted against the wet spruces. I unlock the door and slide behind the wheel.

Before I turn the key, I glance up at the tall glass panes of the second-floor master bedroom.

He didn't go back inside. He's standing right at the glass with one palm pressed flat against it, his gaze fixed on my car while gray fog curls under the eaves behind him.

# Chapter 5

My Civic's heater takes half the causeway just to push lukewarm air against the fogged windshield. Behind me, the timber gate grinds shut and the spruces swallow the glass house, though my shoulders stay tight all the way to the mainland like Graham's palm is still pressed to that upstairs pane. Every frost heave along the narrow neck rattles the loose muffler shield under the floorboards and knocks the tissue-wrapped brass **B-4** key against my ribs.

At the mainland fork, I wait with my blinker ticking over wet asphalt. Graham told me to take the main harbor route because a county crew was patching a culvert washout past the lobster pound. I take the harbor road anyway. Past the Salt Point firehouse, the spruce canopy thins out over the salt marsh and my phone finally jumps from one bar to four.

Notifications dump onto the screen before I clear the curve: two missed calls from my landlord, one automated reminder from the Cumberland County clerk. At a four-way stop, I brace my wrist against the wheel and type *Kiefer Soundworks Arlington* with my thumb. Three hits load before the pickup behind me taps its horn: a spring defense-tech press release about a pending patent buyout, and a short regional item noting Kiefer Soundworks missed its third-quarter lab lease extension in Cambridge.

Then red and blue strobes fill my rearview mirror and cut bright across the fog. I haven't even touched forty. I signal right, ease onto the soft gravel shoulder beside a tangle of bare sumac, and kill the engine. A Knox County sheriff's cruiser rolls in close behind my bumper, its light bar pulsing across the wet alder branches in the mist. I pull my license and creased registration out of the glovebox and rest both hands on top of the steering wheel while heavy boots crunch up the gravel.

The deputy who steps up to my window is broad-shouldered under a navy rain slicker and clean-shaven. Before he even bends down to the glass, he takes a slow inventory of my rusted front fender and the Cumberland County sticker on my Portland plates. His brass badge catches the dash light, and above it is the exact same unbothered stare I watched across a Vermont counter six years ago right before they closed Nina's binder. When this one talks, a slow coastal drawl softens his edges, and a slightly crooked upper incisor shows in the dim cab light.

"Afternoon, ma'am. License and registration."

I pass the cards through the gap while cold drizzle spits across my knuckles. "Was I speeding, Deputy? Thought it was forty through here."

"Right brake lamp's out," he says. He takes his time reading the address on my license. "And that muffler shield's hanging low enough to throw sparks on dry blacktop. Tessa Calder. Long way up from Portland."

"Short contract. Packing an estate out on Salt Point."

Deputy Cole Halstead doesn't blink. He hands my license back, hooks a thick forearm along the open door frame, and leans in close; his slicker carries the damp tang of oilskin and the wintergreen dip tucked behind his lip.

"Kiefer's glass box," he says, his voice dry and unimpressed. "I wrote the marine report out there three weeks ago. Lydia grew up climbing those ledges before Boston money ever bolted steel and glass onto them. People on this point don't care much for mainland contractors poking through a local girl's things just to pad a probate bill."

I keep both hands loose on the top of the wheel. Twenty-four hours on the peninsula, and the local sheriff's office already knows where I'm sleeping. "I'm boxing winter coats and logging furniture," I say. "Not poking into anything."

"Good. Keep it that way." He pulls a thin yellow warning pad from his slicker pocket, clicks a black ballpoint against his thigh, and flips the metal clipboard onto his forearm. "We get fog tourists every fall wandering onto private launches like a drowning's weekend entertainment. Those cove steps will snap an ankle on wet granite, and the boathouse is locked under county seal. Stay away from it."

*County seal.* Against my ribs, the tissue-wrapped B-4 key suddenly feels five pounds heavier. If he asks me to step out into the drizzle and pop the trunk, this ends right here on the shoulder of Route 1. He begins writing my plate number and the date across the yellow slip, and I watch his hands. He braces the clipboard hard against his right hip, curls his left wrist high over the top of the paper, and drags the pen backward against the grain in a hard, hooked push.

"Get that bulb swapped before Friday," he says, tearing off the yellow sheet and handing it through the window. "Hate to impound a mainland car on the causeway over a busted light."

I take the slip by the corner without touching his fingers. "Thanks, Deputy. I'll pick one up at the general store."

"See that you do." He taps my roof twice with his palm, walks back to his cruiser, and swings a U-turn across the wet double yellow line toward the Salt Point causeway and the peninsula I just left.

My hands shake so hard I grind first gear twice before pulling back onto the asphalt. Nobody pulls a car over for a brake lamp in a coastal fog bank unless they were already parked in the spruce bend waiting for it. And now he's rolling back up Kiefer's private lane while everything I brought is shut behind a cheap turn-bolt lock in the guest room.

I drive into Salt Point harbor checking my mirror every ten seconds. The town is barely a crossroads: a brick post office with salt-crusted steps, a shuttered bait shop, and a weathered clapboard general store beside a diesel dock where three lobster boats rock against black pilings.

I park nose-out by the wooden steps. Before cutting the engine, I pull up the public Coast Guard missing-person bulletin for Lydia Kiefer, pair the vessel ID with the passport number listed on the broker's preliminary estate tax sheet, and send an encrypted text to Samira Patel—a marine-claims investigator in Portland who still has read-only access to the Atlantic Marine Underwriters' cross-border port-clearance watch—asking her to flag any border hit while I still have four bars. Then I lock the doors and head inside under a jangling brass bell.

Fluorescents buzz overhead along low pressed-tin panels as I grab two rolls of heavy packing tape, a twin pack of permanent markers, and a blister pack of replacement brake bulbs near the register. Behind the counter, a skinny kid in a faded Patriots hoodie drags my items across the scanner one-handed while his thumb scrolls a cracked phone screen. Beside him, a salt-warped corkboard is layered thick with laminated tide tables, church supper notices, and faded lost-dog flyers.

The register chimes. Behind me, the door bell jangles again.

A draft of harbor fog follows the new customer inside and carries the smell of wet rubber and dock diesel across the scuffed linoleum. I turn by a wire rack of work gloves and run straight into Maren Lindqvist. Up close without fifty yards of fog between us, she looks harder: deep weather lines carved around pale blue eyes, silver braid tucked under a salt-stained wool cap, rubber boots caked in dried mudflat silt. She carries a dented tin of roll-up tobacco in one cracked hand.

"Rough water coming Friday," she says. She pitches her voice so the kid at the register can hear and walks past me to the coffee urns like we're making dock small talk. "Spring tide's going to top the causeway culvert if the wind swings east."

I keep my voice low. "I'll watch the road."

She fills a paper cup with black coffee, snaps the plastic lid down with her thumb, and squares her shoulder between us and the convex security mirror bolted over the counter. "Heard Kiefer brought in help from Portland. Smart girl would cash whatever check she's holding and head back down Route One before dark."

"You held up two fingers on the mudflat this morning," I murmur, pretending to check the seams on a pair of leather work gloves. "Who else was down at that boathouse?"

Maren checks the kid at the register to make sure he's still glued to his phone, then looks down at her coffee lid. "Not in here. My aquaculture lease comes up for county sign-off in January, and Cole Halstead's marine unit boards my brother-in-law's boat every time somebody talks out of turn. I owe too much on winter dockage to go rounds with a badge over summer money."

"Then why wave me off from the cove?"

Maren pulls a wad of brown paper napkins from the counter dispenser. Her cold, salt-cracked knuckles brush the back of my hand, and she jerks back like she touched a hot stove. "Because I don't like watching an outsider walk blind onto slick rock," she mutters. "That's all you get. Don't take Kiefer's word on when the gale hit, and don't turn your back on Cole Halstead when the tide comes up."

The clerk clears his throat behind me and drops my receipt into a brown paper bag on the counter beside the tape rolls. "Eighteen forty-two, miss."

Maren doesn't wait. She steps right past my open paper bag to drop a crumpled five beside the coffee urn, her free hand grazing the top edge of the sack for a split second before she shoulders out the glass door. I hand over a twenty, wait for my change, grab my bag, and hurry out onto the plank porch just in time to see her rusted pickup rattle away up the harbor hill.

Back in the Civic, I push both door locks down and blow a long breath into the cold cab until the windshield clouds over. I drop the brown paper bag onto the passenger seat next to the courthouse letter and reach for the ignition. A corner of stiff, weatherproof white card stock catches my eye inside the open sack, wedged deep between the tape rolls and the blister pack of brake bulbs. The kid at the register only threw my crumpled receipt on top.

I pull the card out and go still. It's a torn strip of plasticized marine tide-chart card—the same heavy coated dock stock pinned by the coffee urns where Maren stood. Someone tore off the October column, circled *Oct 15* at the top, and boxed a single line in thick carpenter's pencil:

*2:14 AM — High Water.*

Underneath the tide mark, cramped block letters press hard into the coated paper:

*Ask him why his dock lights were off.*

I fold the strip twice and slip it into my jeans watch pocket, separate from the B-4 key. Outside, mist closes over the lobster boats rocking at the pier. Past the harbor bend, the causeway leads back behind Graham Kiefer's gate, where a left-handed deputy drove fifteen minutes ahead of me.

# Chapter 6

By the time I climb back onto the Salt Point causeway, the wind has swung east just like Maren said it would. Rain drives sideways across the asphalt while surf slams into the granite culvert and throws spray over my hood like gravel. Even with the defroster cranked high, fog creeps across the corners of the windshield and squeezes the road down to two slick wheel tracks between gray water.

I punch the four-digit pin at the timber gate, my cuff soaking through while the barrier groans back. When I pull through, my headlights sweep the gravel turnaround.

Graham's Audi is parked under the spruces. There's no sheriff's cruiser, only fresh, deep-lugged tire tracks cutting a wide loop through the wet gravel where a heavy vehicle pulled up to the house, swung around, and headed back down the lane before I made it home from the harbor.

I cut the engine and grab the brown paper store bag. As I head up the slate walk, the tissue-wrapped B-4 key rides flat and heavy against my ribs.

The front door isn't locked. It swings in as soon as I turn the brass handle, letting out warm kitchen air that smells of sage and chicken stock. Through the archway, Graham stands at the stainless gas range and stirs something pale in a copper saucepan.

"You're drenched," he calls over his shoulder. "Did you hit the washout?"

"Took the harbor route like you said." In the mudroom, I slip out of my dripping jacket and keep the breast pocket turned away from the archway as I hang it on a brass peg beside the coat closet. "Got heavy tape and markers. Kept the receipt."

"Leave it on the counter." He turns the burner down and watches through the arch while I unlace my wet boots beside the boot tray. "I made leek soup. Eat before you get back to work. Boston counsel called while you were out, and they moved the preliminary filing window up to Thursday night."

I carry my store bag into the kitchen, set it on the maple table, and stop in my socks. "Thursday?"

Twenty-four hours gone, right in the teeth of a November storm. "This morning you said you had joint signatory control over Lydia's trust," I say. "Why does one day matter if you've got a normal probate window?"

His ladle stops against the rim of the pot, though he doesn't turn from the stove. "Because the trust has a felony moral-turpitude forfeiture clause," he says. "If her brother in Zurich—or anyone up here—gets a prosecutor to bring formal criminal charges before our preliminary inventory seals, my signatory rights vanish on the spot."

"No grace period?"

"None. Under that clause, any civil cure window disappears. The Arlington buyer walks, and I'm locked out overnight." He ladles soup into two white stoneware bowls without asking if I want any. "Which means we work late tonight. Grab a spoon, Tessa. Let's go over the basement exclusions and the mudroom gear."

Before sitting down, I tell him I'm dropping my store bag in my room.

Down the north hall, my guest room door stands unlocked the way I left it, the cheap privacy bolt open. My navy duffel rests undisturbed on the luggage rack. I crouch beside it in the dim lamp light and look at the zipper.

The loose lining thread I hooked across the teeth last night is gone.

I yank the zipper back and reach inside. The five-thousand-dollar Portsmouth bank band is still there, all fifty hundreds untouched. Next I slide a hand into the side sleeve onto my laptop. The matte aluminum lid feels faintly warm along the battery edge.

I powered it completely off before driving into town. A warm battery by itself could be a fluke, except the thread on the zipper didn't pull itself out. Whoever opened my bag wasn't after cash; they wanted to see what I brought, or what I'd been writing down. My local note is encrypted behind a twelve-character passphrase, so they didn't get past the lock screen. Still, I'm being watched inside a locked gate, and a dead brake bulb gives a local deputy all the excuse he needs to impound my Civic on the causeway.

I zip the main compartment shut and leave the thread out. The small outer pouch where my spare Civic key fob rides still has its brass zipper pull tucked under the canvas seam. I shove the brown store bag out of sight behind the radiator.

Back in the kitchen, gusts buffet the ocean glass and rattle the aluminum frames while rain sheets down the dark panes, blotting out the cove.

I sit at the island with a bowl of leek soup I can barely taste. My fingers stay cold around the spoon.

"Did Deputy Halstead stop by while I was in town?" I ask.

Graham sets his spoon down against the saucer rim. "Why?"

"Fresh heavy-lug tracks in the turnaround," I say, my eyes on my bowl. "Thought maybe the Coast Guard sent him out with a storm advisory."

"He checked the gate keypad," Graham says, and sets his spoon beside his napkin. "Somebody reported a loose dinghy drifting near the outer rocks this afternoon. He didn't even come inside."

Funny. Halstead hadn't mentioned a stray dinghy an hour ago with his arm hooked over my car door.

I nod and swallow a spoonful of hot broth, then tilt my chin toward the mudroom beside the coat closet. "Speaking of marine gear, your probate spreadsheet has a line for safety equipment and boats. There's an orange offshore life vest hanging on peg two above the boot tray. Want that logged under Lydia's personal gear or the house emergency kit?"

Graham glances toward the archway, the quiet grief sliding back over his face. "Leave it on the peg for now. The Coast Guard pulled it off the cove rocks the morning after the squall and brought it back once they logged the hull number. I couldn't throw it away, and I didn't feel like boxing it up with her coats either."

"Got it. Mind if I grab the manufacturer tag and serial number off the lining? Boston counsel likes serial numbers on marine items."

"Go ahead." He picks up his bowl, carries it to the sink, and turns his back while the tap runs. "Just don't take it off the property."

The orange foam vest hangs squarely on brass peg two right over Graham's salt-crusted duck boots. Up close under the entry light, it looks wrong. I lift the heavy nylon chest strap with two fingers. The shell is bright and factory-stiff: no sun fade, no fraying along the reflective tape, no trace of the chalky salt crust Penobscot Bay usually leaves behind when gear dries. Even the stainless buckle rings are bright and dry.

I open the collar lining and lean in. Tap water and damp basement concrete, without a trace of brine or kelp. Somebody could have rinsed a vest in a laundry sink or hung up a spare from a boat locker, and my nose alone won't win an argument. "Get the serial number?" Graham asks from the archway, drying his hands on a linen towel.

"Got it." I let the vest settle back against the paneling and turn around, my notepad tucked against my hip. "Graham—can I ask you one thing about the night of the fourteenth? Just so my notes line up with the Coast Guard timeline if Boston asks."

He draws the linen towel slowly across his knuckles. "What about it?"

I watch his eyes. "You said Lydia went down to the cove around ten-thirty to check the mooring line. Last night the turnaround floodlight was dead, and you told me to stay off the cliff stairs after dark. Were the dock lights on that night? Or did the squall knock the power out before she went down?"

He folds the damp dish towel into a neat rectangle and lays it flush with the edge of the limestone before he answers. His voice doesn't waver.

"We shut the dock floodlights off at the sub-panel past mid-October," he says. "Salt mist trips the exterior ground-fault loop every time the wind turns southeast, and Lydia hated the glare off the glass wall at night. If you want the circuit specs for the property schedule, I'll pull the electrician's diagram tomorrow, though I wouldn't waste Boston counsel's time on exterior breakers."

Before I can answer, a wet slam of wind shakes the whole house, and the recessed LED downlights overhead flicker twice and die. The kitchen, the mudroom, and the thirty-foot wall of ocean glass go black all at once. Just past the end of the kitchen counter, inside the narrow corridor leading to the pantry, the smart-home wall tablet stays lit on its backup battery and throws a pale blue square across Graham's cheekbones. Neither of us moves. Rain hammers the roof slate and drowns out the buoy bell while the low mechanical hum beneath our feet goes dead silent.

"Stay put," Graham says, sharp. "The diesel generator takes a few seconds to kick in."

I stay where I am, eyes locked on his silhouette in the blue wash. If the grid's down across Salt Point, nobody calls out without a hardline.

Outside, a heavy thump vibrates through the foundation as the diesel generator catches. An engine rumble rises through the slate, and a whiff of exhaust seeps past the window seals. The ceiling lights flicker back on, dim at first before they steady.

Graham starts past the island toward the pantry corridor to clear the power-loss alert, and I take two steps behind him toward the corner of the counter. Mounted right beside the gray steel breaker box, the wall tablet runs off the security hub's battery backup and cycles through white status lines as the house circuits switch over to generator power. A bright yellow service tag is wired to the conduit above the panel: `EXT-DECK / LOGS — 40M DIAGNOSTIC BYPASS BUS`. Across the bottom of the screen, a green status line shows the forty-minute service loop on standby: `LOCAL LOG HOLD WHEN BRIDGED`.

Graham reaches for the glass, half a second too late. Right before the display resets to **BACKUP POWER / ARMED**, a red-trimmed amber banner flashes across the top third of the screen:

**MOTION DETECTED — BASEMENT ZONE 4 (PERIMETER BYPASS ACTIVE)**

Graham's thumb hits the glass and wipes the banner gone. He keeps his back to the kitchen while he taps twice to dim the display.

"Power surge trips the old basement sensors," he says, though the skin across the back of his neck has gone dark red. "Happens every November storm. Finish your soup, Tessa. We start the master closet at eight sharp."

Across the main hall, tucked under the open oak stairs, the flush wood door to Basement Zone 4 stands closed in the shadows, its latch plate untouched.

# Chapter 7

At seven-fifteen Wednesday morning, Graham leaves for Portland in a charcoal suit with his leather briefcase locked in the Audi's trunk.

I stand in the main hall in my wool socks and watch him pause inside the front vestibule to tap a temporary guest invite onto his phone. A second later, mine chimes with a Kiefer Soundworks home-access link, valid until five, with gate and delivery-door feeds while he's out.

"Skip the kitchenware today," he says, and turns his wool coat collar up against the drizzle spitting off the cove. "Stick to the master closet and Lydia's vanity. I need all of it shot and logged before tomorrow night."

"I'll stay on schedule." I lean a shoulder against the archway. "Boston confirmed Thursday?"

"They're sending a courier tomorrow evening once I upload the preliminary ledger at five." He looks up at me under the vestibule light, eyes tired and hard. "Text me before you leave the property. Wind's supposed to peak around noon, and I don't need you stuck on the causeway if high water tops the culvert."

"Not going anywhere."

The second his taillights clear the timber gate and the barrier clunks shut, the house settles back into its daytime noise: the crawlspace dehumidifier rumbling underfoot, wind ticking spruce needles against the ocean glass. Half the day alone, with no Graham hovering by the island and no keyboard clacking across the north hall.

The relief wears off fast. Yesterday I had until Friday to finish this ledger, take my remaining ten grand, and get back to Portland before anybody dug into my Harbor Mutual file. Now I'm down a day, a left-handed county deputy knows my plates, and somebody already rifled my duffel while I was buying tape.

Before I touch a single sweater upstairs, I walk over to the floating oak stairs. I crack the unlocked cedar stairwell door an inch and look down the concrete steps at the heavy steel fire door of Basement Zone 4. Her silver-streaked dark hair is pulled into a low knot, a slim oxblood leather briefcase tucked under her arm, not a speck of road salt on her boots. She doesn't ask if the seat is taken. She unbuttons her coat, slides onto the red vinyl across from me, and sets her briefcase on the Formica between the salt shaker and the ketchup bottle.

"Ms. Calder," she says, her voice low and clipped. "You're harder to pin down than a probate subpoena. Your number's been going straight to voicemail since November fifth."

"Dropped the phone in a boathouse," I say. I keep both hands on my mug. "Who are you?"

"Margot Kiefer-Hall." She pulls a heavy cream business card from her coat pocket and sets it beside my coffee. No title, just a State Street address in Boston and a direct private line. "Managing partner at Kiefer-Hall Fiduciary. We issued the thirteen-thousand-five-hundred-dollar cashier's check our courier brought to your apartment last month."

I read the card and look up at her.

"Kiefer-Hall?"

"Distant branch," she says. "My grandfather founded the fiduciary arm in Boston; Graham's side took the acoustics patents and the coastal land. We hadn't spoken in a decade until he retained us six weeks ago, strictly as an independent archiving escrow agent, separate from Bramwell and Shaw."

"Graham told me he was paying out of his own pocket."

"He did." Margot rests her gloved hands on top of her briefcase. "Through a separate contractual escrow he funded ahead of the Arlington closing. When he drove down to Portland that Wednesday to collect the pre-closing packet—after moving your deadline up twenty-four hours into a coastal gale—I saw your name on the contractor voucher and added the thirty-five-hundred-dollar storm adjustment myself. Once his arrest voided the closing, the escrow terms still required release to whichever contractor delivered an unredacted accounting of the estate."

She pauses while the waitress walks past with a fresh carafe and waits until the booth behind us empties out.

"You handed over two-point-four million dollars' worth of forged trust records when you could have taken fifty thousand over a gas burner and walked away," Margot says in a low voice. "Most people sitting under a Monday bank levy take the cash."

"Most people don't like getting locked in a basement," I say. "If you're here for a receipt, the clerk's office already cleared the check."

"I'm not here for a receipt, Tessa." She unsnaps the brass latches on her oxblood briefcase. "My firm represents families whose problems can't survive a public probate docket. Quiet archiving. Discreet asset verification. Your investigator bond cleared this morning, and your court balance is zero."

I keep my face still. She knows the day my bond reinstated and she knows my county docket balance. That kind of reach doesn't stop at a Boston escrow file.

"I have an interview on Congress Street at ten," I say. I nudge my marine-insurance application toward the edge of the table. "I'm done working off-book inside other people's houses."

Margot doesn't push. She just folds her hands back over the leather and holds my gaze.

"Triple the Salt Point rate," she says. "Forty-five thousand dollars. Ten days in northern Vermont, starting tonight if you can clear the notch before dark. A private mountain lodge sealed for the winter after a sudden family disappearance—except the caretaker walked off the job Tuesday without returning the keys. Inventory the estate, flag anything off before probate counsel arrives, and stay clear of the local sheriff."

I take a slow swallow of bitter coffee and set the mug squarely back on the paper placemat. My fingers brush the folded bond letter inside my jacket pocket.

"Pass," I say. "Find somebody else."

***

Out past the fogged window, a tugboat horn groans across Portland Harbor, and the vibration hums up through the tabletop.

Margot studies me for a beat. Then she reaches into her open briefcase and sets two things on the Formica. First, a heavy brass skeleton key, its bow worn smooth from decades of hands, with dark pine resin clinging inside the cut of the teeth and a blank manila tag on the ring by a twist of copper wire. Next to it, she slides a glossy color photograph across the table until its white edge stops against my coffee mug.

I look down despite myself. A sprawling timber-and-stone lodge is buried halfway to its cedar eaves in fresh Vermont snow, dark spruces crowding the drive behind a wrought-iron gate. Thin gray smoke curls out of a massive stone chimney against a gray-white sky.

"I said no, Ms. Kiefer-Hall." I rest my palm on the marine-insurance packet and reach for my coat on the hook. "Take your key back to Boston."

"The lodge has been legally empty since October," Margot says. She taps one gloved fingertip against the white border of the print. "It's clear you like a little mystery, that you don't walk past a loose thread once you see it. Turn it over."

Everything I dragged myself out of at Salt Point tells me to grab my duffel, walk out into the cold harbor air, and keep walking. I didn't crawl out of a frozen cove just to step into another rich family's locked house in the snow.

Still, Margot hasn't moved her hand.

Slowly, I lift the corner of the photograph and flip it over.

A black-and-white fax printout of a lined paper guest ledger is taped square to the matte backing. It's just one corner of the page, cropped close around a date stamp and a signature line.

The date reads two days ago.

In the margin beside a room number, three words are written in the cramped, back-slanted cursive I haven't seen on paper since the night my younger sister vanished six years ago. Nina's handwriting:

*Don't trust him.*

My thumb goes still against the glossy border. Across the Formica, Margot watches me with her gloved hands folded—and I don't know which is worse: that she has no idea whose handwriting she just put in front of me, or that she knew before she ever walked through the door.
