# The Wife's Inventory

### By David Seelmann

---

# Chapter 1

The Salt Point causeway sits barely higher than the tide line, a narrow ribbon of wet asphalt stretched between black granite ledges and dark water. My Civic's muffler shield rattles through the floorboards. I grip the wheel until my palms ache, watching salt spray speck the windshield faster than my worn wipers can clear it.

Ahead, rusted tide-depth markers bolted to a concrete culvert slide past my mirror. The water is low tonight, chewing at flattened marsh grass along the shoulder, though the mainland already feels swallowed by fog. Down to the left, a weathered shingle shack squats on pilings over a mudflat stacked with wire oyster traps. A single yellow bulb smears against the mist beside its door.

My phone lost service past the lobster pound. Now it clings to one bar, the blue GPS route ending at a private peninsula lot listed under a Delaware LLC. I pulled the corporate filing at six this morning outside Portland over cold coffee, because getting sued into the ground makes you check who hires through burner texts.

On the passenger seat sits the latest letter from the Cumberland County clerk. *Eighteen thousand, four hundred and twelve dollars.* Another missed installment triggers a bank levy Monday. Fifteen grand cash for fourteen days of boxing a dead woman's clothes won't erase the judgment, yet it buys off the sheriff and stops an eviction. I didn't take this job because I'm brave. I took it because my checking account holds four hundred dollars and change.

At the timber gate, two cedar posts flank a brass keypad mounted on rusted pipe. I crank my window down with a dry rubber groan. Freezing brine and rotting kelp rush into the cab, thick enough to coat my tongue.

I punch the pin from the broker's message. Nothing clicks. I wipe dried sea salt off the brass buttons and press the numbers harder. A sharp beep cuts the fog, and the gate motor grinds under wet gravel, dragging the cedar barrier back just wide enough for my Civic.

Behind me, the gate latches shut with a heavy hydraulic clunk. I shift into drive. Once you pass a private gate on a one-road peninsula, you don't slip out unnoticed.

Past the spruce stand, the Kiefer house looms low over the cliff edge—modern glass and blackened steel bolted onto the fieldstone foundation and chimney core of Lydia's family's 1970s coastal house. Weathered cedar fades into mist while black-framed windows face the cove, dark except for one amber strip under the eaves. The turnaround floodlight pole stands dead overhead.

I park beside a charcoal Audi sedan and kill the ignition. My engine ticks as it cools. Out past the breakwater, a buoy bell clangs twice, flat and distant.

I haul my navy duffel off the seat—the worn zipper biting my thumb—lock the doors manually, and cross the slick slate pavers to the cedar landing. Under the wet wood smell hangs something sharper—fresh linseed oil, maybe deck sealer, though the planks look bone-dry.

Before I raise a hand to knock, the smart-lock deadbolt snaps open on its own.

The black door swings back. Graham Kiefer stands in the threshold with one palm on the brushed steel lever. He looks thinner than his spring magazine profile—gaunt under pale gray eyes, charcoal merino cuffs frayed against the glass behind him. His gaze drops from my damp hair to my scuffed duffel, then out to my Civic.

"Ms. Calder," he says. His voice carries the flat steadiness of a man who has repeated the exact same timeline to Coast Guard officers and insurance adjusters until the words feel worn smooth. "You beat the tide."

"Barely. Your keypad is crusted over with salt."

"Everything out here corrodes if you turn your back on it." He steps aside without reaching for my bag. "Come in before the damp settles into the foyer."

***

Inside, the front entry foyer is a low-slung, single-story cedar vestibule projecting out from the main two-story wing, smelling faintly of unscented soap and cold slate. Directly above the heavy coat bench, rain ticks against a low skylight hatch set into the sloping eight-foot pitched roof—the sole pane sitting clear above the exterior hurricane-shutter housings hooded over the lower window bays. Through the archway into the main house and beyond the dining room, a massive wall of ocean glass frames black water and white foam churning below the cliff, throwing my tired face back at me over the drop.

Graham leads me past a pale limestone kitchen island and stops beside a narrow maple table near the stairs. A thick manila folder sits dead center next to a black rollerball pen.

"Can I make you tea?" he asks, gesturing toward an electric kettle coiled neatly beside the sink. "Chamomile, or black? I keep forgetting to turn the thermostat up until the fog rolls in."

"Water's fine later." My wet jeans stick cold against my knees as I set my duffel by the chair. Men who offer tea over an unsealed folder want to control the pace of the room. "Your broker said five thousand up front. Ten on day fourteen."

"A long drive from Portland, and you ask about cash before taking off your coat." Dry, testing my patience. He reaches into his sweater pocket and lays an unsealed white envelope beside the folder, flap tucked in. "Count it after you sign."

"I'll count it first." I shrug off my damp waxed jacket, drape it over the wooden chair, and flip the manila folder open.

Six dense pages of legal print. Non-disclosure, non-disparagement, a ban on photographing hardware or records on the premises, and a fifty-thousand-dollar liquidated damages clause in bold ink near the bottom.

*Fifty thousand.* Whoever drafted this pulled my Harbor Mutual judgment—knew another lawsuit would bury me. My fingers twitch toward my duffel strap; one pull and I could still beat the tide back across the causeway.

Then I picture the clerk's ivory letter on my passenger seat. I stay in the chair.

"Tell me something," Graham says, leaning one hip against the counter. "Your broker mentioned you handled commercial fraud claims before switching to estate work. Why pack closets?"

He knows I got blacklisted for refusing to sign off on a fraudulent Commercial Street fire manifest without chain-of-custody. He just wants to hear me say it.

I keep my eyes on page four. "Less paperwork. Nobody drags you into superior court over boxed wool sweaters. Though fifty grand in liquidated damages is steep for old clothes."

"That clause isn't for her sweaters," Graham says, his voice level. "Half my acoustic prototypes and pre-buyout defense filings sit inside this house. If a contractor leaks company schematics—or runs to local reporters or county deputies with half-baked rumors before probate clears—my Arlington buyer walks. Keep it inside these walls until Friday."

"It pays cash," I say, tapping the cheap pen against the margin. "Clause four bars removing personal correspondence or digital media. If a thumb drive falls out of a coat pocket, where does it go?"

Graham watches my pen hand rather than my face. "Into the green archival totes in the study. Unopened. My wife's attorneys are coming out from Boston this Friday to file the probate petition."

"This Friday," I say, looking up. "Your broker told me fourteen days."

"Fourteen days covers the full pack-out and charity haul," he says, nudging the corner of the white cash envelope until its paper edge aligns squarely with the table grain. "Her clothes, kitchenware, guest linens, studio canvases. Probate counsel simply wants an itemized preliminary ledger by Friday at five so the court can seal the estate inventory before the weekend. Can you hit Friday?"

Four days to catalog an entire two-story glass house—trapped with a man who can't bear a crooked envelope on his table. "Depends how much she kept."

"Less than you'd expect," he says, glancing toward the dark glass wall where our pale reflections hang over empty blackness. "Lydia wasn't sentimental about possessions. Just meticulous."

I flip back to page two and tap clause three with the pen cap before uncapping it. "Then fix this before I sign. Your agreement bans all photography on the premises, yet probate counsel expects condition records for high-value items. I don't step inside a fifty-thousand-dollar breach clause without chain-of-custody proof of what I logged and what was damaged before I arrived. Write in an exception for timestamped inventory photos strictly for estate verification."

Graham's jaw tightens as he weighs me—then he reaches for the pen. Without arguing, he writes a single line in the margin permitting timestamped estate-verification photographs, adds his crisp *G.K.* initials and today's date, and slides the page back.

I uncap the pen, sign *Tessa Calder* across the three lines, and slide the white envelope toward me to thumb through the bills inside.

Fifty crisp hundreds, bound in a single five-thousand-dollar Portsmouth bank band. Real ink, blue security strips catching the recessed LED downlights. Enough to hold off Monday's levy. I tuck the envelope inside my duffel's inner pocket and hook a stray lining thread over the zipper teeth—a silent tell if anyone searches my bag overnight.

"House rules," Graham says, straightening now that the ink is dry. "One: everything from the master closet, the upstairs studio, and the living areas goes onto the shared spreadsheet. Photograph anything worth over five hundred dollars before wrapping it in acid-free tissue."

"Fine."

"Two: the boathouse at the foot of the cove stairs is locked. It stays locked. The Coast Guard cleared it after the storm, and salt damp ruins winter storage anyway. Don't go down the cliff steps after dark—there aren't any rails."

"Got it."

"Three." He pivots toward the underside of the floating oak staircase, where a flush cedar stairwell door blends into the wall paneling. "Those concrete steps lead down to my acoustic darkroom and prototype servers for Kiefer Soundworks—behind a reinforced steel fire door with a matte brass deadbolt. Half those units fall under Defense Department noise-cancellation NDAs. If you trip the perimeter latch on that steel door, my security vendor in Portland logs an automatic breach alert and your contract voids immediately. Clear?"

"I'm here to pack sweaters, Mr. Kiefer, not steal defense patents."

"Graham." He reaches into his pocket and drops a heavy brass house key onto the tabletop, its metal teeth worn dull. "The porch keypad rotates codes every two days. Use the brass key for the front door. Your guest suite is down the ground-floor north hall, first left. Towels are on the rail."

Before I can ask where the green archival totes are stacked, his phone buzzes once against the counter.

Graham glances down at the screen. The tense line between his eyebrows deepens for a fraction of a second before smoothing out. "Estate counsel again. Take ten minutes to drop your bag in the guest suite. My study is across the north hall from you, though I'm taking this call upstairs in the master suite for privacy—I'll bring down the Wi-Fi password in a minute."

He heads up the treads without waiting for me to answer. His wool socks make no sound on the wood. Halfway up, he pauses just long enough to glance back—not at me, I notice, tracking his angle, but toward the front foyer behind my shoulder. Then he rounds the upper corner, and a heavy latch clicks shut overhead.

***

I stand beside the table, listening to the house settle.

Beneath the slate, a dehumidifier rumbles like an idling engine. Wind flexes the seaward glass, and a refrigerator compressor clicks off behind the island.

Exhaustion hits me all at once. Four days until Friday. Four days in a cold house where every door carries a rule—trying not to remember how fast Harbor Mutual crushed me the last time I trusted my gut.

I hoist my duffel strap onto my shoulder, grab my damp waxed jacket off the chair back, and walk toward the foyer stairs.

My boot catches on the edge of the mudroom tray beside the front bench. On the left side of the ribbed rubber mat sits a single pair of men's duck boots, salt-crusted and angled neatly against the baseboard. The right half of the tray has been wiped spotless—an empty oval where a smaller pair of boots used to sit.

I reach for the recessed handle of the tall cedar coat closet beside the bench, unwilling to leave my damp jacket dripping onto Graham's staircase.

The closet door swings open on oiled hinges. Inside, four heavy men's wool overcoats hang at uniform spacing on wooden hangers, smelling sharply of cedar blocks and dry-cleaning chemical.

At the back of the closet floor, shoved behind the hem of Graham's longest charcoal trench coat, sits an olive-canvas weekender trimmed in saddle leather. Brass zipper teeth. A monogrammed leather tag dangling from the D-ring in gold foil letters: *L.K.*

I freeze with my wet jacket in my left hand.

Nineteen days since Lydia Kiefer took a skiff past the breakwater during an October squall and vanished. Coast Guard called off the search days later; probate files Friday.

Yet her travel bag isn't upstairs waiting for tissue. It's jammed behind Graham's coats a stride from the front door, canvas sides bulging taut.

I crouch slowly, bracing one shoulder against the cedar jamb. Graham aligns envelopes to woodgrain and coils kettle cords like hardware stock. Why cram a packed bag behind winter coats where any live-in cleaner would trip over it night one—unless he panicked when I pulled in early, or left it as bait?

Too late either way. My fingers are already on the brass handle.

A tiny tarnished padlock hangs through the zipper eye, unlocked, the clasp pushed loosely through without clicking shut. Tied to the leather grip by an elastic cord is a stiff paper luggage stub, blue ink printed vertically along a perforated edge—the kind the Bar Harbor–Yarmouth crossing ferries loop onto deck baggage.

I tilt the cardstock toward the light from the foyer sconce.

The port ink at the top is smudged: *BAR HARBOR–YARMOUTH — SLIP 4*.

Below it, the date stamp is dry and black.

**OCT 17.**

My thumb stops on the paper edge. Lydia Kiefer supposedly drowned at eleven-thirty at night on October 14.

Directly above the main hall, floorboards pop. Footsteps start down toward the stairs.

# Chapter 2

I slide the cedar closet door shut with my hip, leaving just enough gap for the latch not to click, and hang my damp waxed jacket on the brass hook beside the bench. By the time Graham's stockinged feet hit the bottom oak tread, I'm already stooping to lift my duffel off the slate floor.

He stops near the archway, phone still pressed loosely against his thigh. His eyes flick from my wet coat on the hook to the closed closet door, then settle on my face. Neither of us moves. I keep my hand loose on the duffel strap. If he saw the gap in the cedar slats, or noticed the trench coat hem shifted inside, he doesn't show it.

"I forgot the Wi-Fi password," he says, holding up a square of torn cardstock he must have scribbled on upstairs. "And you left your front-door key on the maple table."

"Thanks." I step past him to take the heavy brass key, keeping my posture loose while the metal bites my palm. "Which room is Lydia's studio? If you want a preliminary ledger by Friday, I'd rather map the layout tonight than waste daylight."

Graham's mouth tightens—not quite a smile, more like a man checking off an unexpected box. "End of the upper hall, opposite the master suite. Don't start boxing anything tonight, Ms. Calder. Just get some sleep. The wind off the cove gets loud after midnight."

"I sleep fine with noise."

He doesn't argue. He simply watches me carry my duffel down the ground-floor north hall, his hand resting on the archway trim until I turn into the guest suite.

The guest suite is first left off the north hall, just as he said. Small, spare, painted the color of wet chalk. A narrow iron bed sits tucked under a high casement window facing the spruce trees rather than the water, which feels like an odd mercy until I lock the bedroom door behind me and realize the brass turn-bolt slides with zero resistance. Just a cheap privacy latch anyone with a butter knife or a credit card could pop in seconds.

I drop my duffel onto the luggage rack, check the lining thread I hooked over the zipper teeth downstairs—still intact—and pull my laptop from the side sleeve. The room smells of cedar mothballs and radiator dust, untouched since whoever built this house stopped expecting company.

I sit on the edge of the mattress. The springs barely give. Somewhere under the floorboards, the same low dehumidifier hum vibrates through the soles of my boots.

For a long minute I stare at the closed door. Six years ago, my younger sister Nina vanished out of northern Vermont, and local police filed the missing-person binder away after three weeks because nobody pushed past the tidy cover story. A fifty-thousand-dollar gag clause hangs over my head if Graham catches me poking where I shouldn't, yet a dead woman's packed weekender sits downstairs with a ferry stub stamped three days after she drowned.

It sits jammed behind Graham's wool coats, brass zipper pulled taut over packed cloth, that tiny padlock hanging unlatched. If she grabbed a bag to flee a squall, why bring it back to Salt Point on October seventeenth with the Bar Harbor stub still tied to the grip?

I open my laptop anyway. Beside Graham's sterile fifty-row inventory spreadsheet, I open a local encrypted note on my hard drive—offline, unsynced—and type four lines:

*1. Porch deck: fresh linseed/sealer smell, planks dry.*

*2. Mudroom tray: right half wiped clean, women's boots gone.*

*3. Foyer closet: L.K. olive weekender, packed taut, unlocked padlock.*

*4. Bar Harbor Slip 4 ferry stub: OCT 17 (Drowning reported Oct 14, 11:30 PM).*

I stare at the glowing screen until the radiator under the window ticks sharply, making me flinch. If Graham staged a drowning to bury a bad marriage or collect probate early, leaving an October seventeenth ferry tag tied to the grip is sloppy work for a man who straightens envelopes to woodgrain.

Unless someone else brought that bag back after the seventeenth—and Graham shoved it behind his coats because he didn't know who was watching the drive.

Across the north hall, a door shuts softly—the study. Heavy footsteps cross the hardwood floor inside, followed by the scrape of a desk chair.

I slip my wet boots off, pull on thick wool socks from my duffel, and tuck my phone and the brass house key into my jeans pocket. Then I ease the cheap privacy bolt open.

***

The north hall runs along the landward spine of the house, carpeted in a gray wool runner that swallows my steps. Light spills dimly from beneath the crack of Graham's study door opposite mine, accompanied by the faint, rhythmic tap of someone typing on a mechanical keyboard. Every few seconds the tapping stops, followed by the soft rustle of paper, then starts up again.

I keep close to the drywall so the floor joists won't groan, slip past his study door toward the main staircase, and climb to the upper landing. At the far end of the upstairs hall, double doors stand slightly ajar.

Lydia's studio smells different from the rest of the house. Beneath the sterile lemon polish Graham's cleaning crew must have used downstairs, this room still holds turpentine, dried gesso, and dusty linen paper. Moonlight filters dimly through a tall seaward sash window, catching stacks of stretched canvases leaned face-inward against the plaster wall.

I step inside and push the heavy door nearly shut behind me, leaving a sliver open so I can hear if Graham's typing stops down the hall. I don't flip the overhead switch. Instead, I pull my phone from my pocket and toggle the screen brightness down to its lowest notch, using just the pale glow of the display to sweep the perimeter.

It looks like a working botanical studio frozen mid-project—until you look closer.

Three wide oak flat-files sit against the north wall, drawers shut flush. On the worktable, a glass jar holds a dozen sable brushes fanned bristles-up, ferrules gleaming spotless silver. Beside them, the ceramic palette sits chalk-white, scrubbed so hard the glaze looks dull under my phone light. Artists who drop everything for a midnight squall don't bleach their palettes first.

I crouch by the flat-files and slide the top drawer open a fraction, holding my breath against the scrape of wood. Crisp watercolor sheets lie stacked inside, each labeled in neat ink: *Rosa rugosa — Salt Point, Aug 2026.* *Laminaria — Cove Breakwater, Sept 2026.* Every date stops in September, every label slanting sharply upward to the right.

Tucked under glassine interleaving paper lies a folded marine chart of Penobscot Bay, with pencil tick marks along the Salt Point breakwater channel showing low-water depths and boulder shoals. Nothing odd for someone who ran a skiff regularly—except the chart corner is crisp and uncreased, smelling faintly of printer toner instead of salt spray.

I ease the drawer shut. Half a dozen tin watercolor pans sit on the opposite shelf arranged by pigment temperature—cobalt, burnt sienna, Payne's gray—without a single smudged fingerprint across the enamel lids. Even the rag draped over the brass lamp neck feels stiff and unwashed.

Then I turn toward the tall sash window overlooking the cove.

Before I left Portland, Graham's broker had passed along a throwaway detail from the intake notes—Lydia hated central air and kept her studio window cracked even in October storms so the turpentine wouldn't trigger migraines.

I cross past the easel leg and set both palms against the painted wooden sash. I push upward.

Nothing gives.

I angle my phone light along the inner track. Fresh white semi-gloss paint bridges the seam between upper sash and outer frame, cured hard without a single pry mark. Nobody cracked this window in October. Either the broker lied from an old intake sheet, or someone painted the sash shut after Lydia vanished to seal out the draft—or hide tool marks on the sill.

I wipe dry paint dust off my fingertips onto my jeans and turn toward the walnut desk. If this studio is staged for probate—or staged for me—the desk is where the story breaks.

***

A thick green leather blotter covers the center of the walnut table. Centered on it rests a linen-bound sketchbook, closed, with a tarnished brass paperweight holding the corner flat. Beside the book lies a heavy silver Montblanc fountain pen, uncapped, its black resin barrel catching the faint blue reflection of my phone, next to a cream stationery scratch pad topped with a handwritten departure checklist.

Leaving a fountain pen uncapped on a blotter ruins the nib within days, drying the ink feed into a gummy crust. Someone laid it out like a stage prop—or dropped it in a hurry.

I resist the urge to touch the barrel. At Harbor Mutual, you never touched a scene before you photographed it; one smeared print and defense counsel ate you alive. I raise my phone, keep the flash off, and snap two crisp shots of the desk layout—the angle of the sketchbook, the position of the paperweight, the exact direction the Montblanc points, and the ink lines on the scratch pad. If Graham walks in tomorrow and finds things shifted, I want to know whether *I* slipped up or someone else rearranged the room after me.

Then I lift the brass paperweight just enough to open the sketchbook from the bottom corner.

Fine botanical pencil studies of kelp fronds, barnacles, and beach peas fill the early pages—delicate cross-hatching, Latin binomials penciled lightly underneath. Every single stroke slopes forward, right-handed, consistent from page one through page forty. Toward the back, three blank pages show faint indentation marks where someone sliced sheets out flush along the binding seam with a sharp blade. Nothing torn in anger. Someone removed those pages deliberately and kept the edges neat so a casual glance wouldn't catch the missing thickness.

I close the cover gently, aligning the corner flush under the brass paperweight—a fraction off-angle without thinking—and lean closer to the stationery scratch pad without touching the silver Montblanc beside it.

The checklist lists four quick items: *skiff mooring line, studio shutters, cellar latch, harbor tide*. Fresh dark ink, already dry. Along the left margin of the pad, a curved smear cuts straight through the first letters of every line—the unmistakable drag of a left-handed writer hooking a wrist across wet ink.

I flip the sketchbook corner up one more time to compare. Every ink label on the early botanical plates leans forward at a steady right-hand slant, crisp and unsmudged. Lydia wrote right-handed. Whoever jotted that departure note beside the uncapped Montblanc hooked their left hand across the page. Worth logging, nothing more.

Downstairs, the rhythmic tapping of Graham's keyboard stops.

I slide my phone into my pocket and step back toward the flat-files. Stair treads creak softly from the landing, followed by slow, deliberate weight shifting across the upper runner, stopping directly outside the cracked studio door.

My fingers find the brass house key in my pocket. Outside in the dark hallway, the doorknob turns slowly, metal clicking against the latch plate.

# Chapter 3

The studio door swings inward without a sound, flooding the edge of the floorboards with pale amber light from the hallway sconces.

I'm already standing beside the flat-files with my phone screen dark in my palm, holding an unlit brass desk lamp by its neck like I tripped over the cord trying to turn it on.

Graham stops in the doorway. He's holding an empty ceramic mug by the handle, wearing the same frayed charcoal sweater from downstairs. His eyes jump straight past me to the walnut desk—checking the green blotter, the closed sketchbook, the silver pen—before swinging back to my wool-socked feet.

"I thought I heard a latch," he says, voice quiet enough that it barely carries over the wind pressing the seaward glass. "You told me you weren't starting until morning."

"Couldn't sleep." I set the lamp base back onto the flat-file gently so it doesn't scrape. "And you want a preliminary ledger by Friday. Figured I'd count the flat-file drawers and get a rough box estimate before daylight."

He steps inside anyway, crossing the threshold slowly as if testing the air. Smelling for smoke, maybe, or checking whether I pried a drawer off its runners. He stops beside the desk, glancing down at the uncapped Montblanc and the smudged scratch pad on the blotter. For a tense beat I wait for him to notice the slight shift in the brass paperweight—to accuse me of snooping, invoke the fifty-thousand-dollar NDA, and throw me back across the causeway without a dime of the remaining ten grand.

Instead, his shoulders drop slightly. He doesn't touch the pen or the pad, just lets his gaze linger on the blotter before turning toward the window.

"Lydia hated overhead lights," he says, looking out toward the black cove water through the painted-shut sash. "She worked under that brass lamp until two in the morning when a commission was due. I left the room untouched for the lawyers."

"Even the brushes?" I ask before I can stop myself.

Graham turns his head toward me, gray eyes unreadable in the half-light. "Meaning?"

"Nothing. Just neat for an active studio." I've already pushed too far—the exact compulsive itch that cost me my Harbor Mutual license. I force a tired shrug and gesture toward the hallway. "I'll log the canvases first thing after breakfast. Goodnight, Graham."

He doesn't move out of the doorway until I'm inches away, forcing me to brush past his sleeve to reach the runner. He smells faintly of cedar shavings and cold rain, even though he hasn't stepped outside since I arrived. As I head down the stairs toward my ground-floor guest room, I don't hear him follow—just the soft click of the studio door pulling shut behind him, followed by the metallic snick of a key turning in a lock overhead before I reach the bottom tread.

Back in my room, I wedge the wooden desk chair under the cheap privacy knob before sitting on the edge of the iron bed. Five grand in my duffel won't feel safe until it hits a teller window in Portland, and Friday morning suddenly feels an ocean away.

***

Downstairs at seven-thirty, the house smells like burnt French roast and wet kelp.

Low tide has stripped the cove bare, leaving glistening black ledges and knotted rockweed exposed beneath a bruised pewter sky. Fog hangs thick over the outer breakwater, erasing the horizon until the glass dining wall feels like it's floating inside a cloud.

Graham stands at the limestone island in a clean slate-gray oxford shirt, sleeves rolled above his forearms, pouring coffee from a glass carafe into two mismatched mugs. Dark hollows under his eyes look deeper in daylight than they did under the foyer sconces.

"Black?" he asks, sliding one mug across the stone without looking up.

"Please." I wrap both hands around the warm ceramic and pull out a barstool across the island from him. My boots are laced tight; my phone sits in my jacket pocket with the voice-memo shortcut prepped on the lock screen, though I haven't tapped record. Maine is a one-party consent state, yet getting caught holding a hot mic inside a defense contractor's kitchen is the fastest way to trigger a breach lawsuit.

Graham leans his hip against the counter, staring out over the exposed mudflats while he sips. "I took the liberty of ordering twenty wardrobe cartons and acid-free tissue from a supply house in Rockland. They'll drop them at the gate by noon."

"Good. I'll need access to the study totes too, if I'm logging paperwork."

"After breakfast." He sets his mug down carefully beside a folded yellow legal pad, turning the pad slightly until its spine runs square with the countertop edge. "Tell me how your last employer operated, Tessa. Harbor Mutual. When a claim looked wrong—say, a marine loss in bad weather—what did they actually check?"

The casual drop of my first name catches me off guard. Yesterday it was *Ms. Calder*. Today we're having coffee over marine losses.

I take a slow sip of the bitter coffee to buy myself a beat. "Depends on the policy value. Small skiff losses under fifty grand usually clear on a harbor deputy's report and a Coast Guard incident log. Nobody pays an investigator to dive a rocky cove in November unless the hull washes up with drill holes in it."

"And if there's an estate dispute?"

"Then lawyers subpoena phone pings, toll transponders, and bank pulls," I say mildly, watching his fingers tighten around the ceramic handle. "Why? Is Lydia's family contesting the probate petition on Friday?"

Graham looks down at his coffee. "Lydia's brother lives in Zurich. He hasn't spoken to her since their father died, and he's already pushing the Boston trustees at Bramwell & Shaw to freeze our joint accounts until a formal death certificate clears. Under the Lydia Kiefer Irrevocable Trust, I hold conditional joint signatory authority over five-point-four million in family assets—so long as I'm in good standing and no probate freeze locks the ledger. Meanwhile, Kiefer Soundworks has a patent buyout sitting in escrow with a defense prime out of Arlington."

There it is—the real crack beneath the grief-stricken widower act. I keep my expression neutral, nodding slowly like a sympathetic clerk, watching his hands. "Probate freeze locks up company equity?"

"Locks up everything," he says, voice dropping an octave into something rawer, less rehearsed. "My R&D payroll clears next Wednesday. If the trustees freeze the trust before Friday's preliminary filing, the Arlington buyer walks away from the acquisition, my liquidity evaporates, and creditors call the lab lease by Thanksgiving. If the filing clears on schedule, the buyout closes—and I get the cash to settle every company obligation before an audit ever touches it."

"Which still doesn't explain why you hired me through a blind broker instead of calling an estate firm in Camden," I say, keeping my voice flat. "Local outfits pack faster and don't charge cash premiums."

Graham's eyes lift from his mug. He looks exhausted enough to drop the polite host routine altogether. "Local outfits talk at the general store before five o'clock. Every clerk in this county knows Deputy Halstead, and Halstead's office already has three reporters sniffing around the harbor causeway. I needed someone with zero local ties who understands how to document property thoroughly enough that Boston counsel can't claim I hid marital assets—or missed something inside this house."

I stare at him across the pale counter. For the first time since I drove through the cedar gate, the panic behind his gray eyes looks real. I know eviction panic when I see it. Still, five-point-four million in trust money buys far sharper motive than winter sweaters.

"What happened the night of the storm?" I ask quietly, testing the water while he's still talking. "The broker said October fourteenth. Squall came in fast?"

Graham pulls back behind the polished mask. "She took the fiberglass skiff out around ten-thirty to check the mooring line before the wind shifted. I was in the basement darkroom running an acoustic sweep with noise-canceling headphones on. By the time I came upstairs at midnight, the dock light was out and her rain slicker was gone."

"And the Coast Guard found the boat at dawn?"

"Drifting half a mile past the breakwater, oars still shipped." He picks up his coffee mug and turns toward the sliding glass doors facing the back deck. For a second, the winter light catches the faint tremor in his wrist before he sets the ceramic down. "Deputy Halstead logged it before noon. Now, if you've finished your coffee, I'd like you to start in the sunroom before the supply truck gets here."

***

The back deck wraps the seaward face of the house in weathered cedar planks, slick with overnight fog condensation. Below us, steep granite steps—no handrail, just like Graham warned—zig-zag down the bluff toward the dark timber boathouse squatting over the cove pilings. # Chapter 4

# Chapter 5

My Civic's heater takes half the causeway just to push lukewarm air against the fogged windshield. Behind me, the timber gate grinds shut until spruce trees swallow the glass house, yet my shoulders stay hunched as if Graham's hand is still pressed flat against that second-story pane.

The brass **B-4** key knocks softly against my ribs every time the muffler shield hits a frost heave.

# Chapter 6

Thursday. Twenty-four hours shaved off an already impossible deadline, trapping me inside this glass house through the worst of the November storm while Boston lawyers circle overhead.
