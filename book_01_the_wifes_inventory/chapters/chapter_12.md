# Chapter 12

I lean over the steel worktable, keeping my heels planted flat on the rubber mat so I don't jar the aluminum tripod again.

On the left studio monitor, Julian Mercer stays frozen mid-stride in his hooded rain slicker, both gloved hands clamped around the knotted end of a heavy rolled canvas rug. Behind his heels, the sunroom patio doors stand cracked open to the October fourteenth gale, rain streaking diagonally across the infrared lens. Ahead of him, the boathouse slip hatch yawns black.

White digital numbers burned into the upper corner read 02:14:07 AM.

I reach out with two fingers, careful not to nudge the desk mouse until I've checked the open media timeline beneath the frame. The video track isn't a raw security export pulled off a cloud dashboard. It's a three-minute clip isolated inside an encrypted **ARCHIVE** directory—and right beside it in a second window sits an audio waveform editor loaded with four stacked stereo tracks labeled `HYDROPHONE_COVE`, `SUNROOM_EAST`, `BOATHOUSE_LINT`, and `STAIRWELL_4`.

Cold prickles spread across the back of my neck.

Julian didn't just build noise-canceling transducers down here for an Arlington patent buyer. He wired the entire Mercer Glass House—indoor stairwells, sunroom glass, even the submerged cove pilings—with directional acoustic arrays. Every room upstairs is a live microphone feeding straight into this cellar. Even the words Julian and I traded over soup Tuesday night—and the quiet click of my boots in the mudroom—probably sit archived on a spinning platter somewhere inside this aluminum rack.

Beside the worktable stands a waist-high brushed-aluminum server rack humming steadily behind perforated steel mesh. Warm dry air radiates off the top vents, carrying the faint metallic tang of hot silicon and solder flux. Three identical black chassis units sit stacked inside, each tagged with crisp white Dymo tape: `PRIMARY PARALLEL`, `COVE HYDROPHONE`, and `AMBIENT / PERIMETER`. Tiny amber activity LEDs flutter steadily across the drive bays, writing continuous parity backups even with the rest of the house asleep. Coiled XLR patch cables hang neatly from brass hooks along the side rail, beside an acoustic calibration logbook dated back to last winter.

I glance nervously toward the dark steel fire door tucked into the northwest wall under the foyer stairs. Its heavy pneumatic closer glints dully in the blue monitor wash, sealed tight against the steel frame.

If this workstation is tied into the main Mercer Soundworks hub upstairs, waking these monitors might have already tripped a silent power-draw ping on Julian's phone—or worse, alerted whoever climbed down that pantry dumbwaiter shaft before 2:14 AM. Every second I linger down here, I'm betting my life that the swapped bourbon actually put Julian under until dawn, and that nobody else is standing on the other side of that foyer door listening to the drive bays spin.

I nudge the desk mouse a fraction, just enough to wake the right-hand monitor's directory tree without hitting play on the video.

A list of spectral-analysis logs fills the screen, dated from October sixteenth straight through yesterday afternoon. Someone spent three weeks running wind-noise subtraction filters on a single eight-minute audio window—02:14:00 to 02:22:18—trying to strip forty-knot gale roar off a muffled human argument near the dock. The last batch-render timestamp shows 4:12 PM, right before dusk. At four-twelve yesterday afternoon, Julian's Audi was still ninety miles south on I-95, and I was descending the granite cliff trail toward Maren's oyster shack. Somebody else stood right here in this soundproofed room at dusk, scrubbing audio files while the house sat empty—minutes before I climbed back up in fog and saw a flashlight sweeping inside the locked boathouse.

Not a killer scrubbing evidence. Somebody down here was frantically trying to hear what happened on October fourteenth—or someone was trying to scrub a voice out of the mix before Thursday's courier arrives.

***

I step away from the monitors and move quietly across the dark room, letting the pale blue screen glow guide me toward the far side of the basement. Every step across the interlocking rubber mats absorbs the weight of my heels without a whisper.

The air down here feels thick and deadened, smelling of warm electronics, rubberized matting, and damp poured concrete. Halfway across the floor, I pass through the northeast corner, where floor-to-ceiling wedge baffling angles sharply inward behind a pair of heavy freestanding bass traps wrapped in dark charcoal acoustic cloth.

The moment I step into that corner pocket, the low hum of the three server fans vanishes completely.

Not muffled—*gone*, swallowed whole by the angled foam geometry so cleanly that even the rustle of my flannel sleeve against my ribs drops to nothing. Pressure shifts subtly against my eardrums, like stepping inside an anechoic chamber at a testing lab. Only the faint thud of my own pulse remains inside my skull. I take one step back out toward the center of the room, and the fan hum returns instantly, sharp and mechanical against the concrete. An engineered acoustic dead-zone, built to test directional cancellation in total silence—and a blind spot where a person could stand in the dark without a single breath carrying across the concrete.

I file the corner geometry away without touching the foam and turn to the long steel drafting table pushed against the east masonry wall. A gooseneck task lamp clamped to the corner sits unplugged, its shade dusted with pale drywall grit.

No ropes. No spare license plates. No hidden stash of Evelyn's missing winter boots or sliced sketchbook pages.

Instead, the steel surface is buried under three weeks of frantic paper, stacked and cross-referenced like an insurance fraud auditor's war room—every margin marked with red ink and clipped together by date.

A laminated Maine coastal tide chart lies weighted at the corners with brass acoustic weights, October fourteenth's 2:14 AM high-water mark circled twice in red felt-tip pen. Beside it sits a thick printout of Salt Point Harbor VHF repeater logs, with yellow highlighter dragged across every dead-carrier squelch burst between one-thirty and three in the morning—the exact anomaly Maren Lindqvist warned me Evelyn asked about two days before the storm. Marginalia in Julian's sharp handwriting flags a forty-second gap where the harbor transponder signal dropped cleanly off the coastal tower without a single power-loss error code. Beneath the repeater sheets lies a printed Coast Guard search-and-rescue drift grid, with red arrows sketched by hand showing how a swamped hull would drift northwest toward the outer bell buoy rather than wash back into the cove. A cold ceramic coffee mug sits half-empty near the edge, a dry ring of grounds crusted inside the rim, beside a worn silver mechanical pencil with its eraser chewed flat.

Under the corner of the tide table lies a yellow legal pad covered in Julian's neat, cramped block handwriting—columns of boat-hull draft depths compared against the granite ledge depth at low water, tide-current drift estimates in knots, and fuel-burn tables for a single-outboard fiberglass skiff running without navigation lights. At the bottom of the page sits a single underlined question scrawled hard enough to dent the paper below:

*How did the Mercer skiff clear the outer bar at 2:14 without hitting the east shoal?*

My chest tightens.

If Julian Mercer dragged Evelyn's body out of the sunroom in a rolled canvas rug at 2:14 AM, why is he sitting in a soundproofed cellar three weeks later calculating whether a fiberglass skiff could clear the east shoal at high tide? A killer who dumped his wife off the outer ledge doesn't spend hours poring over Coast Guard drift grids and harbor repeater logs trying to figure out where his own boat went. Unless the man on that infrared video isn't the one who took the boat out—or unless Julian is building a paper defense so thick he can fool a Boston grand jury. Every scrap of paper on this steel table reads less like a killer's trophy stash and more like a cornered man trying to reconstruct how his own wife vanished off his own dock.

And yet the timing still doesn't add up. If Evelyn scanned her passport in New Brunswick Tuesday morning—and if Cole Halstead already holds administrator PINs to the house—then someone else has been using this basement too.

I glance back across the room toward the glowing left-hand monitor.

In the grainy infrared frame, Julian's rain slicker hood is pulled low over his brow. You can't see his eyes, and you can't see his left hand—it's buried deep inside the canvas fold of the rug, while the right sleeve hangs slightly loose at the wrist as if the coat fits a broader shoulder.

Before I can cross back to zoom in on the slicker's cuff, the cold beam of my phone catches a tall gray steel cabinet bolted into the masonry beside the drafting table.

***

Stenciled across the upper door in faded white block paint are four words:

`ARCHIVE — DO NOT ERASE`

A heavy chrome cam lock secures the center handle, its brass keyway scratched from frequent use. Thin pale smears in the gray enamel around the handle show where someone wiped fingerprints off recently—not with household lemon oil, just a quick swipe of a dry sleeve. I test the handle once; the steel door doesn't budge a hair. I reach into my front jeans pocket and pull out the rusted **B-4** key I took from behind the sunroom baseboard, holding its warded brass bit up toward the dim blue spill from the monitors. One glance tells me the old mortise bit won't fit a flat cabinet wafer lock; B-4 belongs to the heavy steel fire door behind me, not this storage locker.

I slip the B-4 key back into my pocket, double-checking that the heavy brass front-door key and my folded tide strip are still tucked safely beside it, and unfold the narrow steel pry blade on my multi-tool.

Time is running out. My soles are damp from the cold concrete edge near the wall, and every instinct warns me I've already stayed down here five minutes too long. Upstairs, the house is silent, yet the swinging hemp rope in the dumbwaiter shaft proved somebody was standing right where I'm standing less than twenty minutes ago. If whoever used that shaft comes back for the drive or the cabinet, there's nowhere to hide down here except that pitch-black northeast acoustic corner.

I slide the flat steel tip into the narrow seam between the cabinet door and its steel frame, just above the chrome cam latch, and brace my left shoulder against the side panel. Cold enamel bites through my flannel sleeve. I throw my weight into the handle, twisting the multi-tool slowly so the blade won't snap against the hardened lock plate. For two endless seconds, the steel resists, my fingers cramping around the folded handle as sweat cools down my spine. Then the metal groans softly—once, twice—before the cheap cam tongue bends inward with a sharp *snap* of yielding steel that sounds deafeningly loud inside the foam-lined room.

I freeze, holding my breath, waiting for footsteps above the joists or a floorboard creak in the foyer. Even the server rack's steady fan hum suddenly feels too quiet, as if the whole house is holding its breath right above my head. Nothing moves.

I pull the gray door open, catching the edge with my fingertips so the bent latch doesn't scrape back against the frame.

Inside, the cabinet holds no spare server drives and no audio reels. The upper and lower shelves sit completely bare, wiped clean enough that not even a curl of solder wire or a loose thumbscrew remains on the gray steel.

On the middle shelf lies a single thick padded manila envelope, sealed tight across the flap with overlapping strips of red tamper-evident tape. Someone scrawled `OCT 14 — MASTER BACKUP / DO NOT OPEN BEFORE PROBATE` across the front in thick black marker—Julian's block script, slightly slanted at the end as if his hand shook when he wrote it. Through the stiff kraft paper, I can feel the sharp rectangular edge of folded legal documents and a smaller, hard plastic lump like an encrypted thumb drive tucked inside the lower corner. Not a speck of dust sits on the envelope's upper edge; whoever owns this locker checked it recently, probably right before the 2:14 AM latch click woke me upstairs.

I reach in, wrap my cold fingers around the padded envelope, and lift it clear of the metal shelf.

Across the room, the heavy steel fire door beneath the foyer stairs cracks open slightly—just enough for a thin wedge of dark foyer shadow to cut across the blue monitor glare—then slams shut into its steel jamb with a deafening pneumatic *thud*.

I spin around, clutching the envelope flat against my chest.

A heavy steel deadbolt slides home into the strike plate from the *outside* with a final, echoing *clack* that vibrates straight through the concrete floor.

On the inner door frame, the tiny pinpoint LED above the mortise lock winks from dark to steady, glaring red.
