# Chapter 12

I lean over the steel worktable and keep my heels planted on the rubber mat so I don't bump the tripod again. A figure that looks like Graham Kiefer is frozen on the left display in a dark rain slicker, hood pulled low, gloved hands clamped around the knotted end of a heavy rolled canvas rug. Behind him, the sunroom patio doors stand cracked open to the storm, rain streaking diagonally across the infrared lens. Ahead of him, the open double doors of the boathouse gape black.

White digital numbers burned into the upper corner read **02:14:07 AM**.

A bare-bones media timeline runs beneath the paused frame: a local three-minute export pulled from an **ARCHIVE** folder, not a cloud security clip. Next to it, an audio editor holds five stacked waveform tracks: `SUNROOM_EAST`, `MIC_04_PANTRY`, `STAIRWELL_4`, `BOATHOUSE_LINT`, and `HYDROPHONE_COVE`.

Graham didn't just build noise-canceling prototypes down here for an Arlington buyer. He wired microphones across the property (sunroom, pantry, stairwell, boathouse, even a submerged hydrophone on the cove pilings) into one local recording array. Half the rooms upstairs feed right down into this cellar. Probably the words we traded over leek soup Tuesday night, too.

A waist-high aluminum server rack hums beside the worktable and vents warm exhaust through perforated mesh. Three black chassis units are stacked inside (`PRIMARY PARALLEL`, `COVE HYDROPHONE`, `AMBIENT / PERIMETER`) over a heavy UPS battery bank bolted along the bottom rail to keep the local drives and displays running if main power drops. A compact cellular bridge rests on top with its status light dead. External alerts won't leave this rack over cellular, though local house sensors are a different story.

I glance back toward the steel fire door under the foyer stairs, sealed shut on its pneumatic closer. If the house system logged motion down here, or tipped off whoever used the dumbwaiter, I'm betting my neck that swapped bourbon kept Graham under until dawn.

I nudge the mouse to wake the right-hand screen, careful not to hit play. A batch-render log fills the display from October twenty-first through yesterday morning. Two weeks of wind-noise filters hammered against one eight-minute stretch (02:09 to 02:17) to pry human voices out from under gale roar. The last completed render finished November 4 at 7:05 AM, ten minutes before Graham left for Portland. A red terminal alert right underneath is stamped **4:45 PM**: three failed passphrase lockouts on the encrypted export queue.

At four-forty-five yesterday, Graham's Audi was ninety miles south on I-95, and I was down at Maren's sorting shed. Somebody stood right at this table trying to break into the export queue while the house sat empty, just minutes before I spotted a flashlight moving behind the boathouse window down on the cove.

I step away from the desk and cross the dark room by the cold blue light from the displays. The interlocking rubber mats soak up my footsteps as I cut through the northeast corner, where floor-to-ceiling charcoal wedge foam angles inward behind two heavy bass traps.

The second I step into that pocket, the room's echo dies. The server fans still hum through open air, yet the foam baffles kill the reflections off the concrete so completely that the sound turns dry, flat, and hard to place. When I shift my shoulders, even the brush of flannel against my ribs disappears into the wedges. Take one step back toward bare concrete and the room bounce snaps right back. An acoustic dead-zone built for directional mic testing.

A long steel drafting table stands against the east masonry wall past the corner, an unplugged gooseneck lamp clamped to one edge with pale drywall grit dusting its shade.

Two weeks of charts lie clipped together across the steel by date. Brass acoustic blocks pin down a laminated Maine tide chart with October fifteenth's 2:14 AM high-water mark circled twice in red felt-tip. Salt Point coastal AIS tracking logs lie beside it, highlighted across a forty-second transponder dropout inside a shore-station blind spot. That matches the exact quirk Lydia asked Maren about. A Coast Guard drift grid overlaps the logs, marked with red arrows curving northwest toward the outer bell buoy. Near the edge, a half-empty ceramic mug with dried grounds crusted inside the rim rests next to a silver mechanical pencil chewed down at the eraser.

A yellow legal pad pokes out from under the corner of the chart, covered in Graham's cramped block script: hull draft depths stacked against granite ledge soundings, tide-current estimates, fuel-burn tables for a fiberglass skiff running dark. At the bottom, one underlined question is pressed so hard the letters dent the sheet underneath:

*How did the Kiefer skiff clear the outer bar at 2:14 without hitting the east shoal?*

I stare at the chewed pencil and the clipped charts. None of this clears Graham. He knows the tides, he controls this acoustic array, and he's been lying to me since Monday night. Still, it knocks a hole through the simple version of events. Whoever filled this table spent two weeks trying to reconstruct how his own boat got past the outer bar—unless the hooded figure on that display isn't the one who took the skiff out, or isn't Graham at all.

I look back across the room at the left-hand display. In the grainy infrared frame, the rain slicker's hood hangs low over the figure's brow and the left hand stays buried in the rolled canvas. Look at the shoulders, though. The waterproof shell strains across a broader chest and thicker neck than Graham's and drags the right sleeve short above a heavy wrist and metal watchband.

Before I can cross back to zoom in on that watch, I spot a tall gray steel cabinet bolted into the masonry right beside the drafting table. Four words are stenciled across the upper door in faded white paint:

`ARCHIVE — DO NOT ERASE`

A chrome cam lock secures the center handle, its keyway scratched from use. Pale smears across the gray enamel around the latch show where someone wiped the metal recently with a dry sleeve, trying the handle and cleaning up when it wouldn't turn. I tug the handle; the steel doesn't give a fraction. I fish the rusted **B-4** key out of my front jeans pocket and hold its warded brass bit up to the screen glow. No chance it fits a wafer lock; B-4 belongs to the heavy steel fire door behind me.

I drop the key back into my pocket and flip out the narrow pry blade on my multi-tool. That swaying hemp rope in the dumbwaiter shaft tells me I don't have long.

I wedge the steel tip into the door seam right above the cam latch, lean my shoulder into the cabinet side, and twist slowly so the blade won't snap. The latch holds stubbornly until my fingers cramp. Then the cam tongue bends inward with a sharp *snap* of steel that feels way too loud in the deadened air.

I freeze and listen for floorboards overhead. Nothing moves. I ease the gray door open with my fingertips so the bent latch doesn't scrape the frame.

The upper and lower shelves are empty, wiped bare. One thick padded manila envelope lies on the middle shelf, its brass clasp pressed down under overlapping strips of red tamper-evident tape. Graham's block script crosses the front in thick black marker and slants slightly at the end like his hand wasn't steady when he wrote it: `OCT 14 — MASTER BACKUP / DO NOT OPEN BEFORE PROBATE`. Through the stiff kraft paper I can feel folded legal sheets and a smaller hard lump tucked in the bottom corner, probably a thumb drive. Not a speck of dust coats the paper; it was put here recently, or checked just as recently.

I reach in and lift the envelope off the shelf.

Across the room, the heavy steel fire door beneath the foyer stairs cracks open an inch—a wedge of dark stairwell cuts the blue glare—then slams shut into its jamb with a pneumatic *thud*.

I spin around, clamping the envelope to my chest.

A heavy steel deadbolt slides home from the *outside* with an echoing *clack* that carries straight through the concrete under my socks.

Above the interior mortise plate, the tiny pinpoint LED winks from dark to steady, glaring red.
