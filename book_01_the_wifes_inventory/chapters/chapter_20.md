# Chapter 20

Graham flinches back against the cast-iron radiator as I drop to my knees beside him on the bleached boards. Cold drafts leak around the sealed slider track and carry the oily bite of fresh linseed on the damp air.

"Tessa," he whispers, his voice barely carrying over the wind outside. A dark purple bruise swells across his left cheekbone where Cole caught him, and a crust of dried blood cakes his upper lip beneath his nose. He takes in my soaked jeans and the raw cut circling my left wrist before stopping on the rusted oyster knife in my hand. "How did you get—"

"Quiet." I set the oyster knife down beside my knee and keep one eye on the kitchen arch while I dig the steel multi-tool out of my stiff, salt-soaked pocket with my right hand. "Cole and Lydia are in the basement. Two minutes, maybe less, before they figure out that crash wasn't me."

I flip out the narrow awl. My right hand shakes from the cold, and my slashed left wrist stings every time I try to brace it. Cole slapped standard county hinged cuffs on him, and in his hurry to get down to the boathouse, he skipped the double-lock bar. That means there is no secondary stop blocking the locking pawl.

"Keep your wrist still," I tell him.

"He smashed my phone on the study floor," Graham says under his breath. He glances toward the hall, shoulders hunched against the radiator ribs. "Hit me when I tried to block the door, then dragged me in here. Said Lydia already had my suicide note half-written."

"Shut up and hold still."

I slide the thin steel tip into the keyway, using it as a makeshift key to hook the spring-loaded locking pawl and lever it back. Graham hisses through his teeth as the steel pinches skin. Salt grit inside my multi-tool makes the awl skitter off the catch, so I brace the handle against the heel of my right palm and turn harder.

*Click.* The ratchet pawl clears, and the cuff jaw pops open.

Graham yanks his arm free and rubs the red groove around his bone as he scrambles awkwardly to his feet on the wool rug. I fold the multi-tool back into my pocket and snatch up the oyster knife. Somewhere beneath the floorboards, Cole's voice booms muffled through concrete. He has found the shattered counterweight beside the ladder, and the fury carries even through masonry.

"The back deck," Graham says. He nods toward the sunroom slider. "Unlock the glass and we can take the cliff path down to Maren's—"

"Maren pulled her truck across the culvert before the surge," I say as I turn toward the kitchen. "I left a note jammed behind her shed latch in case she checks her lines. Either way, Cole parked his cruiser sideways across the causeway and high water's over the road. Nobody's driving off Salt Point tonight. Only play left is keeping both of them off the basement server until the storm breaks."

Graham doesn't follow me toward the front hall. He looks past my shoulder toward the kitchen island, and for a man who just spent an hour cuffed to a pipe, he moves fast. He brushes past me in his torn charcoal sweater and bolts for the counter.

By the time I clear the arch, both of his hands are wrapped around the padded manila envelope, the *OCT 14 — MASTER BACKUP* file holding his forged trustee authorizations and the $2.4 million *Bramwell & Shaw* ledger. He spins toward the stainless gas range along the back wall and tears at the brass clasp with shaking fingers.

"Graham," I warn him.

He ignores me. His left hand grabs the front igniter knob and twists. *Click, click.* A ring of pale blue flame catches under the cast-iron grate.

"Lydia tried to hang a murder on me!" he snaps. He holds the thick sheaf of notarized sheets and the three sliced sketchbook pages an arm's length from the fire. "You heard her on the WAV file! Your Portland cloud only has the civil default letter. These traced schedules and the sketchbook templates are the only proof of criminal forgery! Let them burn, Tessa. We still have the pantry tape. Without these pages, all they've got on me is a civil dispute!"

I step into his lane and bring the rusted oyster knife up until the pitted steel point hovers inches from his chest. He freezes, blue gaslight flickering across his swollen jaw and the sweat beading at his hairline.

"Put them back in the envelope," I say.

"Stop and do the math!" Panic drops out of his voice, replaced by that smooth executive cadence from Monday night, torn collar and all. "Think about tomorrow morning if you hand those sheets to state police. Bramwell and Shaw refers the forgery to the DA, Kiefer Soundworks' accounts freeze, and the Arlington buyout dies in escrow before Monday. I default, you never see your ten-thousand-dollar completion check, Harbor Mutual levies your account, and your landlord locks your Portland place by Thanksgiving. You walk off this rock bleeding, freezing, and broke. A fired adjuster who broke a setup and got nothing to show for it."

He edges closer to the burner. He holds the papers steady and doesn't blink at the knife. "Let them burn, Tessa. Nobody gets hurt. Lydia and Cole still do time on the pantry recording. My Arlington buyout closes Monday. I pay off your entire eighteen-thousand-four-hundred-and-twelve-dollar judgment before noon, restore your adjuster's surety bond, and wire fifty thousand into your checking as a clean consulting fee. You get your life back tonight."

My teeth are still clicking behind my lips, and cold brine drips off my flannel cuffs onto the tile around my heels. I don't yank the envelope away right away. The math is right there in front of me: fifty grand plus a zero balance at the Cumberland County courthouse, no sheriff Monday morning, no four-hundred-dollar checking account. Nobody died over those six wire transfers, and Lydia Kiefer is the one trying to drown us both before high tide. All I have to do is lower my arm and watch a few sheets of paper curl into ash over a blue flame.

Then I picture a thin white binder in a Vermont sheriff's office six years ago, closed before anybody bothered checking dates on my sister Nina, and the Commercial Street fire manifest three years later, where a senior partner offered me a quiet raise to sign dirty paper and look away. Letting Graham burn those pages doesn't give me my life back. It just makes me the exact kind of paid signature I blew up my career refusing to be.

"You forged your wife's initials on six transfers and drained two-point-four million out of her father's estate," I say, my throat raw. "You hired a cornered fraud investigator under a fifty-thousand-dollar gag clause hoping I'd crack Lydia's frame without ever looking too hard at your own books."

"I was trying to save my company!"

"Then tell a judge." Gritting my teeth against the burn in my slashed left wrist, I reach in and rip the papers and envelope out of his grip. Raw gas stings my nose as I jam the sheaf back inside, fold the brass clasp down, and twist the stove knob hard to *OFF*.

The blue flame dies. Graham stares at me, his arms dropping loose at his sides. "You're really turning me in."

"Every page, the pantry WAV file, and the confession Lydia just poured straight into your boathouse mic," I say. I back two steps toward the archway, keeping my eyes on his hands. "You want to see morning, stay ahead of me and keep your mouth shut."

***

I march him past the foyer arch into the start of the north hall and into my guest room with the oyster knife low at my side.

Inside, the radiator ticks low as the boiler cycles down. My navy duffel is right where I left it against the wall. I yank the main zipper open with my right hand, shove the padded manila envelope deep underneath my spare clothes, and reach behind the iron radiator pipes for the crumpled brown store bag. The yellow warning slip Cole wrote left-handed on Tuesday is still tucked inside. I slide it into the duffel's outer side pouch along with the matte-black thumb drive and the knotted glove swab from my soaked jeans pockets. The rusted **B-4** key, the brass front key, and my multi-tool stay in my pockets where I can reach them fast.

I pull a dry dark wool sweater off the top of the bag, drag it over my freezing flannel, and sling the heavy canvas strap across my shoulder so the duffel rides snug against my hip. Sharp mothball cedar clings to the knit from months in storage. The dry wool hangs heavy over my soaked denim and stays cold at the seams, yet it quiets the worst of my shivering so I can think.

"Move," I tell Graham and nudge him back toward the doorway. "Into your study. Let's see if the backup array mirrored the boathouse mic, and whether your UPS battery can push anything out over cellular."

We step out into the mouth of the north hall, right beside the foyer arch. Directly across the foyer, the flush cedar stairwell door rattles hard as Cole's duty boots charge up the concrete steps two at a time.

"She's not in Zone Four—she dropped the counterweight!" Cole shouts up the stairwell. "Check the sunroom!"

Graham flinches beside me, his breath shallow and fast. I grab the back of his torn sweater to drag him deeper down the north hall toward his study before Cole hits the top landing.

Before we clear three paces, a heavy mechanical *CLUNK* carries up through the floorboards from the basement: the unmistakable thud of a main transfer switch throwing off. Every standby LED along the hall dies at once. Down by the pantry arch, the smart-home tablet winks black, and the hallway thermostat goes dark.

Half a heartbeat later, the gravity-drop steel hurricane shutters roar down inside their exterior aluminum tracks and slam home with a deafening metallic *CRASH* across the sunroom glass, the front sidelights, and the north-hall windows. The steel cuts off the last gray twilight and seals every window in total dark right as Cole's boots hit the foyer floor behind us.
