# Chapter 20

Graham flinches back against the cast-iron radiator as I drop to my knees beside him on the bleached boards. Cold air leaks around the slider track, and the wind outside slams salt spray against the glass in heavy, irregular gusts.

"Tessa," he whispers, barely audible over the storm. A dark purple bruise swells across his left cheekbone where Cole caught him, and dried blood crusts his upper lip. He takes in my soaked jeans and the raw cut circling my left wrist before stopping on the oyster knife in my hand. "How did you get—"

"Quiet." I set the knife beside my knee, keep one eye on the kitchen arch, and dig the multi-tool out of my pocket. "Cole and Lydia are in the basement. Two minutes, maybe less, before they figure out that crash wasn't me."

I flip out the narrow awl. My right hand shakes from the cold, and my wrist stings every time I try to brace it. I've never picked a pair of hinged police cuffs in my life, and if Cole locked the safety pin, we're both stuck here.

"Keep your wrist still," I tell him.

"He smashed my phone on the study floor," Graham says under his breath. He glances toward the hall, shoulders hunched against the radiator ribs. "Hit me when I tried to block the door, then dragged me in here. Said Lydia already had my suicide note half-written. She's going to—"

"Shut up and hold still."

I poke blindly into the keyhole, trying to feel the catch. My fingers slip and jam the awl tip straight into my own thumb—hard enough to draw blood. Graham jerks as the metal scrapes bone. I swear under my breath, pin his forearm against the radiator pipe with my knee, and try again. Salt grit inside the hinge makes the tool skitter twice before I brace the handle against the heel of my palm and shove.

*Click.* Cole had been in too much of a hurry to double-lock them. The jaw pops open.

Graham yanks his arm free and rubs the red groove around his wrist as he scrambles to his feet on the wool rug. His knees hit the radiator valve, clanging iron against iron, and both of us freeze for half a second, waiting. I grab the knife. Somewhere beneath the floorboards, Cole's voice booms muffled through concrete. He's found the shattered counterweight beside the ladder, and the fury carries even through masonry.

"The back deck," Graham says. He nods toward the sunroom slider. "Unlock the glass and we can take the cliff path down to Maren's—"

"Maren pulled her truck across the culvert," I say as I turn toward the kitchen. "Cole's cruiser is blocking the causeway, and high water's over the road. Nobody's driving off Salt Point tonight. Only play left is keeping them off the basement server until the storm breaks."

Graham doesn't follow me toward the front hall. He looks past my shoulder toward the kitchen island, and for a man who just spent an hour cuffed to a pipe, he moves fast. He brushes past me in his charcoal sweater and bolts for the counter.

By the time I clear the arch, both of his hands are wrapped around the manila envelope—*OCT 14 — MASTER BACKUP*. He spins toward the gas range along the back wall and tears at the brass clasp with shaking fingers.

"Graham," I warn him.

He ignores me. His left hand grabs the front igniter knob and twists. *Click, click.* A ring of pale blue flame catches under the cast-iron grate.

"Lydia tried to hang a murder on me!" he snaps. He holds the thick sheaf of notarized sheets and the three sliced sketchbook pages an arm's length from the fire. "You heard her on the tape! Your Portland cloud only has the default notice! Without these traced sheets and sketchbook pages, they've got nothing criminal on me—it's just a civil dispute!"

I step into his lane and bring the oyster knife up until the pitted point hovers inches from his chest. The heat from the burner hits the backs of my knuckles, sharp against skin gone numb from the bay. He freezes, blue gaslight flickering across his swollen jaw and the sweat beading at his hairline.

"Put them back in the envelope," I say.

"You're not listening to me." His chin comes up a fraction of an inch, even with the point lined up on his sternum. "If Cole comes up those stairs and finds us fighting over a stack of paper, we're both dead anyway."

He takes half a breath, and panic drops out of his voice, replaced by that smooth executive cadence from Monday night, torn collar and all. "Stop and do the math! Think about tomorrow morning if you hand those sheets to state police. Bramwell and Shaw refers the forgery to the DA, Kiefer Soundworks' accounts freeze, and the Arlington buyout dies in escrow before Monday. I default, you never see your ten-thousand-dollar completion check, Harbor Mutual levies your account, and your landlord locks your Portland place by Thanksgiving. You walk off this rock bleeding, freezing, and broke. A fired adjuster who broke a setup and got nothing to show for it."

He edges closer to the burner. He holds the papers steady and doesn't blink at the knife. "Let them burn, Tessa. Nobody gets hurt. Lydia and Cole still do time on the pantry recording. My Arlington buyout closes Monday. I pay off your entire eighteen-thousand-four-hundred-and-twelve-dollar judgment before noon, restore your adjuster's surety bond, and wire fifty thousand into your checking as a clean consulting fee. You get your life back tonight."

Cold brine drips off my flannel cuffs onto the tile, and my shoulders won't stop shaking. I don't yank the envelope away right away. A cleared docket, a real bank account again, my license back. It's right there, two seconds from ash. Nobody died over six wire transfers, and Lydia is the one trying to drown us both before high tide. All I have to do is lower my arm, let the flame catch the corner of the top page, and step back.

Then I picture a thin white binder in a Vermont sheriff's office six years ago, closed before anybody bothered checking dates on my sister Nina, and the Commercial Street fire manifest three years later, where a senior partner offered me a quiet raise to sign dirty paper and look away. Graham looks at me like he's already calculating which wire to send first, and for a second I hate his neat little executive haircut more than I hate Cole. Letting him burn those pages doesn't give me my life back. It just makes me the exact kind of paid signature I blew up my career refusing to be.

"You forged your wife's initials on six transfers and drained her father's estate," I say, my throat raw. "You hired me under a gag clause hoping I'd clear your name without ever looking too hard at your own books."

"I was trying to save my company!"

"Then tell a judge." Ignoring the burn in my slashed left wrist, I reach in and rip the papers and envelope out of his grip. He lunges half a step to grab them back, then stops short when the knife comes up toward his ribs. Raw gas stings my nose as I jam the sheaf back inside, fold the brass clasp down, and twist the stove knob hard to *OFF*.

The blue flame dies. Graham stares at me, his arms dropping loose at his sides, chest heaving in the dark kitchen. "You're really turning me in."

"Every page, the pantry WAV file, and the confession Lydia just poured straight into your boathouse mic," I say. I back two steps toward the archway, keeping my eyes on his hands. "You want to see morning, stay ahead of me and keep your mouth shut."

***

I march him past the foyer arch into the north hall and my guest room, the knife low at my side. Every step through the dark house feels too loud, my wet sneakers squeaking against the pine floorboards, Graham's breathing echoing off the plaster. He tries to slow down at the threshold, glancing back toward the kitchen, so I catch his elbow and shove him inside ahead of me.

Inside, the radiator ticks as the boiler cycles down. My duffel is against the wall. I yank it open, shove the envelope underneath my spare clothes, and reach behind the pipes for the store bag with Cole's left-handed yellow warning slip tucked inside. Everything goes into the bag, zipped tight.

I pull a dry wool sweater over my freezing flannel and sling the duffel over my shoulder. The wool hangs heavy and itchy against my neck, and my jaw aches from clenching my teeth. Still, the dry knit quiets the worst of my shivering so I can think.

"Move," I tell Graham and nudge him back toward the doorway. "Into your study. Let's see if the backup array mirrored the boathouse mic, and whether your backup battery can push anything out over cellular."

We step out into the north hall beside the foyer arch. Directly across the foyer, the stairwell door rattles hard as Cole's duty boots charge up the concrete steps two at a time, the heavy tread shaking the floor joists under my feet.

"She's not in Zone Four—she dropped the counterweight!" Cole shouts up the stairwell. "Check the sunroom!"

Graham freezes. His breath comes shallow and fast against my shoulder. I grab the back of his sweater to drag him deeper down the hall toward his study before Cole hits the top landing.

Before we clear three paces, a heavy mechanical *CLUNK* carries up through the floorboards from the basement: the unmistakable thud of a main transfer switch throwing off. Every standby LED dies. Down by the pantry arch, the smart-home tablet winks black, and the hallway thermostat goes dark.

Half a heartbeat later, the gravity-drop steel hurricane shutters roar down inside their exterior tracks and slam home with a deafening metallic *CRASH* across the sunroom glass, the front sidelights, and the north-hall windows. The steel cuts off the last gray twilight and seals every window in total dark right as Cole's boots hit the foyer floor behind us.
