# Chapter 8

I stop dead two steps above the bottom landing of the back service stairs with no shoes on and nowhere to go except back up ten creaking pine treads. My phone is still in my hand, the live camera tile spinning into a dead gray circle.

Around the corner of the pantry corridor, the breaker box door swings wide with a low groan. Below me, the narrow hall runs behind the kitchen between the walk-in pantry and the deck door, and Halstead is two steps from the corner.

A toggle snaps inside the metal panel, followed by a soft relay click.

I step off the bottom tread, slip into the pantry, and ease the louvered door shut just shy of the catch. Olive oil tins and stoneware crocks crowd me in a space barely wider than my jacket. The edge of the middle shelf digs into my ribs when I turn sideways to fit.

Upstairs, my clipboard and two open wardrobe boxes are sitting in plain sight outside the master closet. Every sheet I've logged since eight is up there.

Through the angled slats, I have a slanted view across the corridor to the breaker box and the wall tablet beside it, and diagonally past the kitchen island toward the mudroom arch and the main stairs.

Halstead steps into sight in his navy patrol windbreaker, rain beading dark across his jacket and dripping off his cap brim onto the floor.

He pulls a short black jumper wire from his pocket and reaches into the open panel. A faded contractor sticker clings beside the circuit map inside the door: *Halstead Marine & Low-Voltage, Rockland, ME.* Before he ever pinned on a badge, he or his family wired the alarms out on this point.

He leaves the main house circuits alone; the recessed kitchen lights stay steady and the fridge compressor keeps humming behind me. He doesn't even glance at the diagram. He isolates the lower loop for the exterior deck logs, clips the wire across two copper terminals, and checks his watch. The forty-minute service mode suppresses remote push alerts and holds camera events locally. Nothing pings Graham's phone while those terminals stay bridged.

Halstead eases the panel shut, steps to the end of the corridor, and looks up the main stairs.

"Ms. Calder?" he calls out. His voice is low, more of a test than a real summons.

I hold still behind the slats. I could step out and play surprised; he'd turn right back into a helpful county deputy checking a storm latch and walk out without showing me a thing. A cop who bridges a private security bus isn't here on routine patrol. I keep my mouth shut and watch.

Only rain ticks against the ocean glass. Halstead listens for a few seconds, his gaze lingering on the upper landing, then turns back to the wall tablet.

I press my back harder against the bottom shelf to stay clear of the slats. A tin of smoked paprika scrapes my shoulder blades. Directly behind my spine, the paneling under the shelves gives a hair under my weight, hollow, like there's open air behind the boards—maybe dead framing beside the chimney chase, or a void that shouldn't be there at all.

At the tablet, Halstead taps six digits onto the dark glass with his left hand and unlocks the installer menu on the first try.

Nobody guesses a six-digit installer code on a defense contractor's security hub.

He scrolls quickly through the morning event log, his crooked front tooth catching the blue screen glow. He stops on the master-suite motion pings—my own tracks from half an hour ago.

His head lifts toward the main staircase. Now he knows I didn't drive back into town.

He turns back to the screen and taps twice to wipe his keypad entry off the local log. At Harbor Mutual, I watched a warehouse manager in Lewiston scrub a single loading-dock zone the exact same way two nights before an electrical fire took out half a block. They always worked through the screen with that same calm, practiced precision, like they'd rehearsed the taps in their sleep.

He crosses the kitchen light-footed, glances up the stairs, and heads toward the mudroom arch.

I shift half a step to keep him in the slats. A jar clinks against a bottle by my elbow; I pin it to the shelf with my forearm.

Halstead stops mid-step in the archway, head tilted back toward the kitchen. I stay still behind the slats. Then he checks the empty upper rail once more and steps into the mudroom.

He drops to one knee beside the boot tray, his wet trouser leg darkening the stone. He lifts the factory-stiff life vest off peg two, turns the collar to check that the inside tag hasn't been stripped, and hangs it back square against the wall paneling.

Next he scans the coat hooks, his gaze sliding past Graham's wool coat to my jacket beside it. B-4 is tucked inside that breast pocket, wrapped in tissue. One casual pat and his fingers hit metal through the lining. My chest locks up. His hand hovers near the hem.

He skips the coats. Instead, he sweeps two fingers along the floorboard seam under the boot tray lip, checks the deadbolt throw on the mudroom side door, and stares down at the empty right half of the tray where Lydia's duck boots belong. For a second he just stays crouched there, listening to the rain on the roof, before he stands and heads back into the kitchen.

My right calf locks into a slow cramp from holding my weight off the creaky floorboard. I don't dare shift. Even if I bolted past him for the front hall, my boots are sitting in the mudroom and the Civic is trapped behind a slow hydraulic gate. I press my palms flat against the cold plaster behind the shelves and keep my breathing shallow.

The kitchen tap runs, then shuts off. Halstead comes back with a wet paper towel, kneels to wipe a muddy heel mark off the stone where he stepped in from the cove trail, and tosses the towel toward the sink. A second later his boots turn toward the back service stairs.

He stops at the foot of the narrow stairwell and looks up into the dark, his left hand resting on his belt.

"Ms. Calder," he says. No question in it this time.

He waits and listens. Nothing answers him. He turns back into the pantry corridor, scans the narrow space, and stops on the louvered door a few feet away. The shadow of his boots cuts across the thin strip of light under the threshold. He takes a slow half-step closer, his wet sleeve rustling as his right hand lifts toward the doorknob.

***

A muffled buzz vibrates against his chest.

Halstead stops short of the door, pulls a prepaid flip-phone from his windbreaker, and snaps it open with his left thumb. A pale nick crosses his knuckle where he caught a latch or a boat cleat. He steps sideways to stay off the front windows. His wet sleeve almost brushes the slats, and a sharp hit of wintergreen dip cuts through the gap.

"Yeah," he mutters under the hum of the refrigerator and glances back toward the stairs. "I'm inside. Deck bus is bridged."

He listens for a few seconds and shifts his weight from heel to toe. Through the gap between two slats I can see the gray stubble along his jaw under the dim sconce, rainwater dripping from his cuff onto the baseboard.

"Gate log's clear," he says. "Still down south—won't be back before two."

Another pause, then an impatient exhale. "Portland cleaner's dragging her feet in the master closet," he mutters. "Move it up to Thursday."

*Move what up?* Graham already pushed the probate filing to Thursday night. Whoever is on the other end of that burner isn't Graham Kiefer. They're moving their own clock to beat the Boston courier.

Outside, a gust rattles the deck door in its weatherstripping and pushes a strip of cold air under the sill across my ankles.

"Don't give a damn what the Boston courier thinks," Halstead says, dropping his voice so low I have to strain over the wind. "Guy thinks a Cambridge address and a fat check let him treat this whole point like his private bank. Keep yourself tucked out of sight down the cove until dusk. I'll pull the rest before any suit from Boston sets foot on the causeway."

He snaps the phone shut and checks his watch. The bypass window is burning down, and Graham is due back on a shorter clock now. Halstead stands motionless outside the slats, his jaw set, staring straight at the pantry door as if he's weighing the risk of one quick pull on the knob. Whatever is behind this closet is what he came inside to reach, yet with me still in the house he doesn't push it. Whatever they just moved up to Thursday matters more than blowing his cover before dusk.

He turns back to the breaker box, pops the latch, and pulls the jumper wire off the terminals. Beside the box, the wall tablet chimes as the exterior deck camera resumes live streaming.

Halstead closes the panel, scans the floor once more for stray tracks, and walks straight to the deck door. He steps out into the rain, pulls the door shut, and taps the lock key on the outside pad. The electronic deadbolt slides home, and the LED blinks back to steady amber.

I count to thirty, then sixty, waiting for him to double back and test the handle. Only the rain answers. I ease the louvered door open, glance at the stove clock—Graham's due back from Portland before long—and decide whatever's behind these shelves can wait until after I talk to Maren Lindqvist.
