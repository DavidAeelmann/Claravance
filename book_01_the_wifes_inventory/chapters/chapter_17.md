# Chapter 17

You don't argue with a Glock pointed at your chest.

I lower my phone, crouch by the workbench, and lay it face-down on the wet planks. My scraped palm stings where I caught myself on the cliff stairs.

"Back off it," Lydia says, the barrel leveled at my ribs. "Hands up. Over by the slipway piling."

I take two steps back with my palms open at shoulder height. Lydia moves in and kicks my phone across the boards until it skids out of sight under a stack of lobster traps. There goes the screen protector, probably, not that it matters right now. She grabs a thick black marine cable tie out of a plastic bin on the bench.

"On your knees. Back to the teak piling, hands behind it."

There's no cover on the open decking. If I lunge, she puts a bullet in me—and the only reason she hasn't pulled the trigger already is that a nine-millimeter slug ruins a staged drowning. I drop onto the wet boards beside the piling bolted to the corner of the bait table. Brine soaks straight through the knees of my jeans. Cold, immediate, miserable.

Lydia steps behind me and presses the muzzle against the base of my skull while she drags my wrists together behind the timber. She threads the cable tie around both wrists, catches the ratchet, and pulls until the plastic bites into bone. I push against the loop with my thumbs. Zero give. The edge cuts so deep my fingers will go numb in minutes, and the damp wood smells faintly of old tar.

"Cole will be right down once he's done with your little stall at the front door," she says. She circles back where she can watch both me and the double doors, sets the gun on the workbench within six inches of her right hand, and picks up an iPad Mini.

Lydia's iPad Mini wakes under her thumb. She opens a blank note and starts tapping with steady, unhurried thumbs. She doesn't even look winded. Most people at gunpoint at least breathe hard, or sweat, or let their voice shake, and Lydia looks like she's answering an email about catering.

"What are you writing?" I ask.

"Graham's apology," she says, her eyes fixed on the screen. "He's devastated about the two-point-four million. Even sorrier that the woman he hired to clean out the house panicked when a sheriff's deputy knocked on the door."

My wrists ache under the nylon, my fingers already going dull as my sneakers slip on wet moss. I shift my hips to take some of the weight off my shoulders, and my right knuckles graze the underside of the bait-table rail bolted flush behind the piling. Something sharp pricks the heel of my hand.

I keep my eyes on Lydia so my shoulders don't give anything away. Just above my bound wrists, an oyster knife is wedged blade-out into a split seam along the underside of the rail, its swollen oak handle stuck fast in dried salt crust. Somebody probably jammed it there years ago to shuck bait and forgot it.

I leave it alone for now and drop my gaze to the lower cross-brace of the bait table beside my left sneaker. A *Kiefer Soundworks* wired mic is bolted deep in the shadow under the timber, running into the waterproof conduit that climbs the cliff to Graham's basement server. From floor level, I can see a pinpoint blue LED glowing steadily under its rim.

Live. Hardwired back to the Zone Four array. From where she stands by the workbench, Lydia can't see it tucked under the overhang. She thinks she owns Graham's network just because her tablet still reaches the house Wi-Fi from down here.

None of it helps me if Cole walks through those doors first. I need her talking into that mic, and I need my hands free before the padlock turns.

"We already decrypted the pantry mic, Lydia," I say. "Heard you laughing with Cole at two in the morning while wiping down an oar, and telling him to leave the Montblanc next to that smudged checklist. You got sloppy in St. Andrews when you scanned your real passport like an amateur. And now you're letting a county deputy run the whole frame while Boston ties up your Cayman money in probate for five years."

Her thumbs stop on the glass. For a second the only sound in the boathouse is the harbor water slapping the underside of the planks.

"Cole couldn't stage a parking ticket without me," she snaps. "My father's Cayman trustee wouldn't transfer control without an in-person passport check outside the U.S. I gambled a quiet New Brunswick marina wouldn't trip a marine-insurance port watch before Graham was dead."

"And you think Boston's going to hand over three million without a fight?"

"There won't be a fight," she shoots back, sharp and impatient. "The second Graham's dead with a signed confession next to him, the trust cuts his estate off cold. Not one dime goes to his company's creditors—it all rolls straight to me."

"Right. That's why Cole had to hide your weekender behind Graham's coats."

"That bag was supposed to make it look like Graham intercepted me walking out on him October fourteenth and hid my luggage, except Cole forgot to strip the Yarmouth ferry tag Monday morning and shoved it behind the overcoats with October seventeenth still hanging off the handle," she says. "Same way I had to babysit him Sunday when Graham drove south and we finally had an empty house to bleach the sunroom floor without fumes pouring down the cellar shaft. The idiot dropped his pilfered B-4 key behind the baseboard and hammered the trim right back over it. Everything in that house ran on my clock, Tessa. Including your five o'clock Boston courier. I canceled him an hour ago."

"The Coast Guard—"

"Won't put a boat in the water until morning." She takes two steps closer and leans one hip against the bait table, right above the shadowed blue LED. "High water tops the causeway at five-fifteen. Nobody in Augusta chases ferry stubs or Canadian marina logs for an embezzler and a washed-up adjuster who walked into Penobscot Bay together."

Behind my left shoulder, harbor water sucks hard against the slipway planks. My toes are freezing inside my sneakers, and my shoulders ache from the zip tie.

"You didn't even get the life vest wet with salt water," I push. Behind the piling, I angle my bound wrists upward until the taut nylon ridge presses against the blade of the oyster knife. "I shot the clean lot stamp on that vest, and I bagged a swab of the paste you flushed down the master bath P-trap before dusting Graham's rocks glass. Wait till a state lab runs a tox screen on that."

"Cole signed the report." She glances down at the screen, completely unbothered. "Nobody's ordering a second medical examiner when there won't be a body left once the crabs are done. By five-fifteen high tide, there's nobody left on Salt Point to argue trust law with a Boston judge."

***

I press down and drag the nylon across the edge of the blade. The plastic is tough and slick with sweat, and every downward stroke sends a dull vibration through the piling into my spine. I force my jaw loose and keep my breathing steady so my shoulders don't telegraph the motion. Keep her talking. Keep her leaning over that blue light.

"Why pull me into it?" I ask as her nails tap the screen. "You already had Graham framed."

"Because Graham was arrogant enough to hire a fired fraud investigator carrying eighteen grand in court debt," she says. She glances down at my damp flannel like I tracked mud onto her hall rug. "Anybody desperate for five grand cash under a fifty-thousand-dollar gag clause makes herself the ideal accomplice the second she crosses the porch."

A strand of nylon parts behind my wrists. The loop gives a fraction and eases off my left thumb, though salt grit burns in the raw skin along my forearm. Cold spray mists up through the slipway seams around my sneakers as the rising tide surges against the pilings below. The angle is awkward—if I twist my elbows too far out to get leverage, Lydia will spot the shift in my shoulders. I have to work purely from the wrists, short half-inch strokes, blind and slow while my fingers go numb.

Lydia looks down at the tablet and reads aloud in a dry, mocking tone: *"I couldn't bear what I did to Lydia, and when Miss Calder threatened to go to the state police unless I tripled her fee, I panicked—"*

Graham never called me Miss Calder in his life. He barely even called me Tessa when he was irritated, just launched straight into whatever he wanted done. Leave it to Lydia to write a suicide note that sounds like a Victorian butler, though I doubt Cole's sheriff's department is going to critique the prose.

She breaks off as a gust slams the cedar-shingled roof overhead and sends a rattle of pine needles down the eaves. While she glances up at the rafters, I saw harder against the steel. Two more strands give way. Blood slicks my left wrist where the jagged nylon and pitted blade tear the skin above my palm, and the slack lets me twist my fingers toward the oak handle wedged in the seam.

My thumb brushes the rough wood. Almost. Just another quarter inch of give.

"You're really going to let Cole walk away with half your father's trust?" I ask to buy ten more seconds while I work the last frayed core against the edge. "A county deputy with a crooked tooth, a badge on the line, and a grudge. Once Graham's dead, Cole owns you."

Lydia sets the iPad down on the workbench, scoops up the Glock, and steps in close, the barrel aimed straight between my eyes. "Cole knows what happens to people who—"

Outside the north wall, boots crunch across crushed oyster shells. Unhurried strides come off the cliff stairs, followed by the jingle of a sheriff's key ring and brass sliding into the padlock.

Lydia turns her head and right shoulder toward the double doors as the iron latch rattles outside. Her weight shifts for half a beat.

I rip my wrists apart. The last frayed threads of the zip tie snap, my right hand locks around the handle of the oyster knife, and I wrench it free from the wood.
