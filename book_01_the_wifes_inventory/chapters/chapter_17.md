# Chapter 17

You don't argue with a Glock pointed at your chest.

I lower my phone slowly, crouch by the scarred workbench, and lay it face-down on the wet planks. My scraped palm stings where I caught myself on the cliff stairs.

"Back off it," Lydia says, the barrel leveled at my ribs. "Hands up. Over by the slipway piling."

I take two steps back with my palms open at shoulder height. Lydia moves in, kicks my phone hard across the boards until it skids out of sight under a stack of lobster traps, and grabs a thick black marine cable tie out of a plastic bin on the bench. The encrypted thumb drive is still tucked in my watch pocket. My phone is out of reach under those traps, along with the life-vest shots, the Bramwell & Shaw photos, and the bundled upload packet.

"On your knees. Back to the teak piling, hands behind it."

There's no cover on the open decking. One lunge and she puts a bullet in me. The only reason she hasn't pulled the trigger already is that a nine-millimeter slug ruins a staged drowning. I drop onto the wet boards beside the thick teak piling bolted to the corner of the bait table, and brine soaks through the knees of my jeans.

Lydia steps behind me and presses the cold muzzle against the base of my skull while her left hand drags my wrists together behind the timber. She threads the black nylon loop around both wrists, catches the ratchet, and pulls hard until the plastic bites into bone. The wet piling reeks of creosote and old kelp. I push against the tie with my thumbs. Zero give. The nylon cuts so deep my fingers will go numb in minutes.

"Cole will be right down once he's done with your little stall at the front door," she says. She circles back where she can watch both me and the double doors, sets the pistol on the workbench within six inches of her right hand, and picks up a sleek iPad Mini.

`L_KIEFER_IPAD_MINI_02.` The same tablet that just dumped my fake confession into Knox County's queue. She wakes the screen, opens a blank note, and starts tapping with steady, unhurried thumbs.

"What are you writing?" I ask.

"Graham's apology," she says, her eyes fixed on the screen. "He's devastated about the two-point-four million. Even sorrier that the woman he hired to clean out the house panicked when a sheriff's deputy knocked on the door."

My wrists ache under the nylon, my fingers already going dull as my sneakers slip on wet moss. When I shift my hips to ease the pull on my arms, my right knuckles graze the rough wooden underside of the bait-table rail bolted flush behind the piling, and something cold and jagged pricks the heel of my hand. I keep my gaze on Lydia so she doesn't see my shoulders tense. An old rusted oyster knife is wedged blade-out into a split seam along the underside of the rail, inches above my bound wrists, its swollen oak handle stuck fast in years of dried salt crust.

I leave it untouched for now and glance down at the lower cross-brace of the bait table beside my left sneaker. A small matte-black *Kiefer Soundworks* PoE microphone puck is bolted deep in the shadow under the timber, wired into the waterproof `BOATHOUSE_LINT` conduit that runs up the cliff to Graham's basement server. From floor level, I can see a pinpoint blue LED glowing steadily under its rim.

Live, hardwired back to the Zone Four array. It's recording everything in this room. From where she stands by the workbench, Lydia can't see it tucked under the overhang. She assumes she owns Graham's network just because her iPad still reaches the house mesh from down here.

None of it matters if Cole walks in with another handful of zip ties. I need her talking into that mic, and I need my hands free before that padlock turns.

"We already decrypted the pantry mic, Lydia," I say. "Heard you laughing with Cole at two in the morning while wiping down an oar, and telling him to leave the Montblanc next to that smudged checklist. You got sloppy in St. Andrews when you scanned your real passport like an amateur. And now you're letting a county deputy run the whole frame while Boston ties up your Cayman money in probate for five years."

Her thumbs stop on the glass.

"Cole couldn't stage a parking ticket without me," she snaps. "My father's Cayman trustee wouldn't transfer control without an in-person passport verification outside the U.S. I gambled a quiet New Brunswick marina wouldn't trip a marine-insurance port watch before Graham was dead."

"And you think Boston's going to hand over three million without a fight?"

"There won't be a probate fight. Under the trust's governance rider, a signed confession paired with Graham's death triggers immediate forfeiture. That blocks his estate and Kiefer Soundworks' creditors from touching a dime of the remaining corpus."

"Right. That's why Cole had to hide your weekender behind Graham's coats."

"That bag was supposed to make it look like Graham intercepted me walking out on him October fourteenth and hid my luggage, except Cole forgot to strip the Yarmouth ferry tag Monday morning and shoved it behind the overcoats with October seventeenth still hanging off the handle," she shoots back. "Same way I had to babysit him Sunday when Graham drove south and we finally had an empty house to bleach the sunroom floor without fumes pouring down the cellar shaft. The idiot dropped his pilfered B-4 key behind the baseboard and hammered the trim right back over it. Everything in that house ran on my clock, Tessa. Including your five o'clock Boston courier. I canceled him an hour ago."

"The Coast Guard—"

"Won't put a boat in the water until morning." She takes two steps closer and leans one hip against the bait table, right above the shadowed blue LED. "High water tops the causeway at five-fifteen. Nobody in Augusta chases ferry stubs or Canadian marina logs for an embezzler and a washed-up adjuster who walked into Penobscot Bay together."

Behind my left shoulder, black harbor water sucks hard against the slipway planks.

"You didn't even get the life vest wet with salt water," I push. Behind the piling, I angle my bound wrists upward until the taut nylon ridge presses against the rusted edge of the oyster knife. "I shot the clean lot stamp on that vest, and I bagged a swab of the paste you flushed down the master bath P-trap before dusting Graham's rocks glass. Wait till a state lab runs mass spec on that."

Her mouth curves in a thin smile. "Cole signed the report. Nobody's ordering a second medical examiner when there won't be a body left once the crabs are done. By five-fifteen high tide, there's nobody left on Salt Point to argue trust law with a Boston judge."

***

I press down and drag the thick black nylon across the rusted steel. The marine plastic is tough and slick with sweat and salt mist, and every downward stroke sends a dull vibration through the piling into my spine. I force my jaw loose and keep my breathing steady so my shoulders don't telegraph the motion. Keep her talking. Keep her leaning over that blue light.

"Why pull me into it?" I ask as her nails tap the screen. "You already had Graham framed."

"Because Graham was arrogant enough to hire a fired fraud investigator carrying eighteen grand in court debt," she says. She glances down at my damp flannel like I tracked mud onto her hall rug. "Anybody desperate for five grand cash under a fifty-thousand-dollar gag clause makes herself the ideal accomplice the second she crosses the porch."

A tough strand of nylon parts behind my wrists. The loop gives a fraction and eases off my left thumb, though creosote and salt grit burn in the raw skin along my forearm. Cold spray mists up through the slipway seams around my sneakers as the rising tide surges against the pilings below.

Lydia looks down at the iPad and reads aloud in a dry, mocking tone: *"I couldn't bear what I did to Lydia, and when Miss Calder threatened to go to the state police unless I tripled her fee, I panicked—"*

She breaks off as a gust slams the cedar-shingled roof overhead and sends a dry rattle of pine needles down the eaves. While she glances up at the rafters, I saw harder against the rusted edge. Two more strands give way. Blood slicks my left wrist where the jagged nylon and pitted steel tear the skin above my palm, and the slack lets me twist my fingers toward the swollen oak handle wedged in the seam.

"You're really going to let Cole walk away with half your father's trust?" I ask to buy ten more seconds while I work the last frayed core against the blade. "A county deputy with a crooked tooth, a badge on the line, and a grudge. Once Graham's dead, Cole owns you."

Lydia sets the iPad down on the workbench, scoops up the Glock, and steps in close, the barrel aimed straight between my eyes. "Cole knows what happens to people who—"

Outside the north wall, heavy boots crunch across crushed oyster shells. Unhurried strides come off the cliff stairs, followed by the jingle of a sheriff's key ring and brass sliding into the padlock.

Lydia turns her head and right shoulder toward the double doors as the heavy iron latch rattles outside. Her weight shifts for half a beat.

I rip my wrists apart. The last frayed threads of the zip tie snap, my right hand locks around the swollen oak handle of the oyster knife, and I wrench it free from the wood.
