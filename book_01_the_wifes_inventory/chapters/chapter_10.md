# Chapter 10

"You look chilled to the bone," Graham says, nodding toward the sofa on his way to the credenza. "The radiator in the guest wing never keeps up in a southeast wind. Have a drink before you turn in."

He's traded his suit for a dark olive sweater, though he still looks wrung out from the drive. A birch fire snaps behind the screen, pushing dry heat against the cold off the seaward glass. Outside, Salt Point Cove is solid black, not a single dock light burning along the point. Twenty minutes ago I barely beat his garage bay through the mudroom side door, palming the B-4 key inside my jacket pocket so it wouldn't clink against the hook, and sprinted upstairs to kneel outside the master closet with a strip of packing tape across my knuckles before he crossed the hall.

"Water's fine, Graham."

"Nonsense. You've been hauling boxes alone all day." He pulls the stopper from a half-full bourbon decanter and pours two fingers into a pair of rocks glasses on the tray. He reaches automatically for the heavier one at the front left, his thumb finding the grip by muscle memory before polite habit catches him. He sets it on the coaster by my elbow instead, takes the second glass for himself, and drops into the armchair across from me. "Besides, we need to pin down tomorrow's schedule before the Boston courier drives up."

I look down at the amber pour catching the firelight. Neat bourbon. Graham's glass gleams clear as honey. Down in the facets at the bottom curve of mine, the alcohol is loosening a dried glaze into a pale, hazy swirl. It looks just like the tablet residue caked inside the master-bath P-trap upstairs.

"Tomorrow's courier," I say. I clasp my hands around my knees and leave the coaster untouched. "Still five o'clock?"

"Five sharp." He leans back and gives his glass a slow turn. Firelight sharpens the hollows under his cheekbones. "The Arlington buyers want the preliminary probate schedule uploaded by five-thirty so their counsel can clear the lien release Friday morning. That means every high-value item in the studio and master suite needs a signed tag and a timestamped photo by four."

"That's tight," I say, and watch him over the rim of his glass. "Especially without the boathouse logged. You're asking me to sign off on an incomplete estate schedule."

His fingers go still on the glass. Behind the firescreen, a birch log pops and throws a hiss of sparks against the wire.

"Your Harbor Mutual license isn't active, Tessa," he says, the polite edge still in his voice. "You're a private contractor working under an NDA. Finish the house interior by four tomorrow, hand me a clean ledger, and the remaining ten thousand wires to your account before the courier hits the causeway. If you walk away short of that, we both know what the breach clause costs."

He doesn't have to raise his voice to remind me who holds the checkbook, or what's waiting on my Civic's passenger seat. For a second I just want to throw the glass at the hearth and walk out, breach clause or not. Funny how my career arc has gone from testifying in federal subrogation hearings to getting lectured about contract law by a man in an expensive sweater. Give me another six months of smart choices like this and I'll be appraising haunted storage lockers in Bangor for gas money. Still, I keep my hands folded around my knees and let him finish.

"Leave the boathouse off the preliminary schedule," he adds. "I told you Monday night. The county seal stays until the Coast Guard signs the final closure memo. Stick to what's inside the house."

*County seal.* Monday night he hadn't said a word about a county seal, only that the Coast Guard cleared it and locked it against salt damp. Now he's repeating Halstead's line word for word.

I open my mouth to ask about the boathouse, but his phone buzzes twice on the side table. He checks the screen and his mouth goes thin.

"Hold that thought," he mutters, and sets his untouched glass on the tray by his chair. "Patent counsel in Boston. Let me grab ice for yours, too. The decanter's been left too close to the radiator."

He heads toward the kitchen with the phone already against his ear. I'm alone with the fire and two glasses. I don't hesitate. The second his footsteps clear the archway, I lean across the low table, lift my heavier glass by the base, and set it square on the tray beside his chair. Up close, the etched rim is worn smooth on one side where a thumb always rests. Definitely his regular glass. I pick up his untouched pour and set it on my coaster, matching the angle. If he spots the chalky swirl at the bottom of his own tumbler when he sits back down, we're both done pretending.

If there's crushed zolpidem in that bourbon, he can drink his own pour.

Ice clatters into a metal bucket in the kitchen, followed by Graham's voice dropping sharp and low near the pantry corridor. He's pacing as he argues with Boston about a bridge extension and an escrow holdback—two minutes, maybe three, before he hangs up. I slip off the sofa and cross silently to the door of his north-hall study.

Cold rises off the hearthstone. Only a pale wedge of hall light falls across the wide desk blotter. Yesterday morning the desk looked bare as a showroom. Tonight, a leather portfolio rests crooked beside the inkwell, and a shredder tucked under the return hums with a red **JAM** light, strips of cream bond paper wedged in its teeth. A half-torn wire-transfer receipt pokes out of the wastebasket underneath, smudged with toner.

I verify my phone's flash and shutter sound are off. Then I flip the portfolio open.

A cream envelope slit along the top flap lies inside, stamped **CERTIFIED MAIL — RETURN RECEIPT REQUESTED**. Two pages stick halfway out under the letterhead of *Bramwell & Shaw Fiduciary LLP, Boston*, clipped to a highlighted trust summary. A yellow sticky note clings to the margin in tight legal script: *Final cure window. No further extensions.*

I angle the first page into the hall light and scan fast.

*Notice of Default & Acceleration: Kiefer Soundworks LLC.*

Under *The Lydia Kiefer Irrevocable Trust*, Graham holds shared signing rights over a $5,400,000 trust account. On October fifteenth, Bramwell & Shaw issued a formal default demand over $2,400,000 in unauthorized draws pulled against trust-backed collateral over fourteen months, which leaves $3,000,000 in the trust. The letter gives him a thirty-day window to pay it back, expiring November fourteenth (ten days left), before the trustees pursue recovery and refer the accounting to the Suffolk County DA.

A special forfeiture rule is highlighted in yellow near the bottom: if Graham is formally charged with murder or a major felony, his signing rights strip immediately and the grace period vanishes. Clipped to the back page, a Cayman rollover schedule specifies that upon forfeiture, the remaining $3,000,000 automatically transfers out of Boston probate into an offshore Cayman trust established by her father's trustee for Lydia's sole line.

Under the signature block, Graham has penciled three bridge-loan dates in neat block script, every one crossed out, followed by a fresh margin note: *Arlington buyout closes Friday if inventory seals.*

If the Arlington buyout closes before an audit locks his accounts, Graham plugs the hole in time, whereas a murder charge wipes him out overnight.

I shoot four quick photos of the demand letter and rollover page to catch the trust account number, the $3,000,000 balance, and the October fifteenth service stamp, then slide the pages back into the flap along their exact crease.

Shoes scrape across the kitchen floor. Ice clinks against metal, and the pantry switch clicks off. I slip back into the living room and drop onto the sofa seconds before Graham walks in with tongs and an ice bucket.

***

"Sorry about that," he says, and drops two clear cubes into my swapped glass with a sharp *clink*. He doesn't notice a thing. He picks up the tumbler from his tray, the one with the pale swirl settled at the bottom, and raises it toward the hearth. "Lawyers think a coastal gale runs on their billing cycle. To surviving Thursday."

I lift his original, clean glass and touch the rim to my lips. I let the burn of bourbon wet my mouth—God, I hate bourbon—swallow nothing, and set it back on the coaster.

Graham drinks.

He takes a slow swallow, gray eyes locked on mine over the rim. His throat works once, completely at ease. He lowers the glass back onto the tray and wipes a drop off his thumb.

Someone had prepared his usual glass while he was away, and out of host habit, he'd handed it right to me. Whatever was in it, I wasn't going to find out by drinking it.

"You're quiet tonight, Tessa," he says. He leans forward with his forearms on his knees and watches my face a little too closely. "Be honest with me. Did you run across anything strange in the master suite today? Anything out of place?"

"Mostly cashmere and three hatboxes," I lie, keeping my eyes on the fire. "Lydia's jewelry tray was intact: platinum band, pearl studs, Cartier tank watch with a dead battery. Logged all of it."

"And the medicine cabinet?"

"Ibuprofen, retinol, usual prescriptions. Nothing missing."

He looks back at the fire. "Good. Get some sleep. Tomorrow's going to be a long haul before the courier gets here."

I leave the clean bourbon two-thirds full on the coaster, say goodnight, and walk down the north hall to the guest suite. Once the privacy bolt is turned and the desk chair is wedged under the knob, I lean back against the door and exhale. My ribs have been braced since the study. Across the main hall, the hearth screen clanks shut as Graham banks the fire, and the radiator under my window ticks sharply as steam hits the pipes.

Behind the radiator, my brown store bag hasn't moved. I pull out my laptop and upload the four demand-letter photos to my off-site vault in Portland.

Before the upload bar finishes, my phone buzzes against my leg. The Kiefer Soundworks guest link expired at five. This is an encrypted text from Samira Patel, followed two seconds later by a second message: *I'm in bed with a migraine, Tess. Don't call me after eight unless someone's actually on fire.*

I tap the attachment. It's a port-clearance alert for Lydia Kiefer's passport, scanned Tuesday morning at St. Andrews Private Marina in New Brunswick.

I stare at the screen in the dark room until the display dims to black, then tap it awake again just to make sure the timestamp didn't move.
