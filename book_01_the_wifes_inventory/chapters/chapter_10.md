# Chapter 10

I make it through the mudroom side door three breaths before Graham's garage bay groans open.

My boots squelch once on the rubber mat. I kick them off beside the tray, hang my damp waxed jacket back on its peg—pressing the breast pocket flat so the rusted B-4 key doesn't clink against the brass hook—and take the back service stairs two at a time in my socks. By the time Graham's dress shoes cross the foyer floor below, I'm kneeling beside the master closet with a fresh strip of packing tape across my palm, pretending I've been sorting cashmere sweaters since four o'clock.

Twenty minutes later, Graham calls me down to the living room.

He has changed out of his charcoal suit into a dark merino crewneck, though his shoulders still carry the stiff tension of a man who spent four hours staring down an interstate. Woodsmoke and damp pine hang in the air. A fire mutters low behind the blackened hearth screen, filling the glass-walled room with dry birch heat while salt rain ticks steadily against the seaward panes. Outside the floor-to-ceiling windows, Salt Point Cove is a wall of wet ink, not a single dock light burning along the entire point.

"You look frozen," Graham says, gesturing toward the low leather sofa as he crosses to the walnut credenza. "The radiator in the guest wing isn't keeping up with this southeast wind. Have a drink before you turn in for the night."

"I'm fine with water, Graham."

"Nonsense. You've been hauling boxes alone all day." He reaches for the walnut credenza, pulls the stopper from a half-full bourbon decanter, and pours two fingers of amber liquor into the pair of heavy cut-crystal rocks glasses waiting on the silver tray. Out of host habit, he lifts the slightly heavier tumbler sitting front-left—his usual evening glass, judging by the faint thumb wear on the etched rim—sets it on the slate coaster nearest my elbow, and keeps the second glass in his right hand while lowering himself into the armchair opposite mine. "Besides, we need to lock down tomorrow's schedule before the Boston courier drives out."

I stare at the amber liquid catching the firelight. There's no ice in either glass, just neat bourbon. Graham's tumbler gleams jewel-clear—yet near the bottom curve of *mine*, a faint pale sediment swirls slowly through the liquor like undissolved chalk.

Upstairs in the master bath, white crystalline paste still cakes the inside of the brass P-trap. Someone tested dissolving crushed sleeping pills, then coated a single glass before Graham even drove home from Portland.

"Tomorrow's courier," I say, keeping my hands tucked around my knees instead of touching the coaster. "You're still expecting him at five?"

"Five sharp." Graham leans back, swirling his glass once. Shadows from the hearth flicker across the sharp hollow beneath his cheekbones, making him look older than he did Monday night. "The Arlington patent buyers want the preliminary probate schedule uploaded by five-thirty so their counsel can clear the lien release Friday morning. Which means every high-value item in the studio and master suite needs a signed tag and a timestamped photo by four."

"That's tight," I tell him, watching his eyes over the rim of his glass. "Especially if I'm still waiting on the boathouse inventory. You're asking me to sign an incomplete estate log under my own audit license."

Graham's fingers stop moving against the crystal. The fire pops once against the mesh screen, sending a sharp smell of scorched bark across the rug.

"Your Harbor Mutual license isn't active, Tessa," he says softly, his voice dropping an octave without losing its polite edge. "You're working as a private contractor under an NDA. Finish the house interior by four tomorrow, hand me the clean inventory, and the remaining ten thousand wires to your account before the courier hits the causeway. Walk away short of that, and we both know what the breach clause costs."

He didn't even need to raise his voice to remind me who holds the leash—or that my Civic is parked outside with a court judgment sitting on the passenger seat.

"Leave the boathouse off the preliminary schedule," he says. "I told you on Monday night—the county seal stays until the Coast Guard signs the final closure memo. Focus on what's inside the house."

*County seal.* He hadn't said a word about a county seal Monday night—only that the Coast Guard cleared the boathouse and locked it against damp. Now he's matching Halstead's script word for word.

Before I can ask who was sweeping a flashlight across the boathouse windows an hour ago, Graham's phone buzzes twice on the side table. He glances at the display, his mouth thinning.

"Hold that thought," he mutters, setting his untouched glass on the brass tray beside his chair. "That's my patent counsel in Boston. And let me grab ice for yours—the bottle was sitting near the radiator."

He steps out toward the foyer, already tapping the screen to his ear, leaving the living room empty except for the hiss of wet birch logs and two identical crystal glasses.

***

I don't waste a second.

The moment Graham's footsteps fade toward the kitchen archway, I lean across the low coffee table, lift my glass by its heavy base, and set it onto the brass tray beside his chair. Then I pick up his untouched glass, match the tilt of the coaster so it sits at the exact same angle he left it, and wipe the faint damp condensation ring off my slate coaster with my sleeve.

I set his glass down in front of me. The pour levels look close enough in the low firelight, though if Graham notices the faint chalky swirl at the bottom of his own tumbler, we'll both know the game is up before he even sets the ice bucket down.

If there's crushed zolpidem at the bottom of that bourbon, Graham can drink his own pour.

From the kitchen comes the muffled clatter of ice cubes hitting a stainless-steel bucket, followed by Graham's voice dropping low near the pantry corridor. He's pacing while he argues with Boston—snapping curtly about a bridge extension and an escrow holdback—which buys me two minutes, maybe three, before he hangs up and walks back through that arch.

I slip off the sofa without a sound and cross to the heavy walnut door of Graham's north-hall study.

The room smells of lemon furniture oil and cold stone. No desk lamp burns inside, just the pale spill of the hallway light across the wide walnut blotter. Yesterday morning this desk looked sterile as a model home. Tonight, a thick leather portfolio sits crooked beside the silver inkwell, and a strip-cut paper shredder tucked under the return hums faintly with a solid red **JAM** light, strips of cream bond paper wedged tight between its steel teeth. A half-torn wire-transfer receipt sticks out of the wastebasket below it, smudged with black toner.

I pull my phone from my jeans pocket, kill the shutter sound and flash, and lift the leather portfolio's cover.

Inside lies a heavy cream envelope slit along the top flap, stamped **CERTIFIED MAIL — RETURN RECEIPT REQUESTED**. Two pages of crisp bond paper sit folded halfway out, bearing the embossed letterhead of *Bramwell & Shaw Fiduciary LLP, Boston*, clipped to a highlighted trust covenant summary. A yellow sticky note clings to the upper margin in cramped legal shorthand: *Final cure window — no further extensions.*

I tilt the first page toward the hallway's amber spill and read fast.

*Notice of Default & Acceleration — Kiefer Soundworks LLC.*

Three clear rules sit outlined under the trust summary for *The Lydia Kiefer Irrevocable Trust*. First: Graham holds conditional joint signatory authority over the $5,400,000 corpus—and nearly three weeks ago, on October fifteenth, Bramwell & Shaw served him for $2,400,000 in misappropriated licensing collateral pulled over fourteen months, leaving $3,000,000 remaining. Second: Graham has a thirty-day civil cure window expiring November fourteenth—ten days left—before criminal referral to the Suffolk County DA, *unless* he is formally charged with murder or felony moral turpitude, which strips his signatory rights immediately and overrides the cure window. Third: clipped to the back page, a Cayman beneficiary rollover schedule confirms that upon Graham's forfeiture, the remaining $3,000,000 automatically transfers out of Boston probate into an offshore Cayman bearer structure controlled solely by Lydia.

Below the signature block, a handwritten pencil tally in Graham's neat block script lists three failed bridge-loan dates, every single one crossed out—followed by a fresh margin note: *Arlington buyout closes Friday if inventory seals.*

If the Arlington patent buyout clears before an audit locks his access, Graham gets the liquidity to cure his shortfall inside the November fourteenth deadline; if someone charges him with murder first, the moral-turpitude clause strips him overnight and drops three million offshore.

I snap four rapid photos of the demand letter and rollover rider—catching the trust account number, the $3,000,000 remaining corpus, and the October fifteenth service stamp—then smooth the bond paper back under the leather flap in its exact crease. Every second I linger beside this desk risks Graham walking down the hall and catching me standing over his personal mail.

Footsteps scrape across the kitchen slate. Ice clinks sharp against metal, followed by the soft *snick* of the pantry corridor light switch clicking off.

I slip back out the heavy walnut door, drop my phone into my pocket, and sink onto the leather sofa just as Graham steps back into the living room carrying silver ice tongs and a small crystal bucket.

***

"Sorry about that," Graham says, dropping two clear cubes into *my* swapped glass with a sharp *clink*. He doesn't notice the swap. He picks up the glass from his own brass tray—the one with the faint pale swirl settled at the bottom—and raises it toward the fire. "Lawyers think a coastal gale runs on their billing cycle. To surviving Thursday."

I lift my glass—his original, clean pour—and touch the rim to my lips without swallowing, letting the sharp burn of bourbon wet my mouth before setting it back on the slate coaster.

Graham drinks.

He takes a slow, measured swallow, his gray eyes fixed steadily on mine over the crystal rim. He doesn't grimace. He doesn't spit it out or pause mid-sip. He swallows and sets the glass back on the tray, wiping a drop off his thumb.

He didn't blink, and the second pour sits crystal-clear in front of me. The decanter wasn't poisoned at all. Someone pre-dusted *Graham's habitual rocks glass* on the credenza tray while he was in Portland, expecting him to pour himself a nightcap alone—and Graham handed it to me out of polite host reflex.

"You're quiet tonight, Tessa," he says softly, leaning forward with his forearms resting on his knees, watching my eyes with sharp, anxious focus as if waiting for me to drop a hint. "Tell me honestly. Did you find anything unusual in the master suite today? Anything that looked out of place?"

"Just a lot of cashmere and three hatboxes," I lie, keeping my gaze steady on the firelight while my fingers press against the phone in my pocket. "Lydia's jewelry tray was intact. Platinum band, pearl studs, Cartier tank watch with a dead battery. I logged them all."

"And the medicine cabinet?"

"Ibuprofen, retinol, standard prescriptions," I say. "Nothing missing."

A tiny flash of disappointment crosses his gray eyes before he looks back at the fire. "Good. Get some sleep. Tomorrow's going to be a long haul before the courier arrives."

I leave the glass two-thirds full on the coaster, say goodnight, and walk down the north hall to the guest suite. Once the privacy latch clicks shut and I wedge the desk chair tight under the knob, I lean back against the panel, catching my breath. Two point four million dollars in trust embezzlement. Three million poised to roll into a Cayman bearer structure. A deputy with co-owner installer PINs. A flashlight sweeping inside a locked boathouse. Across the main hall, the hearth screen clanks shut as Graham banks the fire for the night, and the cast-iron radiator under my window ticks sharply as steam surges through the pipes.

I cross to the radiator, check that the brown paper hardware bag tucked behind the iron fins hasn't been shifted, and unzip the main compartment of the faded navy duffel. The Portsmouth bank band tucked under my spare shirts sits untouched—all five thousand dollars intact—and the laptop sleeps cool against the liner. I pull the laptop out, thumb the power button, and plug in my phone cable to push the demand-letter photos straight into my off-site vault.

Before the upload bar even finishes, my phone vibrates sharply against my thigh.

Not Graham's Kiefer Soundworks app—that guest link expired at five. It's an encrypted text from Samira Patel, an old coastal marine-claims investigator in Portland who still holds legitimate read-only access to the Atlantic Marine Underwriters' cross-border port-clearance watch. Yesterday afternoon from Salt Point harbor, while I had four bars at the store, I texted her Lydia Kiefer's passport number off the public Coast Guard missing-person bulletin on a long shot. St. Andrews Private Marina syncs its small-craft customs manifests on a twenty-four-hour batch—and Samira just forwarded the automated clearance alert straight from the evening run.

I tap the attachment.

**ATLANTIC MARINA PORT-CLEARANCE WATCH — PRIORITY HIT**

**NAME:** KIEFER, LYDIA S.

**DOCUMENT:** US PASSPORT #••••••3907

**PORT:** ST. ANDREWS PRIVATE MARINA, NEW BRUNSWICK

**TIMESTAMP:** NOV 3, 2026 — 09:42 AST

I stare at the glowing white text in the dark bedroom until my eyes burn. Yesterday morning. While Graham stood in this kitchen telling me she drowned nearly three weeks ago, Lydia Kiefer walked off a dock across the Canadian border and scanned her own passport.
