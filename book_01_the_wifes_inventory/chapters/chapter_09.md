# Chapter 9

The temporary guest-feed link on my phone chimes with a thirty-minute expiration warning just as coastal fog settles over the headland like wet iron.

In the mudroom, I pull my jacket off the hook beside Graham's coat, my knuckles brushing the tissue-wrapped B-4 key inside the lining. Upstairs, three taped wardrobe cartons sit stacked in the master closet, enough logged work to hold up at a glance when Graham gets home.

He texted from Portland at two-thirty to say a patent attorney ran late and he wouldn't clear the causeway until dark. That leaves me maybe thirty or forty minutes before his headlights hit the drive.

I ease the mudroom door shut, hug the cedar siding under the deck camera's blind spot, and cut across wet grass toward the spruces. Past the eave lights, the bluff drops steep toward Salt Point Cove along a stone path chewed out of the ledge by generations of lobstermen. Rainwater runs down the risers in cold threads, turning the railroad ties slick as grease. Twice my boots slide on wet pine needles. I catch myself on spruce bark that scrapes my palms, and cold water seeps straight through the seam of my left boot until my toes go numb.

Down here the Atlantic wind drives spray up the rocks. Below me, the tide pulls back off mussel beds and leaves the pilings dark and wet. Halfway down the switchback, diesel fumes drift up from the shingle beach.

Maren Lindqvist's sorting shed squats where the ledge flattens into crushed shell, a shingle box tied down with turnbuckles and one amber bulb glowing over the loading door. Beside it, a dented skiff rocks against rubber fenders on an iron ringbolt. My boots slip on kelp before I hit solid shell.

Maren stands under the tin awning beside black plastic oyster cages, pulling rubber bands off culled shells in orange gloves. At the crunch of my heels, her oyster knife freezes over the sorting table. She looks up from under her wool cap.

"You're not supposed to be down here," she says over the surf. "Road's clear up top. You can still drive out."

"I'm not leaving tonight, Maren." I stop just outside the spill of the bulb. "And we both know Deputy Halstead didn't walk into the Kiefer house at eleven this morning to check a mooring line."

She sets the knife down with a dull *clack* and tugs both gloves off, her knuckles red and swollen from brine.

"Didn't see any deputy." She turns back to her cages. "I was out on the outer lines till noon."

"He came up from the cove trail." I step onto the duckboards so she can't get past me unless she goes inside. "Used a six-digit co-owner installer code on the back pantry door, bridged the exterior camera logs inside the breaker box, and checked the salt-free life vest in Graham's mudroom like he was verifying a stage prop."

Maren looks up at the dark bluff where the glass house hides in the mist. Nothing up there shows through the fog.

"Shouldn't be standing under a yellow bulb," she mutters. She wipes her damp hands on her apron. "Anybody on the water with binoculars sees two coats talking."

She stands there a second listening to chop slap her skiff and wind rattle the tin eaves.

***

"Get inside before somebody on the ridge spots you." She bumps the plank door open with her hip.

The sorting shed is cramped, damp, and fiercely warm in the middle, where a kerosene heater hums beside a workbench stacked with hose fittings and tide almanacs. I stay near the door while Maren pours coffee from a steel thermos into a chipped mug and slides it onto a crate by my elbow. The dark ring inside the rim says burnt chicory.

"Drink it or don't." She curls her red hands around a second mug. "I told you at the store, Miss Calder—I don't hold with mainland trouble. My oyster lease comes up in January, and Cole Halstead's marine unit boards my brother-in-law's lobster boat twice a month whenever somebody opens their mouth."

"I'm not going to the county." My fingers are numb enough that the hot ceramic barely registers, and I leave the mug where it is. "Whoever Halstead's working with just shoved my deadline up to tomorrow night. If I walk out before I finish the inventory, Graham hits me with a breach penalty I can't pay, and I lose the fee keeping my apartment off a sheriff's sale. So unless you want me living in my car in your gravel lot, we're having this conversation."

She goes still, suddenly wary. "Deadline for what?"

"Closing out the probate inventory." I lean closer over the workbench so she can hear me over the heater. "You slipped that tide chart into my bag yesterday to warn me about two-fourteen in the morning. Today Halstead walked into the kitchen while Graham was in Portland and told somebody on a burner phone to stay tucked out of sight down the cove until dusk."

Maren stares into her coffee and says nothing. Her mouth sets in a hard line, and she reaches over to turn the heater's damper down until the flame stops guttering.

"Shouldn't have put that slip in your bag," she says, eyes on the floorboards. "I'm three months behind on winter dockage. Sixty days from losing my mooring. You think I'm lining up to testify against a Knox County deputy?"

"Nobody's asking you to testify. I'm asking why you warned me twice if you're scared of Cole Halstead."

Her cracked thumb rubs hard along the edge of the bench. When she speaks, her voice is low and reluctant. "Because I took five thousand dollars cash to forget somebody walked down my trail. Folded bills dropped right on this bench so I'd keep my mouth shut and get the harbor master off my back. Sold my eyes once to keep my pilings out of foreclosure. Told myself it was rich folks' divorce mess."

She looks up, pale eyes hard with disgust at herself. "Doesn't mean I'm watching somebody from off-point get hung out to dry on my beach just so I stay solvent. You repeat my name to a deputy or a Boston lawyer, though, I'll swear on a stack of Bibles you're crazy and I never spoke two words to you."

"Fair."

She sets her mug beside a pair of wire cutters and glances toward the doorway like she expects boots on the shell. "That's why I held up two fingers on the flat yesterday. I saw two silhouettes down by the boathouse doors at two-fourteen the night of the storm. Everybody in Salt Point figures Graham Kiefer ran his wife out past the bell buoy because his patent money dried up. Guy's cold enough to freeze bait, sure, and he never looked twice at the water before he married her."

"And you don't buy it?"

Maren drops her voice low over the surf. "Graham doesn't know a cleat hitch from a shoelace. Put him in that skiff in a southeast blow at two in the morning and he'd have piled it onto the ledges before he cleared the cove mouth."

"Then who took it out?"

"Somebody who planned for it two days early." Maren shifts her weight, the floorboards creaking under her boots. "Lydia came down my cliff trail Monday afternoon—two days before the gale hit. Thin sweater, no coat, shivering like a wet cat and ruining leather loafers in the kelp. That five-grand envelope? She dropped it right next to my twine spools before she even said what she wanted."

"What did she ask for?"

"Told me to keep my dock lights killed and stay indoors behind drawn shades once the blow came in. Claimed she was shooting night photos and didn't want floodlights ruining the water." Maren's mouth twists. "While I was counting the bills, she picked a brass bracket off my bench and started asking about boat trackers—whether a hard squall blocks the shore signal along the point if a boat shuts its radio off."

"Did she give an excuse for asking about the radio?"

"Said she was doing a magazine piece on night poaching." Maren lets out a dry snort. "Woman couldn't tell a Jonah crab from a quahog, and suddenly she's worried about boat trackers. Now get out of here before full dark drops. I've already said ten times more than my mooring is worth. Graham or Cole catches you coming off my beach, they'll lock that gate before you hit your car."

***

I step back onto crushed shell. Night has closed fast over the cove. Fog rolls off the water and smears Maren's amber bulb into a dull smudge behind me before I even reach the granite stairs.

A planned disappearance doesn't need a painted-shut studio window sash or a deputy sneaking in through the pantry. Someone's staging the house before tomorrow's courier arrives, and if I don't get back upstairs before Graham clears the causeway, I'm going to have to explain why my boots are soaked through with cove water.

Every wet riser jars my knees in the dark. Halfway up, my thighs burn and my ears ache from the wind. I stop where the cove trail joins the main cliff stairs on a narrow cedar landing under a twisted pitch pine, bracing a hand against the bark to catch my breath.

Below me, the seawall curves black against the outgoing tide. Through spruce boughs, the pitched roof of the boathouse squats over the water, its double doors still padlocked at the hasp.

I'm turning back toward the upper stairs when white light cuts across the boathouse's frosted side window—the exact cliff-side window that was shuttered from inside yesterday morning. A flashlight beam sweeps slowly left to right behind the glass, catching the dark curve of a suspended kayak hull for half a second before dipping below the sill.

*Stay tucked out of sight down the cove until dusk,* Cole told his burner caller. The padlock still hangs on the doors, yet someone is moving around inside.

Before I can take a step back down toward the seawall, gravel crunches through the fog up on the ridge. Twin headlights cut through the spruces as the gate groans open, and Graham's Audi sweeps into the turnaround.
