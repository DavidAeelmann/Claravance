# Chapter 14

"How did you get past the deadbolt?" Graham's voice comes down the concrete stairwell thin and uneven. "The hall sensor never chimed."

Cold air drifts from the open front hall behind him, and my fingers curl around the multi-tool. He stands in the fire doorway with gray dawn light at his back and an iron pipe wrench hanging low in his right hand, checking my face first, then the manila envelope tucked under my arm, and finally the worktable where the server rack hums beneath the lit blue displays.

"Didn't use the hall door." I keep the worktable between us. "Drop the wrench, Graham. Now."

He takes a slow step onto the concrete. He clocks the pried-open gray cabinet marked `ARCHIVE — DO NOT ERASE`, then the cut strips of red tamper tape hanging off the kraft flap.

"You broke into my cabinet," he says.

"I wanted to see why five o'clock today matters so much." I keep my voice level. "I already shot your *Bramwell & Shaw* default notice in the study last night: two-point-four million, thirty-day cure window, referral to the Suffolk County DA. Those photos are already sitting in my off-site vault in Portland. Swing that wrench, or throw that deadbolt again, and if I miss my noon check-in my contact there forwards the vault link directly to Bramwell & Shaw's inbox."

It's a half-bluff. Samira doesn't have my vault credentials or a noon clock, yet Graham can't afford to test it.

He stops dead beside the steel jamb. He looks down at the pipe wrench as if he's only just realizing he's holding it, and his fingers open. The metal hits the bottom concrete tread with a dull *clang* and rolls up against the doorframe.

"I didn't lock you down here, Tessa." He raises both empty hands to chest height and squints against the blue glare. "I drank half a bourbon last night and woke up on the living room sofa at quarter to six, head feeling like wet cement."

"Funny. You seemed wide awake when you poured it."

"Somebody dried a clear dose into the cut-crystal grooves of my tumbler on the credenza while I was in Portland. When I got home, I poured into my usual glass and handed it to you out of habit. Only way I ended up out cold on that sofa is if you spotted the haze at the bottom and switched them while I was getting ice."

"You didn't throw the deadbolt at two-thirty?"

"I didn't move until quarter to six. I heard the fans running when I woke up. My keys were on the rug, a chair was shoved under the pantry knob, and the front door was standing wide open." He rubs his eyes like he's trying to scrape the Ambien off them, then stops and stares past my shoulder at the dark opening behind the iron ladder. "Wait—is that the old dumbwaiter shaft? Somebody came down through the pantry, saw you sitting in the blue light, and threw the deadbolt on their way out so you couldn't follow them. Come up. If I were the one who locked you down there, I wouldn't be standing here unlocking it."

***

I don't trust him for half a second, though staying in a concrete box with one door isn't a plan either. I pocket the multi-tool, grab the wrench off the bottom step, and follow him up into the kitchen at arm's length.

Thin gray morning light washes across the island. Rain streaks down the back deck glass. Graham doesn't look for another weapon. He walks to the sink, fills a tall glass with cold tap water, and drinks it down in three hard swallows before leaning both palms on the counter with his head hung between his shoulders. Whatever was at the bottom of that bourbon hit him hard; his fingers are still shaking. My left heel is cold from the cellar floor, and I'm tired enough that the sound of him swallowing gets under my skin.

"You forged Lydia's initials on six trust transfers," I say from the far side of the island. I set the envelope next to the toaster; the thumb drive stays hidden in my watch pocket. "October tenth, four days before the storm. You even sliced three signed study pages out of her sketchbook to trace her initials on a light table."

Graham exhales slowly, straightens, and squares his shoulders despite the tremor in his hands.

"Those draws were temporary collateral advances against my defense-patent equity," he says, though he aims his answer past my shoulder instead of at me. "Every dollar went into Kiefer Soundworks payroll and the Cambridge lab lease while Arlington cleared export review. As joint signatory, I had operational authority to bridge liquidity—"

"Without your wife's signature," I cut in. "You cut three signed leaves out of the back of her sketchbook so you could trace her initials on a light table without leaving grooves on the pad. Don't call forgery a bridge loan, Graham."

He looks down at the sliced red tamper tape on the envelope, and whatever excuse he was lining up dies on his tongue. "That sketchbook on the studio blotter is why I locked the studio door Monday night after I caught you in there," he admits. "If Arlington closed on time, every cent went back into the trust with interest. Nobody loses a dime."

"Except Lydia caught it, and she didn't hand you over for wire fraud."

"Because a civil fraud case wouldn't strip me bare fast enough for her," he fires back before catching himself and pulling his voice down. "Look at the timeline, Tessa. I didn't touch her on October fourteenth."

"You told the Coast Guard you came upstairs at midnight."

"Because I didn't want to explain staying sealed inside a soundproofed cellar all night when my wife walked into a gale." He wipes a shaking hand across his face. "Truth is, I was down there wearing noise-canceling headphones until three in the morning, when the storm finally dropped the grid. When I came up, the sunroom glass was latched, the skiff was gone off the slip, and Deputy Halstead was walking up my drive at five-thirty telling me not to go near the boathouse."

"Halstead," I say, and watch his face. "He was in this kitchen yesterday at eleven, when you were in Portland. Punched a co-owner installer PIN right into your wall tablet and bridged your deck camera logs inside the breaker box."

Graham stares at me, dead still. "He doesn't have an installer PIN. Nobody knows that code except me and—"

He cuts himself off. His fingers dig hard into the edge of the stone counter.

"Lydia," I finish.

He nods slowly. "October twenty-first, a week after the storm, I caught a six-minute hole in the basement audio from the fifteenth. Started checking the house, looked at the mudroom, and realized the life vest Halstead logged still smelled like tap water—fresh lot stamp that never came off our boat. I took Zone Four off the Portland security link that same afternoon so I could run wind filters in private."

"Which is why you swiped Tuesday night's motion alert off the pantry tablet," I cut in.

"Yes," he says, rubbing his temple. "With the hall fire door shut right in front of us, I figured the generator switchover tripped my own bypass. I didn't even know that dumbwaiter shaft opened."

"And you didn't call state police."

"Walk into a barracks screaming setup while sitting on forged trust draws?" His mouth twists. "They'd have locked me up before they ever checked the vest."

"Somebody bleached the sunroom floor under the wool rug right before I got here too," I say. "Linseed oil's still tacky under the backing. Whoever pried the baseboard dropped a B-4 key behind the trim and missed it when they hammered the board back. Which still doesn't explain why you hired a blacklisted fraud investigator under a gag clause to box up sweaters."

He glances down the north hall toward his study, weighing how much else I've already seen. "The Arlington probate deadline is real, Tessa. Anything else stays encrypted on my drive—not coming out of my mouth while you're standing there with a pipe wrench."

He's still keeping something back. Worse, whoever threw that deadbolt at two-thirty now knows I found the cellar, and knows I didn't stay locked inside. Great.

***

"Then explain the video," I say. "I saw the paused frame on your basement display, Graham. Two-fourteen in the morning. A man in a hooded slicker dragging a rolled canvas rug out of the sunroom toward the open double doors of the boathouse."

He shakes his head. "That's what I've been trying to pick apart since October twenty-first. Somebody spoofed the exterior feed, or put a man my height in my spare foul-weather slicker and walked straight past the infrared lens while the indoor breaker was tripped. Look at my drafting table down there. Would a man who staged his own wife's drowning spend two weeks working out how a fiberglass skiff could even clear the east shoal at high water?"

The cuff on that frozen display had ridden up over a thick wrist and a metal watchband. Not Graham's build, and Graham was ninety miles south on I-95 when those three passphrases failed at four-forty-five.

"Lydia's passport was scanned at a private marina in St. Andrews, New Brunswick, Tuesday morning," I tell him.

Graham goes completely still, his hand slipping half an inch off the stone edge. "She's *alive*?"

"Unless somebody else is crossing the Canadian border with her passport." I jerk my chin toward the front hall arch. "And what about the duck boots dripping by the basement stairs? Those are Lydia's missing pair from the master closet."

His mouth opens, then closes. "I didn't take those out of the closet, Tessa," he says, and steps around the island toward the front hall. "Come look."

I follow him to the front entry archway. Rain beads across the low overhead skylight above the coat bench, and cold gray light falls onto a dark puddle by the stairwell door.

"And the olive weekender shoved behind your wool coats?" I nod toward the tall closet beside the bench. "Bar Harbor–Yarmouth, Slip Four, stamped October seventeenth, three days after you say she drowned."

Graham turns sharply, yanks the closet door open, and shoves two overcoats aside. He stares down at the canvas bag on the floor, his hand dropping off the wool sleeve. "That wasn't there Sunday night. Halstead came by Monday morning to drop off a supplemental marine report while I was down in the cellar. He stood right here in this entryway for five minutes."

Past him, through the tall glass sidelights, mist curls over the wet gravel turnaround. My Civic is parked beside the trees, the trunk lid sitting flush enough at a glance until you notice the dark gap along the weatherstripping where the latch never caught.

Graham points down at the duck boots and the navy tote dripping brackish rainwater onto the entry floor.

"Muddy boot prints right across the gravel to your rear bumper," he says, gesturing toward the sidelights. "A corner of blue tarp was pinched in the weatherstripping, keeping the lid from latching. I pulled Lydia's boots and that tote out before I saw what was under them in the spare-tire well—a bleach-soaked boat rag wrapped around a bronze cleat from our dock. I didn't touch either one. Just eased the lid back down. Halstead serves a warrant on your Civic, they pin that sunroom on both of us."

I stare out at the Civic's rear bumper. Going out there for the cleat puts my own prints on the cleanup hardware, and whoever's watching from the cove will take notes—or maybe they're already halfway down the drive, which doesn't leave me much time to be clever. Calling the state police over the house Wi-Fi right now just brings troopers into a house full of Graham's forged transfers while Lydia's boots sit in my spare-tire well, and I am not leaving Salt Point in handcuffs because I couldn't wait for a thumb drive to decrypt.

"Give me the passphrase for the raw basement partition," I say. "Right now."

Graham shakes his head and nudges the muddy duck boots and tote out of sight behind the coat bench where nobody watching the sidelights can spot them. "Whoever hammered those three failed passphrases into the cellar console at four-forty-five yesterday tripped a twenty-two-hour lockout across the master key. Even with the passphrase, the study bridge won't mount that partition until after three this afternoon. And Lydia has co-owner view on the shared probate folder. If your inventory photo timestamps stop ticking into the cloud this morning, she and Cole know we're onto them and hit this house before the lockout clears. Shoot the upstairs rooms to keep our cover live; the master key unlocks by three-thirty."

I look down at the muddy rubber toe peeking out from under the bench. He's still lying about something, I'm still holding his stolen ledger, and neither of us has a better move than pretending to pack sweaters until three-thirty.
