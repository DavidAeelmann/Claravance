# Chapter 22

Rain pours through the open skylight and turns the dark stone of the vestibule slick under strobing red and blue light. Lydia already has one boot hooked onto the cedar roof frame above her head, her torn jacket sleeve dark with blood. Before she can pull herself up onto the wet shingles, I drop the oyster knife onto the floor, lunge onto the coat bench beside her, and grab the back of her collar.

"Let go!" she shrieks, and kicks backward.

Her boot heel catches my left knee hard and nearly buckles my leg. I don't let go. I drop my weight backward, the bench tips out from under us, and both of us hit the wet stone together. Lydia's elbow cracks against the floor, and the small backup drive shoots out of her grip and skids across the hall toward the stairs.

Graham doesn't lift a finger to help her. He just stands back against the wall in the flashing light and watches the woman who tried to bury him thrash on the floor.

"Stay off that drive," I pant. I pin Lydia's good wrist against the stone with my knee while my duffel strap digs into my collarbone. "Don't move."

Heavy boots hammer up the granite steps outside. A bright flashlight cuts through the glass sidelight beside the door, followed by three hard knocks that rattle the frame.

"Maine State Police! Open the door!"

I dig the brass front-door key out of my jeans with my free hand and toss it across the vestibule to Graham. "Unlock the deadbolt. Now."

Graham catches it against his chest, stares at me for half a beat, then turns and works the double-keyed lock beside the sidelight. The door swings wide. Wind and pine needles blow in behind two Maine State Troopers in dripping yellow slickers, weapon lights up and pistols at the low-ready. Out through the doorway, a third officer jogs up the gravel from a heavy high-clearance State Police tactical 4x4 parked nose-in behind my Civic.

"Hands up! Both of you, off the woman on the floor!"

I lift both hands and step back off Lydia's wrist as the lead trooper drops down and snaps steel cuffs behind her back.

"That backup drive by the stairs came off the study tower," I tell him, and nod toward the tiles. "And keep everybody out of the upstairs studio. There's a silver Montblanc on the desk that needs latent prints before anyone touches it."

Lydia glares up from the floor, blood smeared across her cheekbone where her sleeve brushed her face. She keeps her mouth shut.

The second trooper sweeps his light over Graham, then toward the open cedar door under the stairs. "Anybody else in the house?"

"Deputy Cole Halstead." I point down the hall. "Locked in the basement darkroom behind the steel fire door. Armed, slashed left wrist. The exterior deadbolt's thrown from this side."

I back clear of the stairwell so the backup trooper can move past. "Cover the spice-rack wall in the walk-in pantry too. There's an old dumbwaiter shaft behind the shelves that drops right into that room."

He gives one short nod, racks his shotgun, and moves into the dark hall.

***

Twenty minutes later, generator floodlights off the State Police rescue rig wash the gravel turnaround in harsh white glare. I stand under the rear awning of a Knox County ambulance with a scratchy wool blanket over my wet sweater as two troopers walk a cuffed Cole Halstead out the front door toward a waiting cruiser. Fresh white gauze wraps his left forearm. He keeps his eyes dead ahead, never glancing at the SUV where Lydia sits in the back, and never looking at me.

Maren Lindqvist stands beside the rescue rig's idling fender in her yellow oilskin bib with a thermos pressed against her chest. Her silver braid is plastered wet to her collar.

"Parked my pickup on the high mainland shoulder before the surge, then walked the shoreline path to check my skiff's storm chain," she says as I step out from the awning into the drizzle. "Found your gouged tide card jammed behind my shed latch. Saw that and Cole's dark cruiser wedged sideways across the causeway, so I drove straight to Thomaston. Dispatch couldn't raise him on county radio. Troopers let me ride back across the washout in the storm rig to point out the lane."

"Thank you," I say, my throat raw from salt water and rust dust. "If you hadn't—"

"I took her money once," Maren cuts in. She glances toward the tinted glass of the lead SUV. "Wasn't letting her bury two people so I could keep my dock."

A plainclothes State Police detective steps away from the open rear hatch of the tactical rig with a clipboard and an evidence box. He's lean, gray at the temples, his badge clipped to the belt of a soaked nylon windbreaker.

"Ms. Calder. Detective Sergeant Ward," he says, and flips open a rain-spotted pad. "Techs have secured the Zone Four server. Preliminary check shows the boathouse recording is intact. We've logged Halstead's cruiser sideways on the causeway, the iPad Mini by the sunroom radiator, the bleach rag and bronze cleat in your trunk, and the bleach jugs and scratched oars down in the boathouse."

"Good," I say. I slide the wet navy duffel off my shoulder and set it on the rescue rig's tailgate. "There's more."

Ward pauses, pen poised over an evidence bag. "Show me what you pulled."

I unzip the outer pouch under the floodlights, my scraped palm stinging in the cold. I set down the matte-black thumb drive (`RAW_BUFFER_OCT14_15`), the yellow warning slip Cole wrote on Tuesday, and the knotted nitrile glove finger holding my foil-wrapped P-trap swab. "The raw overnight audio from the storm: Cole and Lydia in the sunroom and boathouse around two-fourteen. Plus Cole's warning slip as a left-handed sample, and a swab of white paste I pulled from the master bath P-trap."

Ward bags each piece with gloved fingers and waves an evidence tech over. "Have the studio team print that silver Montblanc before anyone moves it," he tells the tech. "And pull Halstead's October fifteenth report so documents can compare his writing against this slip and that smudged checklist on the blotter. I want his prints, his installer logs, and the two-fourteen audio lined up together."

"There's a half-empty rocks glass on the living-room tray with pale sediment at the bottom," I add, and gesture back toward the house. "And an orange life vest hanging on mudroom peg two. It looked factory-clean when I checked it, and I have shots of the lot stamp backed up in my cloud account off my phone. There's also a fireplace poker down in Zone Four and an oyster knife on the vestibule floor."

Ward makes three quick notes and murmurs into his shoulder mic to have the lab pull the glass, the vest, and the weapons. Then he looks back down at my open duffel.

Across the wet gravel, Graham stands beside an unmarked sedan with a uniformed trooper at his elbow while another officer takes a preliminary statement. He catches my eye through the rain, waiting to see what I do.

My fingers rest on the damp canvas zipper. Hand over what's still buried under my spare shirts, and I kiss that ten-thousand-dollar completion check goodbye, along with the fifty grand and clean courthouse balance he offered me over the stove. I drive back to Portland broke, with an eighteen-thousand-dollar judgment still hanging over my license and no next job lined up.

I hold Graham's gaze, reach deep into the main pocket of my duffel, and pull out the padded manila envelope stamped `OCT 14 — MASTER BACKUP / DO NOT OPEN BEFORE PROBATE`. I set it down on the tailgate.

"And this," I tell Ward. "Graham Kiefer's unredacted trust ledger and three pages sliced out of his wife's sketchbook. Two-point-four million pulled from her father's estate, with trust initials that look traced. He tried to burn it on the kitchen stove tonight so nobody would look past his wife's setup."

Across the drive, Graham shuts his eyes and turns his head away from the lights. He doesn't pull back when the trooper reaches for his wrists.

Ward studies the thick manila envelope before looking up at me. "You worked for him under an NDA, Ms. Calder."

"NDAs don't cover felony fraud or burning evidence," I say. "Take all of it."

***

An hour later, the gale has died down to a cold drizzle. Most of the cruisers have pulled out, and the ambulance bay hums under overhead LEDs. Diesel exhaust drifts off the wet gravel and mixes with the sharp bite of antiseptic from inside the rig. Down by the cove, a portable generator still drones outside the boathouse while crime-scene techs finish bagging the gear.

I sit on the metal ambulance step with a paper cup of black coffee warming my palms while a paramedic clips the last thread on three neat stitches across my left wrist and tapes fresh gauze over the seam. The wool blanket smells of bleach.

Detective Ward walks back over from the yellow tape strung across the Kiefer porch, his boots crunching on wet gravel. In his gloved hand he carries a pink carbon-copy State Police property receipt and a clear evidence bag holding my cracked, waterlogged phone. A trooper fished it off a submerged slipway cross-beam with a boat hook before the surge could wash it out into the bay.

"Preliminary inventory on the house and Mr. Kiefer's coat is wrapped," Ward says as he stops under the yellow bay light. "Coat pockets held his Audi fob and a Portland fiduciary courier receipt—nothing else. Your Civic's trunk is shot and cleared; flatbed can pull you off once the tide drops below the culvert."

I set my coffee down and sign the clipboard with stiff, bandaged fingers. "What about the five-thousand-dollar cash retainer in my duffel?"

"Logged as disputed estate funds pending probate review, though your signed margin rider and the *Bramwell and Shaw* default file back up your contract," Ward says. He tears off the pink copy and hands it over. "Keep your contact info current with the Rockland DA's clerk once you replace that phone. They'll need you in person for grand jury testimony."

I fold the pink receipt and slide it into my damp jeans beside my multi-tool. Overhead, the low clouds thin out over Penobscot Bay—no completion check in my pocket, a dead phone in an evidence bag, and Monday's court levy still waiting on Congress Street.
