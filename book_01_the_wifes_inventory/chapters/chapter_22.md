# Chapter 22

Rain drives straight down through the open skylight, turning the slate foyer into a black mirror under the strobing blue-and-red glare.

Lydia has one boot on the cedar frame above her head, her torn waxed-canvas sleeve dark with blood. Before she can haul her weight onto the slick cedar roof, I drop the oyster knife onto the floor, lunge onto the bench beside her, and grab the back of her jacket collar.

"Let go!" she shrieks, kicking backward with her heel.

Her boot catches my bruised knee. Pain flares white behind my eyes, sharp enough to steal my breath, but I don't release the waxed canvas. I haul backward with everything left in my shoulders. The bench tips beneath us. Both of us crash hard onto the wet floor together, Lydia's elbow cracking against the stone as the backup drive skitters across the tiles toward the stairs.

Graham doesn't move to help her. He stands back against the wall, his bruised face slack in the pulsing crimson light, watching his wife thrash on the floor he tried to scrub clean.

"Don't touch that drive," I pant, pinning Lydia's good wrist against the stone with my knee while my duffel strap digs into my collarbone. "And don't move."

Heavy boots pound up the granite front steps outside. A high-powered flashlight beam sweeps across the frosted sidelight beside the door, followed by three sharp knocks that rattle the cedar jamb.

"Maine State Police! Open the door!"

I reach into my jeans pocket with my free hand, pull out the brass front-door key, and toss it across the hall to Graham. "Turn the deadbolt. Now."

Graham catches the key against his chest. For half a second he stares at me, then turns and fumbles the brass lock open.

The front door swings wide. Freezing gale-force wind and pine needles blow straight into the foyer behind two Maine State Troopers in dripping yellow slickers, tactical flashlights leveled and weapons drawn at the low-ready. Behind them, through the open doorway, a third officer in a high-visibility vest jogs up the gravel from a massive, high-clearance State Police tactical 4x4 rig—the kind built to ford storm-surge washouts and push right past a deputy's cruiser wedged sideways across the causeway throat.

"Hands up! Both of you, step back from the woman on the floor!"

I raise both hands slowly, stepping off Lydia's wrist as the lead trooper moves in and snaps steel cuffs behind her back.

"That backup drive by the stairs came off the study tower," I tell him, nodding toward the tiles. "And seal the upstairs studio—there's a silver Montblanc fountain pen on the blotter that needs latent print lifts before anyone touches it."

Lydia glares up at me from the floor, blood slicking her cheek where her forearm brushed her face. She doesn't speak. She doesn't have to.

The second trooper sweeps his beam toward Graham, then toward the open cedar door under the stairs. "Who else is in the house?"

"Deputy Cole Halstead," I say, pointing toward the basement stairwell. "Locked in the basement darkroom behind the steel fire door. Armed, with a slashed left wrist. The exterior brass deadbolt is thrown from the hall."

I step clear of the stairs so the backup trooper can move in. "And watch the spice-rack wall inside the pantry," I add as he passes. "There's a manual dumbwaiter shaft behind the shelves that drops straight into that basement room. Nobody clears the stairwell without covering the shaft."

The trooper nods once, racks his shotgun, and disappears into the dark foyer.

***

Twenty minutes later, the generator-powered floodlights on the State Police rescue rig turn the gravel forecourt into a blinding white stage.

I stand under the rear awning of a Knox County ambulance, a stiff wool blanket draped over my wet sweater, watching two troopers escort a cuffed, stone-faced Cole Halstead out the front door toward a waiting cruiser. His left forearm is wrapped in fresh white gauze. He doesn't look at Lydia, who sits silent in the back of the lead SUV, and he doesn't look at me.

Beside the rescue rig's diesel-rumbling fender, Maren Lindqvist stands in her yellow oilskin bib, a thermos steaming between her cracked knuckles. Her silver braid is plastered to her collar, and her eyes meet mine with quiet, steady relief.

"Parked my pickup on the high mainland shoulder before the surge, then walked down the shoreline path to check my skiff's storm chain," Maren says as I step out from the awning into the drizzle, my sneakers squelching on wet gravel. "Found your carved tide chart behind my shed latch. Saw that, Cole's dark cruiser across the causeway, and his county radio dead—so I drove straight to Thomaston and told them they had a live framing on Salt Point."

"Thank you," I say. My voice comes out hoarse, raw from salt water and smoke. "If you hadn't—"

"I took her money once," Maren interrupts softly, glancing toward the cruiser where Lydia sits behind tinted glass. "Wasn't going to let her bury two people to keep my dock afloat."

A plainclothes Maine State Police detective—lean, gray-templed, wearing a drenched nylon windbreaker with a badge clipped to his belt—steps away from the open rear hatch of the tactical rig and walks over with a clipboard and an evidence box.

"Ms. Calder. Detective Sergeant Ward," he says, flipping open a rain-spotted pad. "Techs pulled the Zone Four server—your boathouse audio's intact. We've got Halstead's cruiser sideways on the causeway, the iPad Mini by the sunroom radiator, the rag and cleat in your trunk, and the bleach jugs and oars down in the boathouse."

"Good," I say, lifting the faded navy canvas duffel off my shoulder and setting it squarely on the tailgate of the rescue rig. "Because the house holds the rest of the paper trail."

Ward pauses, his pen hovering over the plastic evidence seal. "Let's see what you pulled before the storm hit."

I unzip the duffel and lay the items out under the harsh rig floodlights, keeping my hands steady despite the cold biting into my scraped palm.

First, I set down the encrypted matte-black thumb drive (`RAW_BUFFER_OCT14_15`) beside the yellow warning slip Cole wrote on Tuesday and the knotted glove finger holding my foil-wrapped P-trap swab. "The raw October fourteenth audio—Cole and Lydia in the sunroom and boathouse at two-fourteen. Plus the warning slip Cole wrote left-handed on Tuesday, and a swab of white paste I pulled out of the master bath P-trap."

Ward bags each item with gloved fingers, gesturing to a waiting evidence tech beside the rig. "Have the studio team print that silver Montblanc before anyone touches it," he tells the tech. "And pull Halstead's October fifteenth incident report so documents can match his writing against this slip and that smudged note on the blotter. I want his prints, his installer logs, and the two-fourteen tape lined up together."

"Grab the crystal rocks glass off the living-room tray too," I tell him, nodding toward the glass house. "See if the sediment at the bottom matches what I swabbed out of the P-trap. And check the orange life vest on mudroom peg two—there are photos in my cloud backup—because it's bone-dry and never hit the bay. There's also a fireplace poker down in Zone Four and an oyster knife on the foyer floor."

Ward makes three brisk ticks on his clipboard, already speaking low into his shoulder mic to relay the toxicology and life-vest orders inside. Then his eyes drop back to my open duffel.

Graham stands near an unmarked sedan by the edge of the drive, a uniformed trooper's hand resting lightly on his elbow while another officer takes his preliminary statement. Across the rain-swept gravel, Graham catches my eye. There's a quiet, desperate plea in his posture—the unspoken reminder of the fifty thousand dollars he offered me over a lit gas burner less than an hour ago, and the ten-thousand-dollar completion check I still haven't collected.

My fingers brush the damp canvas of my duffel. Turning over the rest of this file means kissing that completion balance goodbye. It means walking back to Portland tomorrow with my eighteen-thousand-dollar court judgment still hanging over my license, no bond, and no next contract.

I look straight back at Graham, then reach deep into my duffel and pull out the padded manila envelope stamped `OCT 14 — MASTER BACKUP / DO NOT OPEN BEFORE PROBATE`.

I lay it flat on the metal tailgate.

"And this," I tell Ward. "Graham Kiefer's unredacted trust ledger. Two point four million dollars embezzled from Lydia's late father's estate, complete with forged probate initials. He tried to burn it on the kitchen stove tonight so nobody would look past his wife's frame job."

Across the gravel, Graham closes his eyes, turns his head away from the floodlights, and doesn't fight when the trooper reaches for his wrists.

Ward stares down at the thick manila envelope, then up at me. "You worked for him, Ms. Calder. Under an NDA."

"The NDA doesn't cover felony fraud or attempted arson," I say. "Take all of it. Every page."

***

An hour later, the storm has thinned to a cold, steady drizzle. Most of the gravel forecourt has cleared out, leaving only the ambulance bay lit by humming overhead LEDs and the smell of wet pine and diesel exhaust, sharp with antiseptic gauze hanging thick in the air. Down toward the cove, a portable generator still drones beside the boathouse doors while crime-scene techs bag the last of the gear.

I sit on the edge of the ambulance step, letting a paramedic finish knotting three neat stitches across my gashed left wrist and tape clean gauze over the seam while a fresh paper cup of black coffee warms my hands. The wool blanket smells faintly of bleach and cedar smoke. For the first time in four days, nobody is watching me through glass, and nobody is waiting on the other side of a locked door.

Detective Ward walks back over from the yellow crime-scene tape strung across the Kiefer porch, his boots crunching steadily across the wet gravel. His windbreaker drips onto the stone. In his gloved hand, he holds a carbon-copy Maine State Police property receipt and a clear evidence sleeve containing my cracked, waterlogged phone screen—pulled off the boathouse slipway sill by his dive tech.

"We finished the preliminary inventory on the house and Mr. Kiefer's wool overcoat before transport," Ward says, stopping just inside the yellow glow of the ambulance bay. "Coat pockets held his truck keys, a shattered cell phone, and a Portland fiduciary courier receipt—nothing else. Your Civic's trunk has been photographed and cleared so a flatbed can winch you out once the causeway tide drops."

I set my coffee cup down on the metal step and sign the clipboard receipt with stiff, bandaged fingers. "What about the five-thousand-dollar cash retainer in my duffel?"

"Logged as disputed estate funds pending probate review, though your signed margin rider and the *Bramwell and Shaw* default file back up your contract," Ward says, tearing off the pink carbon copy and handing it over. "Keep your phone number current with the Rockland district attorney's clerk once you replace that handset. We'll need you in person for grand jury depositions."

I fold the pink evidence receipt once and slide it into my damp jeans pocket beside my multi-tool. Above the spruce ridge, the storm clouds break open just enough to show a thin edge of cold stars over Penobscot Bay—no completion check in hand, a busted phone in an evidence bag, and Monday's court levy still waiting on Congress Street.
