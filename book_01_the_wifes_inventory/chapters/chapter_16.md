# Chapter 16

The siren cuts out near the ridge bend, then comes back louder off the granite above the house. Five minutes out, tops.

"Take your car." Graham shoves back from the desk. His chair bangs into the walnut shelves as he points toward the front hall. "Use the gravel spur before he makes the drive. If Halstead walks in with that audio queued—"

"I won't make it past the causeway in my Civic." I keep my focus on the phone screen as the USB transfer from the tower hits ninety-six percent and pulls `MIC_04_PANTRY_020915.wav` off the mounted thumb drive onto my internal storage. "There's a bleach-stained boat rag in my spare-tire well, Graham, and Knox County already has a fake confession in my voice. The minute I hit Route 1, Cole puts out a BOLO and drags me out of my seat at gunpoint."

The phone chimes: `TRANSFER COMPLETE`. I pull the USB cable, yank the matte-black thumb drive from the tower, and open my encrypted cloud queue. The router override has killed the house Wi-Fi; one weak bar of LTE flickers in the corner. I bundle the newly copied WAV file with the life-vest tag photos and the Bramwell & Shaw default letter already on my phone, address the packet to Samira Patel in Portland, type four words—*Salt Point setup. Call MSP.*—and hit *Send*.

The wheel spins. Six percent. Twelve. Wind slams the north siding, the lone signal bar drops to *No Service*, and a red banner snaps across the glass: `UPLOAD PAUSED — CARRIER TIMEOUT`. I try plain SMS twice. Nothing leaves the screen.

I drop the phone into my back pocket and shove the thumb drive deep into my jeans watch pocket. The brass pipe wrench stays on the desk blotter by Graham's elbow; hauling dead iron down wet cliff stairs will only slow me down.

Graham catches my sleeve before I hit the doorway, his fingers cold and clammy through the flannel. "Tessa, wait. You don't know what Cole is when nobody's watching. If he has a warrant, he's not coming up here to read us our rights."

"Then buy me two minutes." I twist my arm free. "Throw the deadbolt, put the chain on, and play the confused homeowner. Make him hold the warrant up to the sidelight and talk him through the glass. Ask for his supervisor, ask for your lawyer's number, anything to keep him on the porch."

"What do I tell him about the boots in the closet?"

"Say you found them on the porch steps and brought them in out of the rain. Don't mention my trunk, don't mention New Brunswick. Just keep him at the front door until I get back up the hill."

"Where are you going?"

I'm already moving fast down the north hall. Cutting past the kitchen arch, I see the padded manila envelope from the basement still lying on the island where I left it this morning. Good. Let Cole waste time chasing paper inside. I angle into the sunroom and step across the gray wool rug where Graham dragged it crooked this morning to look at the bleached planks for himself.

"The boathouse," I call back as Graham catches up to the archway. "Cole's cutting off the front drive, and after that spliced confession in my voice, a raw WAV file off an acoustic engineer's server won't clear us on its own. Cole will just claim you synthesized Lydia's voice in your darkroom. Give me three minutes to shoot the physical staging gear inside that sealed boathouse to back up the tape, then I'll climb the back switchback to the spruce ridge where signal holds."

Graham grabs the doorframe and stares at me. "Don't go down there alone, Tessa. If Lydia's iPad just synced on local mesh, whoever is using it is still on this property."

I know. Stay in this room with an unsent file and I'm in handcuffs before I can grab my jacket.

I throw the latch on the sunroom slider and haul the heavy glass back on its track. Freezing salt wind gusts across the floorboards, stinging my face with cold spray.

***

Cold mist closes around me the second I clear the cedar deck. I don't look back to see if Graham pulls the door shut behind me.

My dry spare sneakers slap hard down the steep slate steps cut into the cliff face. Wet pine needles and black lichen make every riser slick. Halfway down, a gust off the bay nearly folds me sideways against the rock; my right palm scrapes raw granite catching myself, lungs burning as cold salt air hits my chest.

Out across the inlet to the south, the low tidal causeway already shines wet under rising gray chop, white foam lapping over the asphalt shoulders. Once this gale peaks with high tide, nobody drives off Salt Point tonight without a heavy four-wheel-drive rig.

I keep one hand skimming the wet granite wall down the lower switchback. Below me, dark harbor water slaps the boathouse pilings.

Under the cliff overhang the wind eases slightly, though surf still booms beneath the decking. Off to my left through drifting fog, Maren Lindqvist's sorting shed stands dark and shuttered above the tide line, her aluminum skiff hauled high onto the gravel apron and chained to an iron ring. No glow in her window, the tin stovepipe cold. The Kiefer boathouse squats ahead of me, its cedar shingles weathered nearly black. A heavy brass padlock secures the double plank doors, and the faded county paper seal has been sliced clean along the edge.

I step off the bottom stair onto crushed oyster shells and duck low under the north eaves. High up on the ridge, the siren yelps and dies. A few seconds later, gravel crunches overhead as tires roll into the turnaround.

Cole is at the house.

I hug the damp shingles along the side wall until I reach the narrow casement window facing the ledges. Monday night, Graham swore the boathouse had stayed locked since the Coast Guard finished searching. When I run my fingers along the weathered sill, though, the gray paint along the bottom seam isn't swollen shut with salt crust. Someone ran a sharp blade down the joint.

I pull my steel multi-tool out, wedge the screwdriver tip under the warped sash, and lever hard. Wet wood groans and gives half an inch. I slip the blade through the gap and lift the loose, rusted interior hook clear of its eye-screw with a light metallic *ting*.

I flatten against the shingles and listen. Rain ticks on the cedar roof; underneath the floorboards, harbor water slaps hollow against pilings. Nothing else moves. I swing the casement open, sit sideways on the narrow sill, and drop both legs over into the gloom.

***

My sneakers hit damp cedar decking with a dull thud. The dark room smells of stale diesel from an old engine tank, cut with cold harbor mud seeping up between the planks. Below the center boards, black water slaps against the pilings and sends damp echoes up through the gaps. Gray storm light bleeds through the salt-clouded transom above the double doors, laying pale bars across stacked lobster traps, coiled dock lines, and a scarred cedar workbench along the far wall.

Suspended low from the rafters on rope slings at shoulder height, a long yellow fiberglass sea kayak sways almost imperceptibly in the cold draft coming through the open window behind me, its hull blocking off the back corner of the room.

I stay crouched beside the bench while my eyes adjust. Up above the cliff edge, a car door thuds shut in the driveway. Cole's cruiser. Three minutes, maybe less, before he stops playing along with Graham and walks around to the back deck.

I ease upright and move along the workbench. Two plastic gallon jugs are tucked beneath a folded blue tarp on the lower shelf, one unlabeled and the other carrying a faded industrial bleach warning. A pair of cedar oars rests beside them, fresh pale scratches cutting through the varnish on the blades—a match for the wet rag squeaking along varnished wood on the 2:14 AM recording.

I pull my phone from my back pocket, tap the camera icon, and check that the flash is off. I crouch low to frame the bleach jugs and scratched oar blades together in one shot.

A boot heel scuffs against grit behind the hanging kayak.

I freeze, the phone still held chest-high, its pale screen lighting up the damp planks at my sneakers.

Lydia Kiefer steps out from behind the yellow fiberglass hull.

Her dark hair is pulled back into a tight knot, and she's wearing a charcoal waxed-canvas jacket over dark trousers, dry and completely composed. In her right hand, she holds a matte-black subcompact Glock 26 steady at waist height, aimed at my chest.

I stay frozen between the open casement window behind me and the gun four paces away.

"Put the phone on the dock, Tessa," Lydia says, her voice calm over the slap of the rising tide. "You weren't supposed to find the dumbwaiter shaft."
