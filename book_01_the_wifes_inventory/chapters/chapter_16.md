# Chapter 16

The siren cuts out near the ridge bend, then comes back louder off the granite above the house. Five minutes out, tops.

"Take your car." Graham shoves back from the desk. His chair bangs into the walnut shelves as he points toward the front hall. "Use the gravel spur before he makes the drive. If Halstead walks in with that audio queued—"

"I won't make it past the causeway in the Civic." My eyes stay locked on the phone screen as the USB transfer from the tower hits ninety-six percent and pulls `MIC_04_PANTRY_020915.wav` onto my internal storage. "There's a bleach-stained rag in my spare-tire well, and Knox County already has a spliced confession in my voice. The minute I hit Route 1, Cole puts out a BOLO and drags me out of my seat."

The phone chimes: `TRANSFER COMPLETE`. I yank the thumb drive from the tower and open my encrypted cloud queue. The router override has killed the house Wi-Fi. Only one weak bar of LTE flickers in the corner. I bundle the WAV file with the life-vest tag photos and the Bramwell & Shaw default letter, address the packet to Samira Patel in Portland, type four words—*Salt Point setup. Call MSP.*—and hit *Send*.

The wheel spins. Six percent. Twelve. Wind slams the north siding, the signal bar drops to *No Service*, and a red banner snaps across the glass: `UPLOAD PAUSED — CARRIER TIMEOUT`. I step toward the side window, holding the phone high against the cold pane like an idiot trying to catch radio waves in a tin can, and try plain SMS twice. Nothing leaves the screen. Outside, the siren pitches sharper as the cruiser clears the ridge bend, closer now, bouncing off the granite wall behind the garage. Waiting here another thirty seconds for a stray bar of service means handing Cole the phone myself.

Graham is standing behind me, rubbing his palms together hard enough to chafe. His hair sticks up on one side where he dragged his hand through it, and for one ridiculous second I want to tell him to fix it before he answers the door. He opens his mouth, closes it, then reaches toward the coat rack by the hall like he's thinking of coming with me, or running out the back, or doing anything except what needs doing.

"Graham," I snap. "Focus."

I shove the thumb drive deep into my jeans watch pocket. The pipe wrench stays on the desk blotter by Graham's elbow; hauling dead iron down wet cliff stairs will only slow me down.

Graham catches my sleeve before I reach the doorway, his fingers clammy through the flannel. "Tessa, wait. You don't know what Cole is when nobody's watching. If he has a warrant, he's not coming up here to read us our rights."

"Then buy me two minutes." I twist my arm free. "Throw the deadbolt, put the chain on, and play the confused homeowner. Make him hold the warrant up to the sidelight and talk him through the glass. Ask for his supervisor, ask for your lawyer's number, anything to keep him on the porch."

"What do I tell him about the boots in the closet?"

"Say you found them on the porch steps and brought them in out of the rain. Don't mention my trunk, don't mention New Brunswick. Just keep him at the front door until I get back up the hill."

"Where are you going?"

I'm already moving down the north hall. Passing the kitchen arch, I see the manila envelope still lying on the island. Good. Let Cole waste time chasing paper inside. I angle into the sunroom and step across the crooked rug.

"The boathouse," I call back as Graham reaches the archway. "After that fake confession, a raw WAV file off an acoustic engineer's server won't clear us on its own. Cole will just claim you synthesized Lydia's voice in your darkroom. I need three minutes to shoot the physical staging gear inside that sealed boathouse to back up the tape, then I'll climb the back switchback to the spruce ridge where signal holds."

Graham grabs the doorframe, his face gray in the storm light. "Don't go down there alone, Tessa. If Lydia's iPad just synced on local mesh, whoever is using it is still on this property."

I know that. My pulse is hammering in my throat, and I'm still wearing a thin flannel shirt with no coat, which was a brilliant choice, really, given the sleet rattling the glass.

I haul the sunroom slider back on its track. Freezing salt wind gusts across the floorboards, stinging my face with spray.

***

Cold mist closes around me the second I clear the cedar deck. I don't look back to see if Graham shuts the door.

My spare sneakers slap down the steep slate steps cut into the cliff face. Wet needles and lichen make every riser slick. Halfway down, a gust off the bay nearly folds me sideways against the rock. My right palm scrapes raw granite catching myself, and cold air burns my lungs as I drag in a breath. Two steps lower, my left heel shoots out on a mat of wet needles, slamming my hip into the stone wall hard enough to knock the wind out of me. I hang there for a second, blinking sleet out of my lashes, before forcing my legs to move again.

Out across the inlet to the south, the low tidal causeway shines wet under rising gray chop, white foam lapping over the asphalt shoulders. Once this gale peaks with high tide, nobody drives off Salt Point tonight without a heavy four-wheel-drive rig. Which means even if I get the photos, even if Samira gets the file, I'm stuck on this peninsula until the water drops or the state police roll in.

I keep one hand against the wet granite down the lower switchback. Below me, dark harbor water slaps the boathouse pilings.

Under the cliff overhang the wind eases slightly, though surf still booms beneath the decking. Off to my left through drifting fog, Maren Lindqvist's sorting shed stands shuttered above the tide line, her aluminum skiff hauled high onto the gravel apron and chained to an iron ring. Her window is dark. The tin stovepipe is cold, and if she's even on the point tonight, she's tucked inside with the storm shutters bolted, well out of earshot of anything happening down by the water.

Ahead of me, the Kiefer boathouse squats on its pilings, its cedar shingles weathered nearly black. A brass padlock secures the double plank doors, and the faded county paper seal has been sliced clean along the edge.

I step off the bottom stair onto oyster shells and duck under the north eaves. High up on the ridge, the siren yelps and dies. A few seconds later, gravel crunches overhead as tires roll into the turnaround.

Cole is at the house. I picture Graham fumbling with the brass chain, his voice pitching too high, giving everything away in the first ten seconds. Stop thinking about him. Just move.

I hug the damp shingles along the side wall until I reach the narrow casement window facing the ledges. Graham swore the boathouse had stayed locked since the Coast Guard finished searching. When I run my fingers along the weathered sill, though, the gray paint along the bottom seam isn't swollen shut with salt crust. Someone ran a sharp blade down the joint.

I wedge my multi-tool under the warped sash and lever hard. The metal slips once on the slick wood, barking my knuckles against the rough shingles, before I reset the tip and throw my weight into it. Wet wood groans and gives half an inch. Cold rain drips off my hair onto my wrist as I slip the blade through the gap and lift the interior hook clear of its eye-screw with a light metallic *ting*.

I flatten against the shingles and listen. Rain ticks on the cedar roof; underneath the floorboards, harbor water slaps hollow against pilings. Nothing else moves. I swing the casement wide and drop inside.

***

My sneakers hit damp cedar decking with a dull thud. The dark room smells of stale diesel from an old engine tank. Below the center boards, black water slaps against the pilings and sends damp echoes up through the gaps. Gray storm light bleeds through the salt-clouded transom above the double doors, laying pale bars across stacked lobster traps, coiled dock lines, and a cedar workbench along the far wall.

Suspended low from the rafters on rope slings at shoulder height, a yellow fiberglass sea kayak sways almost imperceptibly in the draft from the open window behind me, its hull blocking off the back corner of the room.

I stay crouched beside the bench while my eyes adjust, waiting for the shapes in the gloom to separate from the shadows. My wet jeans are plastered to my calves, and my teeth want to chatter if I let my jaw go slack. Up above the cliff edge, a car door thuds shut in the driveway. Cole's cruiser. Three minutes, maybe less, before he stops playing along with Graham and walks around to the back deck.

I ease upright and move along the workbench, my fingers brushing past a coffee can of rusted nails and a tangle of braided nylon line. Two plastic gallon jugs are tucked beneath a folded blue tarp on the lower shelf, one unlabeled and the other carrying a faded industrial bleach warning. A pair of cedar oars rests beside them, fresh pale scratches cutting through the varnish on the blades—a match for the wet rag squeaking along varnished wood on the 2:14 AM recording.

I pull my phone, check that the flash is off, and crouch low to frame the bleach jugs and scratched oar blades together in one shot. My hands are shaking from the cold, blurring the first preview on the glass, so I brace my elbows against my knees and tap the shutter again. The screen throws a pale rectangle across the damp planks at my feet.

A boot heel scuffs against grit behind the hanging kayak.

I freeze, the phone still held chest-high.

Lydia Kiefer steps out from behind the yellow hull.

My brain stalls for half a beat, trying to square the woman standing in front of me with the glossy photo from Graham's spring profile. Her dark hair is pulled back into a tight knot, and she's wearing a charcoal waxed-canvas jacket over dark trousers, dry and completely composed. In her right hand, she holds a subcompact Glock 26 steady at waist height, aimed at my chest.

I stay frozen between the open casement window behind me and the gun four paces away.

"Put the phone on the dock, Tessa," Lydia says, her voice calm over the slap of the rising tide. "You weren't supposed to find the dumbwaiter shaft."
