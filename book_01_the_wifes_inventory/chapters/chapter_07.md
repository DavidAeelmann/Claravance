# Chapter 7

At seven-fifteen Wednesday morning, Graham leaves for Portland in a charcoal suit with his leather briefcase locked in the Audi's trunk.

I stand in the main hall in my wool socks and watch him pause inside the front vestibule to tap a temporary guest invite onto his phone. A second later, mine chimes with a Kiefer Soundworks home-access link, valid until five, with gate and delivery-door feeds while he's out.

"Skip the kitchenware today," he says, and turns his wool coat collar up against the drizzle spitting off the cove. "Stick to the master closet and Lydia's vanity. I need all of it shot and logged before tomorrow night."

"I'll stay on schedule." I lean a shoulder against the archway. "Boston confirmed Thursday?"

"They're sending a courier tomorrow evening once I upload the preliminary ledger at five." He looks up at me under the vestibule light, eyes tired and hard. "Text me before you leave the property. Wind's supposed to peak around noon, and I don't need you stuck on the causeway if high water tops the culvert."

"Not going anywhere."

The second his taillights clear the timber gate and the barrier clunks shut, the house settles into its daytime noise: crawlspace dehumidifier rumbling underfoot, wind ticking spruce needles against the glass. Half the day with the place to myself.

The relief doesn't even last until the kettle would've boiled. My wool socks are still damp around the toes from the mudroom, clammy every time I shift my weight, and my shoulders ache before I've carried a single box. I could walk out right now, except I can't, and standing in the hall staring at the stairs isn't going to move the ledger any faster. I'm already behind before eight in the morning.

Before I touch a sweater upstairs, I walk over to the floating oak stairs and crack the cedar stairwell door an inch. Down the concrete steps, the steel fire door of Basement Zone 4 sits under a steady red LED, hardwired into the Kiefer Soundworks hub. Even with the B-4 key sitting inside my jacket on the vestibule hook, shoving old brass into that cylinder right now sends a breach alert straight to Portland and Graham's phone.

I crouch by the gap and listen. Dead quiet behind the steel. Yet during last night's blackout, the pantry tablet flashed **PERIMETER BYPASS ACTIVE** before Graham swiped it clear. Someone found a way into that room without touching the deadbolt.

I shut the door, grab my clipboard and two collapsed wardrobe cartons from the hall pile, and head upstairs to the master suite.

The bedroom takes up the whole south wing over the sunroom: pale oak floors, a low linen platform bed stripped to bare cotton sheets, double glass sliders overlooking the gray cove chop below. Cedar from the closet mixes with cold damp off the cove, the exact scent Graham carried on his sweater Monday night. A framed botanical print hangs above the headboard, its glass catching the overcast sky.

Both walnut nightstands are bare except for matching brass lamps and a coiled white phone charger on the left. That's Graham's side, to judge by the hardcover acoustics textbook stacked next to the outlet. On the right side, somebody wiped the wood so clean even the brass lamp base has no dust ring around it.

I drop the cartons outside the walk-in closet, snap on a pair of nitrile gloves, and get to work.

Two walls of structured wool coats, silk blouses, and ankle boots hang spaced neatly on wooden hangers, while half a dozen round hatboxes and three velvet jewelry trays line the back shelf. Most of the tags are still stiff, the cuffs uncreased, like Lydia bought half of it for a life she never quite got around to living. I spend the next couple of hours working at a steady clip so my cloud timestamps look right. I snap photos and log anything over five hundred: a camel cashmere trench, two pairs of Italian riding boots, a Cartier watch with a dead battery in the top tray. I even pull down two of the hatboxes—light, packed only with silk scarves and cedar sachets—and set them back into their dust rings.

Every drawer in the center island glides out on oiled tracks. Folded scarves, leather driving gloves, a silver-backed hairbrush with three dark strands still caught in the bristles. In the top tray, a platinum wedding band rests beside a pair of pearl studs, the kind of ring a woman rarely takes off by accident. Her everyday things are gone, packed into the weekender downstairs. Only the expensive pieces probate lawyers expect to see are left behind.

Then I look down at the bottom shoe rack. All of Lydia's boots (tall suede, riding leather, rubber rain boots) stand heel-to-toe along the cedar slats. At the far right end beside the baseboard, a clean rectangular patch of wood lies empty, the exact footprint of winter duck boots. The missing half of the mudroom tray downstairs. I crouch, snap a quick shot of the empty space, and head into the master bath.

***

The bathroom is all honed slate and frosted glass, cold enough to raise the hair on my arms. A double limestone vanity runs under a wide mirror, spotless except for a dried water ring near the left faucet. I catch my own reflection in the glass—pale, hair coming loose behind one ear—and look away. A hammered-silver soap dish is bone-dry beside two linen hand towels folded into exact thirds, like nobody has washed their hands in here in weeks.

I check the tall linen cabinet first, patting behind stacks of gray bath sheets. Under Lydia's sink, the narrow drawers hold only spare razor cartridges, cotton pads, and a travel toothbrush still sealed in blister plastic. Last, I pull open the shallow mirrored medicine cabinet over the left sink.

Two glass shelves of ordinary clutter: moisturizers, prescription retinol, half a tube of hydrocortisone, and, tucked behind an ibuprofen bottle, an orange pharmacy vial prescribed to Lydia Kiefer, filled at a pharmacy in Camden on October 4. Ten days before the storm.

*Zolpidem Tartrate. 10 mg. Qty: 30.*

Ambien.

I turn the vial toward the frosted window. Written by an internist in Rockland, zero refills. Heavy sleep meds, filled a week and a half before she walked down to a skiff in the dark.

I pop the cap and tip the tablets into my gloved palm, then count twice.

Twenty white oblong pills.

I dump them back in, log the October fourth fill date and count, and put the vial back behind the ibuprofen. Then I glance down into the left sink basin.

The porcelain bowl looks spotless. Up close, though, a faint white ring catches the recessed light along the stopper's chrome edge: chalky, slightly gritty, like crushed powder that didn't rinse all the way down.

I kneel under the vanity with my multi-tool, my left knee popping loud in the quiet room. The curved brass P-trap has been wiped clean on the outside, though if somebody flushed crushed solids down here recently, the bottom bend would still hold trace.

Holding a folded paper towel underneath the elbow, I crack the slip-nut loose. A thimbleful of gray water drips onto the paper, followed by a damp smear of white crystalline paste caked inside the lower curve of the pipe.

I stare at the chalky smear on my glove. Without a lab, it could be scouring powder or mineral buildup—except nobody scrubs a spotless vanity drain and leaves wet paste packed in the bend. If you dissolve crushed tablets in warm tap water to draw off the clear drug and dump the insoluble white binder sludge down the sink, the P-trap catches the paste. The timing makes those ten missing tablets hard to ignore.

I scrape a clean dab of the paste onto a square of foil from my pocket, twist it inside the finger of a spare glove, and knot it shut.

My phone buzzes against my hip, and I crack my elbow on the drainpipe.

***

I stay frozen under the sink while it gives one short chime, a push alert from the Kiefer Soundworks app. I back out from under the vanity and check the screen. A gray banner covers the lock:

**BACK DECK / PANTRY PERIMETER — MOTION DETECTED**

Graham is in Portland. I'm supposed to be alone on this peninsula for hours yet. I tap the banner and pull up the live guest feed.

The grainy wide-angle lens looks across the rain-slicked back deck at the glass pantry entry door tucked beside the kitchen wall. Rain streaks the corner of the lens and blurs the cove fog behind the rail, yet the man standing squarely in front of the pantry keypad is unmistakable.

Deputy Cole Halstead.

He's wearing a dark navy patrol windbreaker instead of his county slicker, collar flipped up against the drizzle. No cruiser headlights show in the turnaround. He must have left the rig down the gravel lane or hiked up from the cove trail to stay clear of the driveway gate where Graham's app would log a vehicle.

I watch him check his wristwatch, glance over his shoulder at the empty deck stairs, and lift his left hand to the lit keypad. He doesn't even pause. He punches six digits into the buttons. A green LED flashes on the housing, and the door swings inward smoothly when he leans into the handle.

A rotating code Graham swore changed every forty-eight hours. A deputy he claimed never stepped inside. Now Halstead is walking straight into the kitchen while Graham's ninety miles south, and he doesn't even knock.

Halstead steps inside and pulls the glass door shut behind him. The video feed stutters, then drops to a gray **FEED INTERRUPTED** box.

I snug the slip-nut back up, swipe the slate dry with the paper towel, and strip the gloves off on my way into the upper hall. If I stay up here, I'm trapped at the top of a single staircase—or worse, he walks back out before I find out what he came for.

Every instinct tells me to grab my duffel, run for the Civic, and let Boston deal with this rotten house. Except walking out before Thursday triggers a breach clause I can't cover.

I take the narrow back service stairs in my socks, hugging the plaster wall where the treads stay quiet, and stop dead two steps above the bottom landing as the steel door of the kitchen breaker box clicks open right around the corner.
