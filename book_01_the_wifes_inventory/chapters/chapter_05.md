# Chapter 5

My Civic's heater takes half the causeway just to push lukewarm air against the fogged windshield. Behind me, the timber gate grinds shut and the spruces swallow the glass house, though my shoulders stay tight all the way to the mainland like Graham's palm is still pressed to that upstairs pane. Every frost heave along the narrow neck rattles the loose muffler shield under the floorboards and knocks the tissue-wrapped brass **B-4** key against my ribs.

At the mainland fork, I wait with my blinker ticking over wet asphalt. Graham told me to take the main harbor route because a county crew was patching a culvert washout past the lobster pound. I take the harbor road anyway. Past the Salt Point firehouse, the spruce canopy thins out over the salt marsh and my phone finally jumps from one bar to four.

Notifications dump onto the screen before I clear the curve: two missed calls from my landlord, one automated reminder from the Cumberland County clerk. At a four-way stop, I brace my wrist against the wheel and type *Kiefer Soundworks Arlington* with my thumb. Three hits load before the pickup behind me taps its horn: a spring defense-tech press release about a pending patent buyout, and a short regional item noting Kiefer Soundworks missed its third-quarter lab lease extension in Cambridge.

Then red and blue strobes fill my rearview mirror and cut bright across the fog. I haven't even touched forty. I signal right, ease onto the soft gravel shoulder beside a tangle of bare sumac, and kill the engine. A Knox County sheriff's cruiser rolls in close behind my bumper, its light bar pulsing across the wet alder branches in the mist. I pull my license and creased registration out of the glovebox and rest both hands on top of the steering wheel while heavy boots crunch up the gravel.

The deputy who steps up to my window is broad-shouldered under a navy rain slicker and clean-shaven. Before he even bends down to the glass, he takes a slow inventory of my rusted front fender and the Cumberland County sticker on my Portland plates. His brass badge catches the dash light, and above it is the exact same unbothered stare I watched across a Vermont counter six years ago right before they closed Nina's binder. When this one talks, a slow coastal drawl softens his edges, and a slightly crooked upper incisor shows in the dim cab light.

"Afternoon, ma'am. License and registration."

I pass the cards through the gap while cold drizzle spits across my knuckles. "Was I speeding, Deputy? Thought it was forty through here."

"Right brake lamp's out," he says. He takes his time reading the address on my license. "And that muffler shield's hanging low enough to throw sparks on dry blacktop. Tessa Calder. Long way up from Portland."

"Short contract. Packing an estate out on Salt Point."

Deputy Cole Halstead doesn't blink. He hands my license back, hooks a thick forearm along the open door frame, and leans in close; his slicker carries the damp tang of oilskin and the wintergreen dip tucked behind his lip.

"Kiefer's glass box," he says, his voice dry and unimpressed. "I wrote the marine report out there three weeks ago. Lydia grew up climbing those ledges before Boston money ever bolted steel and glass onto them. People on this point don't care much for mainland contractors poking through a local girl's things just to pad a probate bill."

I keep both hands loose on the top of the wheel. Twenty-four hours on the peninsula, and the local sheriff's office already knows where I'm sleeping. "I'm boxing winter coats and logging furniture," I say. "Not poking into anything."

"Good. Keep it that way." He pulls a thin yellow warning pad from his slicker pocket, clicks a black ballpoint against his thigh, and flips the metal clipboard onto his forearm. "We get fog tourists every fall wandering onto private launches like a drowning's weekend entertainment. Those cove steps will snap an ankle on wet granite, and the boathouse is locked under county seal. Stay away from it."

*County seal.* Against my ribs, the tissue-wrapped B-4 key suddenly feels five pounds heavier. If he asks me to step out into the drizzle and pop the trunk, this ends right here on the shoulder of Route 1. He begins writing my plate number and the date across the yellow slip, and I watch his hands. He braces the clipboard hard against his right hip, curls his left wrist high over the top of the paper, and drags the pen backward against the grain in a hard, hooked push.

"Get that bulb swapped before Friday," he says, tearing off the yellow sheet and handing it through the window. "Hate to impound a mainland car on the causeway over a busted light."

I take the slip by the corner without touching his fingers. "Thanks, Deputy. I'll pick one up at the general store."

"See that you do." He taps my roof twice with his palm, walks back to his cruiser, and swings a U-turn across the wet double yellow line toward the Salt Point causeway and the peninsula I just left.

***

My hands shake so hard I grind first gear twice before pulling back onto the asphalt. Nobody pulls a car over for a brake lamp in a coastal fog bank unless they were already parked in the spruce bend waiting for it. And now he's rolling back up Kiefer's private lane while everything I brought is shut behind a cheap turn-bolt lock in the guest room.

I drive into Salt Point harbor checking my mirror every ten seconds. The town is barely a crossroads: a brick post office with salt-crusted steps, a shuttered bait shop, and a weathered clapboard general store beside a diesel dock where three lobster boats rock against black pilings.

I park nose-out by the wooden steps. Before cutting the engine, I pull up the public Coast Guard missing-person bulletin for Lydia Kiefer, pair the vessel ID with the passport number listed on the broker's preliminary estate tax sheet, and send an encrypted text to Samira Patel—a marine-claims investigator in Portland who still has read-only access to the Atlantic Marine Underwriters' cross-border port-clearance watch—asking her to flag any border hit while I still have four bars. Then I lock the doors and head inside under a jangling brass bell.

Fluorescents buzz overhead along low pressed-tin panels as I grab two rolls of heavy packing tape, a twin pack of permanent markers, and a blister pack of replacement brake bulbs near the register. Behind the counter, a skinny kid in a faded Patriots hoodie drags my items across the scanner one-handed while his thumb scrolls a cracked phone screen. Beside him, a salt-warped corkboard is layered thick with laminated tide tables, church supper notices, and faded lost-dog flyers.

The register chimes. Behind me, the door bell jangles again.

A draft of harbor fog follows the new customer inside and carries the smell of wet rubber and dock diesel across the scuffed linoleum. I turn by a wire rack of work gloves and run straight into Maren Lindqvist. Up close without fifty yards of fog between us, she looks harder: deep weather lines carved around pale blue eyes, silver braid tucked under a salt-stained wool cap, rubber boots caked in dried mudflat silt. She carries a dented tin of roll-up tobacco in one cracked hand.

"Rough water coming Friday," she says. She pitches her voice so the kid at the register can hear and walks past me to the coffee urns like we're making dock small talk. "Spring tide's going to top the causeway culvert if the wind swings east."

I keep my voice low. "I'll watch the road."

She fills a paper cup with black coffee, snaps the plastic lid down with her thumb, and squares her shoulder between us and the convex security mirror bolted over the counter. "Heard Kiefer brought in help from Portland. Smart girl would cash whatever check she's holding and head back down Route One before dark."

"You held up two fingers on the mudflat this morning," I murmur, pretending to check the seams on a pair of leather work gloves. "Who else was down at that boathouse?"

Maren checks the kid at the register to make sure he's still glued to his phone, then looks down at her coffee lid. "Not in here. My aquaculture lease comes up for county sign-off in January, and Cole Halstead's marine unit boards my brother-in-law's boat every time somebody talks out of turn. I owe too much on winter dockage to go rounds with a badge over summer money."

"Then why wave me off from the cove?"

Maren pulls a wad of brown paper napkins from the counter dispenser. Her cold, salt-cracked knuckles brush the back of my hand, and she jerks back like she touched a hot stove. "Because I don't like watching an outsider walk blind onto slick rock," she mutters. "That's all you get. Don't take Kiefer's word on when the gale hit, and don't turn your back on Cole Halstead when the tide comes up."

The clerk clears his throat behind me and drops my receipt into a brown paper bag on the counter beside the tape rolls. "Eighteen forty-two, miss."

Maren doesn't wait. She steps right past my open paper bag to drop a crumpled five beside the coffee urn, her free hand grazing the top edge of the sack for a split second before she shoulders out the glass door. I hand over a twenty, wait for my change, grab my bag, and hurry out onto the plank porch just in time to see her rusted pickup rattle away up the harbor hill.

***

Back in the Civic, I push both door locks down and blow a long breath into the cold cab until the windshield clouds over. I drop the brown paper bag onto the passenger seat next to the courthouse letter and reach for the ignition. A corner of stiff, weatherproof white card stock catches my eye inside the open sack, wedged deep between the tape rolls and the blister pack of brake bulbs. The kid at the register only threw my crumpled receipt on top.

I pull the card out and go still. It's a torn strip of plasticized marine tide-chart card—the same heavy coated dock stock pinned by the coffee urns where Maren stood. Someone tore off the October column, circled *Oct 15* at the top, and boxed a single line in thick carpenter's pencil:

*2:14 AM — High Water.*

Underneath the tide mark, cramped block letters press hard into the coated paper:

*Ask him why his dock lights were off.*

I fold the strip twice and slip it into my jeans watch pocket, separate from the B-4 key. Outside, mist closes over the lobster boats rocking at the pier. Past the harbor bend, the causeway leads back behind Graham Kiefer's gate, where a left-handed deputy drove fifteen minutes ahead of me.
