# Chapter 23

Four weeks after Salt Point, the cast-iron radiator in my Portland studio still knocks against the wall, and cold harbor air leaks around the single-pane glass.

I stand at the scarred kitchen counter with a mug of black coffee and look down at two sheets of heavy bond paper under the bare overhead bulb. The first is a stamped satisfaction-of-judgment notice from the Cumberland County clerk: $18,412 paid in full, zero balance left on the $42,000 defamation suit that wrecked my career three years ago. The second is an embossed letter from the Maine Department of Public Safety restoring my private investigator bond, effective today, Monday morning.

Once my State Police property receipt stayed Monday's court levy and probate review cleared a week after the storm, the county released my $5,000 cash retainer. Two days later, a certified courier delivered a $13,500 cashier's check out of Graham's separate contractual archiving escrow, untouched by the estate freeze. Together they wiped out the judgment with eighty-eight dollars left over. The four hundred in my checking account covered the state bond reinstatement fee.

My left wrist still shows a pale pink seam under the cuff of my wool sweater where the stitches came out, and my bruised knee aches in the December cold. Two weeks of interviews and grand jury testimony in Rockland took care of the rest. Detective Sergeant Ward and the Knox County DA didn't have much left to argue once their techs pulled the boathouse audio off the Zone Four RAID server. Latent prints lifted from the untouched silver Montblanc matched Cole Halstead. That backed up his installer-PIN logs, and a handwriting match linked his left-handed warning slip to the smudged departure note. MSP toxicology came back positive for zolpidem in both my P-trap swab and Graham's living-room rocks glass, and Coast Guard records confirmed the clean mudroom life vest was an unlogged spare that had never seen Penobscot Bay. Lydia and Cole are sitting in separate cells without bail, and Graham's bail hearing got pushed back after the state auditor finished tallying his $2.4 million trust shortfall.

I skipped the press. I gave my statement, signed the chain-of-custody logs, and drove back down Route 1 before Boston reporters ever found the causeway. Even Maren Lindqvist came out clean. The state granted her full immunity as the reporting witness, and three days ago a wooden crate of Salt Point oysters turned up on my landing with a harbor receipt tucked under the twine: *Dock's paid. Keep your head down.*

The studio is already half-empty. My landlord's eviction notice is dead, though I'm still getting out of this drafty shoebox before the lease rolls. Two taped boxes are stacked beside the door. My faded navy duffel rests zipped on the bare mattress, no longer packed for a midnight run, and my 2014 silver Honda Civic waits at the curb downstairs with its spare-tire well scrubbed clean by state techs and its muffler shield finally bolted down. For the first time in three years, I don't owe a dollar to a lawyer or a court clerk.

I fold the restored bond letter along its crease and slide it into my jacket pocket. At ten o'clock, I have an intake interview with a regional marine-insurance adjuster on Congress Street. Standard desk audits: flooded hulls, dock claims, documented chain-of-custody. Daylight work with real witnesses and paper trails that don't end inside a soundproofed cellar.

I kill the kitchen bulb and grab my duffel. I'm not leaving my laptop in a twelve-year-old Civic parked off the waterfront. Then I lock the apartment door behind me.

***

Ten minutes later, I push through the heavy glass door of Becky's Diner on Commercial Street. Heat from the flat-top hits me first, along with fried bacon and the scorched pot of coffee on the back burner. Out past the fogged front glass, lobster boats idle along the working pier while gulls turn through gray morning mist. I drop my duffel onto the bench of a corner vinyl booth, shrug off my damp coat, and slide in facing the street door.

The waitress tops off a chipped mug before I ask. I wrap my hands around the warm ceramic and pull out the printed marine-insurance application I brought to look over before ten.

The brass bell over the street door jingles. A woman in a tailored charcoal wool coat ignores the host stand and heads for my booth. Her silver-streaked dark hair is pulled into a low knot, a slim oxblood leather briefcase tucked under her arm, not a speck of road salt on her boots. She doesn't ask if the seat is taken. She unbuttons her coat, slides onto the red vinyl across from me, and sets her briefcase on the Formica between the salt shaker and the ketchup bottle.

"Ms. Calder," she says, her voice low and clipped. "You're harder to pin down than a probate subpoena. Your number's been going straight to voicemail since November fifth."

"Dropped the phone in a boathouse," I say. I keep both hands on my mug. "Who are you?"

"Margot Kiefer-Hall." She pulls a heavy cream business card from her coat pocket and sets it beside my coffee. No title, just a State Street address in Boston and a direct private line. "Managing partner at Kiefer-Hall Fiduciary. We issued the thirteen-thousand-five-hundred-dollar cashier's check our courier brought to your apartment last month."

I read the card and look up at her.

"Kiefer-Hall?"

"Distant branch," she says. "My grandfather founded the fiduciary arm in Boston; Graham's side took the acoustics patents and the coastal land. We hadn't spoken in a decade until he retained us six weeks ago, strictly as an independent archiving escrow agent, separate from Bramwell and Shaw."

"Graham told me he was paying out of his own pocket."

"He did." Margot rests her gloved hands on top of her briefcase. "Through a separate contractual escrow he funded ahead of the Arlington closing. When he drove down to Portland that Wednesday to collect the pre-closing packet—after moving your deadline up twenty-four hours into a coastal gale—I saw your name on the contractor voucher and added the thirty-five-hundred-dollar storm adjustment myself. Once his arrest voided the closing, the escrow terms still required release to whichever contractor delivered an unredacted accounting of the estate."

She pauses while the waitress walks past with a fresh carafe and waits until the booth behind us empties out.

"You handed over two-point-four million dollars' worth of forged trust records when you could have taken fifty thousand over a gas burner and walked away," Margot says in a low voice. "Most people sitting under a Monday bank levy take the cash."

"Most people don't like getting locked in a basement," I say. "If you're here for a receipt, the clerk's office already cleared the check."

"I'm not here for a receipt, Tessa." She unsnaps the brass latches on her oxblood briefcase. "My firm represents families whose problems can't survive a public probate docket. Quiet archiving. Discreet asset verification. Your investigator bond cleared this morning, and your court balance is zero."

I keep my face still. She knows the day my bond reinstated and she knows my county docket balance. That kind of reach doesn't stop at a Boston escrow file.

"I have an interview on Congress Street at ten," I say. I nudge my marine-insurance application toward the edge of the table. "I'm done working off-book inside other people's houses."

Margot doesn't push. She just folds her hands back over the leather and holds my gaze.

"Triple the Salt Point rate," she says. "Forty-five thousand dollars. Ten days in northern Vermont, starting tonight if you can clear the notch before dark. A private mountain lodge sealed for the winter after a sudden family disappearance—except the caretaker walked off the job Tuesday without returning the keys. Inventory the estate, flag anything off before probate counsel arrives, and stay clear of the local sheriff."

I take a slow swallow of bitter coffee and set the mug squarely back on the paper placemat. My fingers brush the folded bond letter inside my jacket pocket.

"Pass," I say. "Find somebody else."

***

Out past the fogged window, a tugboat horn groans across Portland Harbor, and the vibration hums up through the tabletop.

Margot studies me for a beat. Then she reaches into her open briefcase and sets two things on the Formica. First, a heavy brass skeleton key, its bow worn smooth from decades of hands, with dark pine resin clinging inside the cut of the teeth and a blank manila tag on the ring by a twist of copper wire. Next to it, she slides a glossy color photograph across the table until its white edge stops against my coffee mug.

I look down despite myself. A sprawling timber-and-stone lodge is buried halfway to its cedar eaves in fresh Vermont snow, dark spruces crowding the drive behind a wrought-iron gate. Thin gray smoke curls out of a massive stone chimney against a gray-white sky.

"I said no, Ms. Kiefer-Hall." I rest my palm on the marine-insurance packet and reach for my coat on the hook. "Take your key back to Boston."

"The lodge has been legally empty since October," Margot says. She taps one gloved fingertip against the white border of the print. "It's clear you like a little mystery, that you don't walk past a loose thread once you see it. Turn it over."

Everything I dragged myself out of at Salt Point tells me to grab my duffel, walk out into the cold harbor air, and keep walking. I didn't crawl out of a frozen cove just to step into another rich family's locked house in the snow.

Still, Margot hasn't moved her hand.

Slowly, I lift the corner of the photograph and flip it over.

A black-and-white fax printout of a lined paper guest ledger is taped square to the matte backing. It's just one corner of the page, cropped close around a date stamp and a signature line.

The date reads two days ago.

In the margin beside a room number, three words are written in the cramped, back-slanted cursive I haven't seen on paper since the night my younger sister vanished six years ago. Nina's handwriting:

*Don't trust him.*

My thumb goes still against the glossy border. Across the Formica, Margot watches me with her gloved hands folded—and I don't know which is worse: that she has no idea whose handwriting she just put in front of me, or that she knew before she ever walked through the door.
