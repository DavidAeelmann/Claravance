# Chapter 15

By three-thirty, rain is hammering the front sidelights and Graham's phone still hasn't pinged with an ETA from the Boston courier.

I come down the stairs with the pipe wrench and look out through the front entry glass. My Civic is parked by the spruces, its trunk lid still cracked just above the latch in the mist.

"We can't leave those boots behind the bench," Graham says behind me, his voice hoarse. He stares at the front door. "Cole Halstead rolls up that drive with a warrant, he won't care who popped your trunk."

"Cole doesn't have a warrant yet," I say. "If he did, he wouldn't wait till three-thirty to kick the door in. He's still stacking paper."

Two sleepless nights are catching up with me fast, a dull ache behind my eyes that won't quit. Even so, I'm not walking out with ten grand unpaid and a trunk full of someone else's staging.

"Grab a towel and wipe the slate behind the bench," I tell him. "Your upstairs inventory's uploaded, and your hardware lockout just cleared. Now take me into your study: the server rack, the admin key, whatever you've got behind that partition."

He hesitates, glancing toward the north hall. "The study rack only mirrors the house array. The raw acoustic buffers are encrypted on the drive in your pocket."

"You wrote the encryption," I say, rapping the wrench against my thigh. "Which means the passphrase is in your head. Move."

He doesn't argue. He grabs a dish towel off the kitchen counter to swab the slate and shove the boots into the coat closet. While he's busy, I duck into the guest room for my dry sneakers. I didn't pack spare socks, so my damp wool ones squelch the second I lace up. Great. I check my duffel.

The cash is still under my shirts, but the side-pouch zipper is flipped outward. I yank it open. My spare Civic key fob is gone. Somebody lifted it while my back was turned, which explains how my trunk popped without a mark on the paint. I cross the north hall.

Graham's study is right opposite my room. Unlike the glass rooms out front, it's narrow and closed in, lined with dark acoustic baffling and walnut bookshelves. A steel server rack hums in the corner behind a smoked-glass door, blue and amber LEDs glowing inside.

"Sit," I say.

Graham drops into the desk chair and wakes the dual displays. White light catches the dark circles under his eyes and the rumpled collar of his charcoal sweater.

I set the thumb drive beside his keyboard. "Mount the raw partition. The full unedited audio from October fifteenth, two-fourteen."

He plugs the drive into the tower under the desk. A terminal prompt pops up on the left screen asking for a thirty-two-character passphrase. His fingers stop above the keys. "Unlocking the master partition opens the whole audit log," he says, eyes fixed on the screen. "Every voice memo I recorded after the trust money was gone. Every transfer receipt."

"Your *Bramwell & Shaw* default notice is already on a server in Portland," I say. I lean a hip against the desk edge where I can see both his hands and the hall door. "You're past saving your reputation, Graham. Right now you're trying to stay out of Thomaston."

He exhales sharply through his nose and starts typing.

***

A green progress bar crawls across the left display while rain beats against the north window and the tower fans kick higher. On the right screen, a local folder labeled `ARLINGTON_STAFFING_OCT21` sits open behind the terminal, and an unsealed manila folder tabbed `CALDER_T — VETTING` rests beside the keyboard. Graham's hands stay in plain sight. I flip the cover back. Inside is a two-page Boston background report dated October twenty-first, listing my Commercial Street fire-manifest termination and my $18,412 Cumberland County judgment. Graham's own typed screening note is stapled to the corner:

*Refused dirty sign-off 2023. $18,412 levy pending Nov. Cornered enough to take cash, too stubborn to walk past a six-minute Zone 4 dropout or a dry life vest. Put under $50k IP NDA (leave Clause 3 photo ban intact to test if she demands chain-of-custody rider). Watch what Oct 15 tampering she flags first.*

I slide the memo onto his mouse hand and tap it with the wrench. "October twenty-first," I say. "You pulled my eviction ledger the exact day you spotted the Zone Four dropout and the dry life vest. You didn't hire me because you trusted me, Graham. You bought a broke investigator as a cheap tripwire, and left me walking blind while Cole Halstead ran my plates. *Too stubborn to walk past.* Jesus."

He doesn't pull his hand back. His fingers curl slightly against the paper. "I needed the Arlington inventory finished before November fourteenth, and I needed eyes nobody in Knox County owned. If I'd briefed a stranger on day one and you went straight to a deputy, I'd be in cuffs before I figured out who was dirty."

"So you let me climb into that darkroom alone."

"I didn't know Halstead had an installer PIN until this morning," he says, eyes on the progress bar as it passes eighty percent. "I knew he couldn't stand me. He and Lydia grew up running these ledges together long before I bought the headland. Even after her father died, Cole acted like I was some Boston tourist who stole his backyard. Hating me doesn't explain faking a drowning."

"Being broke does," I say. "Or whatever offshore money Lydia put in front of him."

His jaw sets as he glances at the screen. "Her father set up offshore trust accounts before his stroke, money I never touched. When I pulled the two-point-four million for Kiefer Soundworks, I intended to put it back before the November audit. I never figured she'd vanish instead of filing for divorce."

Ninety-eight percent. Ninety-nine. The tower chimes softly, and a directory tree opens on the right display: `RAW_BUFFER_OCT14_15`. Twelve timestamped WAV files appear, multi-channel mixdowns pulled straight off the house array, untouched by the automated scrubbers.

I point to the fifth line down: `MIC_04_PANTRY_020915.wav`, the synced five-channel master anchored on the indoor pantry corridor feed.

"Play that one. All the way up."

Graham double-clicks it. A dense, jagged waveform fills the screen as he twists the volume knob on the audio interface beside his elbow. Static fills the study: rain hammering coastal glass, cedar rafters groaning in the wind. Then a sharp metallic *click* cuts through, followed by the dull thud of a heavy latch sliding home.

"That's the pantry corridor," I murmur. "The dumbwaiter hatch."

Rubber soles squeak across wet slate, followed by a muffled grunt as someone drags heavy canvas past the sunroom arch and bumps the paneling.

*"Careful with the varnish,"* a woman's voice cuts through the rain hiss.

Graham goes rigid. Her voice is cool, crisp, unhurried—someone who has never had to raise her volume to get her way.

*"You're smearing the—"* / *"Nobody's checking the oarlocks at five in the morning, Lydie."*

A man's voice answers her, slow and unbothered, with the exact coastal drawl I heard leaning on my Civic door two days ago. Cole Halstead.

Then Lydia laughs, short and bright. Underneath it, a wet rag squeaks back and forth along varnished wood.

*"Leave the Montblanc beside the note on the blotter,"* Lydia says as her steps fade toward the stairs. *"If Graham wants to play tidy widower, let him explain a rushed departure list in the studio."*

At 02:14:44, a heavy door thuds shut. A second later, the waveform collapses into six minutes of dead dropout.

Graham doesn't move, staring at the flat green line, both hands still on the armrests. "She's laughing," he whispers. "God. She's standing in my hallway at two in the morning wiping down a staged oar, and she's *laughing*."

"That was Lydia," I say. "And Cole Halstead was right there with her. Every minute we sit here gives them another step on us."

I plug my phone into the USB cord beside his display. "Export that WAV. Copy it to my phone and push a backup to your off-site cloud before Cole cuts the fiber line."

***

Graham's hand shakes as he drags the raw WAV file into my phone's transfer window. The bar ticks upward: `14%... 38%...`

Before it reaches fifty, the server rack in the corner chimes twice, a clean two-note tone.

"What's that?" I grab the wrench off the desk.

Graham twists around toward the smoked glass, eyes wide. "A registered house tablet coming back in mesh range. I wiped every remote device after the Coast Guard called off the search. There shouldn't be anything paired to this—"

A new folder pops up inside the `CLOUD_SYNC` queue on the left display.

`DEVICE_ID: L_KIEFER_IPAD_MINI_02`

`STATUS: SYNC_COMPLETE`

`FILE: CALDER_CONFESSION_FINAL.wav`

"Don't play it," I snap. "Check the header."

Graham clicks the file to open its properties, and the waveform viewer auto-plays through the studio speakers.

It's my voice.

*"...didn't mean for the skiff to drift that far out,"* I'm sobbing through the speakers, breathless and panicked over the exact rain-on-glass hiss of this house. *"Graham said if I helped him move her out of the sunroom before high tide, he'd double the wire to twenty grand. I just—I needed the judgment cleared. I didn't know she wasn't breathing when we wrapped the rug..."*

I stare at the pulsing waveform. It's uncanny, hearing myself beg like that: clean phrases from my conversations in this house, stitched over a room-tone bed without a single audible seam. My stomach drops. With whatever they planted in my Civic's trunk, a county prosecutor could hand down an indictment before dinner, and nobody on a jury would ever believe that voice wasn't mine. Not for a second.

"They didn't just plant the boots," I say, my mouth dry. "Whoever locked me in the cellar at two-thirty bought themselves three hours to render that file."

On the left screen, the transmission log flashes green: `EXTERNAL UPLOAD COMPLETE — KNOX_COUNTY_EVIDENCE_DROP`. Half a second later, a red banner drops across the network window: `OUTBOUND WAN GATEWAY LOCKED — ADMIN OVERRIDE`. The fake confession is already on a county server. Whoever holds admin access on Lydia's iPad just killed the router's upload path before our raw WAV could leave the study.

Out on the ridge road, muffled through the rain-lashed glass, a siren wails in the dark, two miles out and closing fast.
