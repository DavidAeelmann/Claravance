# Chapter 13

The pinpoint red LED above the fire door glares steady against the dark concrete.

I drop the padded manila envelope onto the steel worktable and bolt straight for the mortise handle, pressing my ear against cold painted steel. On the other side of the door, footsteps cross the foyer slate—unhurried, soft-treaded, fading toward the kitchen archway before a chair leg scrapes softly across tile.

I don't bother testing the rusted **B-4** key in the interior keyhole. From this side, there's no thumb-turn—only a smooth brass escutcheon plate and a heavy commercial deadbolt thrown strictly from the foyer hall. Whoever locked me in didn't just pull the door shut. They threw an exterior bolt that won't budge without a key from the other side.

Which leaves the dumbwaiter shaft.

I snatch my phone off the desk, keep the screen dimmed low, and cross in my damp socks to the iron ladder bolted into the masonry. My calves burn as I climb the freezing rungs two at a time, dust flaking onto my eyelids until I reach the top cedar sill behind the pantry spice rack.

I press one palm gently against the hinged cedar panel.

It gives a fraction—then slams hard against something wedged across the outside shelves. Through the thin crack between the cleats, I hear the unmistakable scrape of a heavy oak kitchen chair braced tight under the louvered pantry doorknob, followed by the metallic *snick* of a deadbolt turning on the mudroom side. A moment later, muffled, stockinged footsteps pad lightly across the dark kitchen slate, deliberate and unhurried, before fading toward the front hall.

Whoever locked the basement door knows about the dumbwaiter shaft. And they just pinned me deep below the slab until morning, cutting off both exits without saying a word. If I try forcing that cedar panel from a vertical iron ladder, I'll either break my wrists or fall backward down the masonry chute.

I climb back down into the deadened chill of the darkroom, my breath pluming faintly under the blue glow of the two studio monitors. My fingers ache from the cold iron rungs, and a fresh smear of masonry dust streaks the front of my flannel shirt. Every muscle in my shoulders feels knotted tight from twenty minutes of adrenaline and damp cellar air.

Back at the worktable, the clock in the corner of the right-hand screen reads 2:34 AM. Cell signal bars on my phone show a flat, mocking **NO SERVICE**. Thick poured concrete and acoustic foam turn Julian's cellar into a Faraday cage—no calls, no texts, no emergency upload of the envelope sitting under my palm.

My teeth chatter hard enough to click. I pull my flannel cuffs down over my wrists, fold my arms tight around my ribs, and stare at the thick manila envelope stamped `OCT 14 — MASTER BACKUP / DO NOT OPEN BEFORE PROBATE`.

If whoever locked me down here wanted me dead in the dark, they wouldn't have left the workstation powered on or spared the red tamper tape inside that cabinet. They sealed me in to wait for sunrise—or to buy three hours upstairs while they clean up whatever they don't want me seeing before Thursday's courier arrives. Worse, if it wasn't Julian who threw that deadbolt, then someone else is walking around his foyer at two-thirty in the morning.

I unfold the narrow utility blade on my multi-tool and slice cleanly beneath the red tamper-evident tape.

***

Inside the padded kraft sleeve sit three items: a bound sheaf of notarized financial schedules, a heavy spiral-bound printout of raw acoustic transcripts, and a matte-black encrypted thumb drive taped to a yellow index card.

I spread the financial sheaf across the steel worktable under the blue monitor wash first.

Every page bears the raised notary seal of a Boston firm dated October tenth—four days *before* the storm. Not Julian's frantic post-disappearance scrambling. These are original wire receipts, bridge-loan ledger sheets, and signed trust-collateral transfers showing Julian Mercer systematically draining $2,400,000 out of The Evelyn Mercer Irrevocable Trust over fourteen months to keep Mercer Soundworks afloat while his acoustic patent stalled in federal review. Three separate quarterly draws carry Evelyn's secondary signatory initials in cramped blue ink—each stroke slightly stiff, traced over pencil guidelines still faintly visible under the glare of the monitor.

My stomach knots tight. Julian didn't just miss a payment. He forged Evelyn's signature on three consecutive transfers, and the October tenth audit packet proves he knew the Boston trustees were days away from uncovering the deficit before Evelyn ever stepped onto a dock. Anyone at Suffolk County DA's office holding these notarized sheets wouldn't need a murder body to put Julian Mercer in state prison for wire fraud—the paper trail alone is a ten-year sentence.

I set the notarized sheets aside and flip open the spiral-bound printout.

The cover sheet reads `RAW TIMELINE TRANSCRIPT — 2026-10-14 // UNFILTERED MULTI-ARRAY`.

I turn to the 02:14 AM tab, squinting at the dense columns of decibel spikes and timecoded dialogue flagged across `SUNROOM_EAST` and `BOATHOUSE_LINT`. Unlike the wind-scrubbed files on the server monitor, these pages were printed straight from the raw intake buffer before anyone ran spectral filters.

Half the lines are buried under `[GALE GUST — 42 KT]` and `[RAIN IMPACT — GLASS]`, yet three fragments jump off the page in bold type:

> `02:14:09 [SUNROOM_EAST]` — *Speaker A (Muffled):* "...told you not to leave the key on the hook—"
> `02:14:17 [SUNROOM_EAST]` — *Speaker B (Low):* "Drop the bag. Tide's already turning at the point."
> `02:14:31 [BOATHOUSE_LINT]` — *Speaker A:* "—can't take the Whaler if the—"
> `02:14:44 [BOATHOUSE_LINT]` — `[METALLIC LATCH IMPACT — 68 dB // SIGNAL CLIPPED]`

After 02:14:44, the transcript drops into six minutes of dead static—every microphone channel cut simultaneously at the main breaker before resuming at 02:21:02 with nothing but empty rain beating against the cove pilings.

I sit back slowly on the edge of the rolling studio stool, staring at the paper until the lines blur. Even the acoustic hydrophone anchored out in the cove channel logged a brief rhythmic oar-splash at 02:18:11—four minutes *after* the sunroom latch slammed—before the gale surge drowned the sensor out. Whoever rowed out into that cove didn't start the Boston Whaler's twin outboards at the dock; they pulled away silently on oars under cover of the blackout, waiting until they cleared the point before firing the engine or cutting the transponder.

Two people were standing inside the sunroom and boathouse corridor at 2:14 AM during the storm—arguing over a boat key and a bag—seconds before someone killed the array at the breaker box. Yet on the drafting table across the room, Julian spent three weeks frantically calculating *how* a Boston Whaler could clear the east shoal without running aground.

If Julian was one of those voices—if he killed his wife at 2:14 AM—why would he keep notarized proof of his own $2.4M felony locked in the same cabinet as an unedited transcript of the crime? Unless someone else is building a frame—or unless this cellar is both a vault and a deliberate puzzle box left for whoever finally pried that locker open.

***

By 5:15 AM, the chill in the concrete floor has climbed straight up my shins into my knees.

I pace the length of the rubberized matting to keep blood moving in my toes, staying clear of the eerie northeast dead-zone where even my own breathing vanishes. Every half hour, I check my phone—still zero bars—and double-check the backup copies I already pushed to my encrypted Portland vault before coming downstairs. The *Bramwell & Shaw* demand letter is safely in the cloud. Even if Julian—or whoever threw that deadbolt—takes my phone and smashes it against the concrete, they can't erase what's already sitting off-site.

I slip the encrypted thumb drive off the yellow index card and tuck it deep inside the small watch pocket of my jeans, beside Maren's torn tide chart. Then I slide the notarized $2.4M ledger and the 2:14 AM transcript printout back inside the padded manila envelope, tuck it under my arm, and lean against the edge of the steel worktable to wait out the dark. My eyelids feel gritty from lack of sleep, and my ribs ache from the cold damp flannel clinging to my back. By five-thirty, my toes are numb inside the rubber mat's chill, and I have to flex my fingers every few minutes just to keep my grip loose enough to use the multi-tool if the door opens fast.

At 5:58 AM, faint water pipes shudder inside the ceiling joists overhead. Someone upstairs just turned on the kitchen tap, followed by the heavy scrape of a chair sliding back across slate—the chair wedged against the pantry door.

I move two steps sideways behind the worktable, keeping the heavy steel edge between myself and the fire door, and grip the folded multi-tool inside my palm. My mouth tastes of copper and stale cellar dust. I shift my weight onto the balls of my numb feet, ready to bolt back into the northeast dead-zone if whoever comes down those steps carries a gun instead of a wrench.

At 6:05 AM, heavy footsteps descend the main foyer stairs outside.

They pause on the slate landing. A brass key slides into the exterior mortise cylinder with a sharp, gritty scrape, and the deadbolt throws back with a loud metallic *clack* that echoes off the acoustic foam.

The pinpoint red LED above the interior frame winks out.

The heavy steel fire door swings inward on its pneumatic closer, letting a cold wash of gray dawn light—and the smell of wet pine needles from the open front porch—spill down the short concrete steps from the foyer hall.

Julian Mercer stands framed in the upper doorway.

He's still wearing yesterday's dark merino crewneck, wrinkled now and dusted across one shoulder with plaster grit, his eyes bloodshot and hollowed out beneath gray stubble. In his right hand, he grips a heavy brass pipe wrench low against his thigh, fingers locked tight around the knurled shaft.

Behind his heels on the foyer slate, half-lit by the gray morning window, sits a pair of muddy women's winter duck boots—fresh wet kelp clinging to the rubber soles, dripping dark rainwater onto the stone tile beside an open, mud-streaked navy nylon tote.
