# Chapter 11

At 1:52 AM, the cast-iron radiator under my window gives a single sharp tick and goes quiet.

I lie on top of the stripped quilt in my jeans and flannel and stare at the ceiling while the pipes cool. For a second I'm back in our old apartment in Burlington, awake on my side of the bedroom while Nina's bed stayed made. It's that same hollow quiet right before a bad phone call, the kind where you already know the morning is ruined before anyone speaks. Then the pale blue glow of my laptop on the nightstand drags me back to Salt Point. Lydia Kiefer's St. Andrews timestamp is burned behind my eyelids: *November 3, 09:42 AST.* Forty hours ago, her passport was scanned at a private dock in New Brunswick. A woman supposedly drowned in Penobscot Bay may still be alive. Meanwhile her husband is somewhere in this house after drinking bourbon swirled with pale sediment, and at four this afternoon I'm supposed to hand him a clean probate ledger.

Walking out at dawn isn't an option. Not with a county deputy holding co-owner installer PINs, and not with Graham's fifty-thousand-dollar breach clause waiting if I bolt without hard proof of what happened on October fourteenth.

Thirty minutes crawl past. I'm reaching for my wool socks at the foot of the bed when a sharp metallic *clack* carries through the framing from the kitchen wing. I glance at the laptop clock: 2:27 AM. The sound is too hollow for the front deadbolt and too clean for the wind. It rings like a heavy latch dropping home inside a wall near the pantry, followed by a low hum vibrating through the baseboard pipes, as if something weighted just settled on a line.

I pull the wool socks on fast, pat my pockets for my phone, multi-tool, and front-door key, and ease the wooden desk chair out from under the doorknob.

The north hall is dead quiet. I ease the cheap privacy bolt back so it won't snap, step onto the gray runner, and pause at the foot of the main stairs. Pitch dark up top. I stand still and listen for floorboards shifting in the master suite, or a bedroom door opening. Nothing. If the swapped bourbon put Graham under, he's out until morning—if he's even up there.

I hug the drywall down the hall toward the kitchen wing. Halfway there, a gust slams the sunroom glass and rattles the frames. I freeze mid-stride until the wind drops. Ahead, the house holds its night quiet: the crawlspace dehumidifier thumps softly underfoot, and gusts press the glass deck door against its rubber seals. As I pass the living room arch, I make out cold gray ash in the hearth and a dull glint off the two crystal tumblers still left on the low table, while the long leather sofa disappears into shadow past the stonework.

I duck into the mudroom first, keep low under the dim blue standby glow of the wall tablet, and slide my hand into the breast pocket of my waxed jacket. The tissue-wrapped **B-4** key is still there. I pull it out, peel back a corner of tissue to feel the warded brass teeth, and drop it into my front jeans pocket. If the pantry route closes behind me downstairs, old brass might be the only thing between me and the foyer stairs.

Back in the pantry corridor, the steel breaker box is shut just the way Halstead left it. Across from it, the louvered cedar door to the walk-in pantry is closed. I turn the knob with my thumb holding the ball-catch back, slip inside, and ease the louvered door shut until the hall disappears. The cedar shelves smell dry and sharp in the dark, peppery from the open tins. I pull my phone out, drop the screen brightness to the bottom, and prop it upright against a glass flour canister so the dim light reaches the back wall.

Yesterday at midday, pressed flat against these shelves while Halstead took his burner call, the wainscoting behind the bottom spice rack gave slightly against my spine.

One by one, I lift the heavy stoneware crocks, paprika tins, and glass spice jars down onto a folded dish towel by my heels so nothing clinks. My palms leave damp prints on the cool glass. With the middle shelf cleared, I run my fingers along the seam where the vertical boards meet the cleat. Two recessed brass flush-latches are countersunk into the dark grain behind the jars, and a fresh thumbprint smudges the dust right across the right-hand slot.

***

I flip out the flathead screwdriver on my multi-tool and slip the tip into the right-hand slot. The brass turns a quarter-inch with a dry *snick* of old grease. I brace my shoulder against the shelf frame so the spring doesn't snap, turn the left latch the same way, then hook my fingers under the cedar cleat and pull. Swollen from coastal damp, the panel sticks at first. I work the flathead blade into the vertical seam and pry gently until the wood gives with a dry scrape.

The whole back wall, shelf cleats and all, swings out on hidden piano hinges. Stale, freezing air spills out of the dark opening, cold as a root cellar and carrying the metallic bite of rust. Unlike the rest of the pantry, the inner cedar lip has been brushed clean of dust where a sleeve or boot heel dragged past recently.

I lift my phone and angle the dim screen inside. A narrow utility chase framed in rough hemlock studs drops away behind the spice rack. It's an original 1970s manual dumbwaiter shaft stripped of its wooden cab decades ago, and a vertical iron service ladder is bolted into the cinderblock wall on the right.

The old cast-iron sheave pulley is mounted just under the cedar header on the left, still strung with a thick braided hemp rope hanging taut down the chase. Nobody had bothered cutting the rigging out when they stripped the cab; instead, a removable steel locking pin with a heavy ring loop is jammed through a stop bracket on the pulley wheel to hold the brake arm rigid. The salt rust around the ring has been rubbed bright where fingers checked it recently. Beside the ladder, the dumbwaiter's cast-iron counterweight hangs inside a wooden guide channel, a dull rectangular block of pig iron suspended mid-shaft above the cellar below. The taut rope still trembles slightly against the header. That's the exact weighted hum I heard through the baseboard pipes right after the flush-latches clacked shut at 2:27.

Tuesday night's alert flashes back: **MOTION DETECTED — BASEMENT ZONE 4 (PERIMETER BYPASS ACTIVE)**. Someone had triggered a basement sensor while the perimeter bypass was active, and this shaft offers a straight drop past the steel fire door and its contact alarm. Between the fresh thumbprint in the latch dust and the 2:27 clack, it looks like somebody just used it again.

I glance back through the louvered slats. The corridor is empty, and the amber keypad LED glows steadily outside the glass deck door. Climb down there, and I'm below poured concrete with zero cell service and one narrow ladder back up.

I can't just stay upstairs and wait for tomorrow's ambush.

I shove my phone into my back pocket, grip the iron side rails, and swing a leg over the cedar sill. The shaft is barely wide enough for my shoulders. I pull the hinged spice-rack panel nearly shut behind me and leave a hair of clearance on the inside cleat so I can get my fingers back under it. Total dark drops over me. The air in the shaft is cold and stale against my face, chalky with damp plaster.

Cold iron bites straight through my wool socks. I climb down slowly and test every rung before putting my weight on it while masonry grit flakes onto my knuckles. Halfway down, the taut hemp line brushes my sleeve. It's still swaying against the studs on its own. My elbow bumps it, and the cast-iron counterweight scrapes against its wooden channel. Pull that locking pin out of the bracket overhead, and dead iron drops onto the cellar slab.

Below the foundation line, the cedar smell dies and the air goes oddly dead and muffled; it swallows even the scrape of my socks on iron. Three rungs from the bottom, the rail ends at a smooth concrete ledge. I step down into the dark.

***

Absolute darkness closes in around me, dead and heavy. I reach out with one hand and brush thick charcoal foam lining the wall, where the damp chill of concrete gives way to the warm smell of running electronics. Across the room, a single green LED blinks slowly in the dark. I pull my phone from my back pocket for light and step off the ledge onto rubberized matting.

My left heel catches a loose microphone cable snaked across the mat. The cord snaps taut and yanks a low aluminum tripod hard against the leg of a steel worktable beside the shaft. The table jolts and nudges the mouse on top.

A heavy relay clicks inside a tower against the far wall. Two wide computer displays flare awake in blinding blue-white light, and cooling fans spin up to fill the deadened cellar with a steady hum.

I drop into a crouch with an arm over my eyes and wait for a siren upstairs or boots hitting the foyer slate. Nothing comes. The foam-lined concrete eats the fan noise before it even reaches the ceiling joists. Whoever climbed out of the dumbwaiter didn't shut the machine down—they just let the screens sleep.

I stand slowly and cross the rubber mat to the worktable, my eyes still watering from the sudden glare as I look at the left-hand display.

A bare-bones media player is paused on a grainy infrared exterior camera frame from the night of the storm. White digital numbers burned into the upper corner read **02:14 AM**.

Graham Kiefer stands frozen mid-stride in front of the boathouse-apron lens. He wears a dark rain slicker with the hood pulled low over his eyes, his face turned half-away as he drags a heavy rolled canvas rug out of the sunroom patio doors toward the open double doors of the boathouse. The rug sags hard in the middle under dead weight, and its wet hem leaves a dark smear across the granite flagstones behind his heels.
